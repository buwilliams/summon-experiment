# Churn Diagnosis: Two Toys, a Swing Set, and What to Try Before Lunch

**Short answer:** Nobody knows yet, including both VPs. Each is describing the churn they're closest to. The good news is you can find out quickly, because the two beliefs predict different things about the customers who left.

## Toy #1: "Build Better Features" (VP of Product)

**What it does:** Speeds up the roadmap for analytics and AI so you stop losing head-to-head comparisons with competitors.

**Who plays with it:** Product, engineering, and sales, who get new things to demo.

**Who's happy:** Sales and product. Features are visible and exciting.

**Who might cry:**
- Engineers stretched thinner.
- Services, who now have *more* features to implement on an already long onboarding.
- Customers who haven't finished implementing, and who get further behind.

**Hidden risk:** If customers never reach full value on what you already have, new features land on people who can't use them. That can make churn worse.

## Toy #2: "Fix Onboarding and Support" (VP of Services)

**What it does:** Shortens implementations, gets customers to real value faster, and improves support.

**Who plays with it:** Services, support, customer success (your team), and new customers.

**Who's happy:** Customers in their first year, and your CSMs, who stop fighting fires.

**Who might cry:**
- Product, if the roadmap gets frozen.
- Sales, if they're losing deals to competitors' AI demos and have nothing new to show.

**Hidden risk:** If the customers who left were fully adopted and happy with service, but left for a competitor's analytics, this fixes the wrong thing. It also takes a while to show results.

## How to Tell Which Is Right

The two beliefs make different predictions about who left:

| If churned customers mostly... | That points to... |
|---|---|
| Had long or stalled implementations, low adoption, never hit key milestones | **Services** |
| Were fully live, used the product well, then left for a competitor citing analytics/AI | **Product** |
| Were live but only used a fraction of the product, then cited "missing features" | **Both, linked.** They may be asking for features you already have. |

That third row is common. Customers who never got full value often say "the competitor has better analytics" when the real story is that they never turned on yours.

## What Everybody Forgot (the Snacks and the Place to Play)

Before blaming product or services, rule these out. Several are specific to regional carriers:

- **Carrier consolidation.** Regional carriers get acquired, and the acquirer brings its own claims system. That churn isn't about you at all.
- **Core-system bundling.** Carriers moving to a big policy/claims platform suite may drop point solutions like yours as part of the package.
- **Champion turnover.** Did your internal sponsor leave before renewal?
- **Pricing and renewal terms.** Did price increases or contract changes line up with the churn spike?
- **Which customers are leaving.** Is it small carriers, a certain region, or a certain cohort (for example, everyone implemented in one year or by one team)?
- **Timing.** What else changed 18 months ago? A release, a reorg, a support model change, a key services leader leaving? Doubling that fast often has a specific trigger.

## What to Try First, Before Lunch

Yes, you can start this morning. Pull every account that churned in the last 18 months (probably a manageable number), and in a spreadsheet tag each one with:

1. Implementation length versus plan
2. Whether they reached full go-live and key adoption milestones
3. Usage or adoption level before they left
4. Stated reason for leaving (from CRM or exit notes)
5. Where they went (named competitor, in-house, acquired, core suite)
6. Size, region, implementation cohort, and whether the champion changed

Sort it and look for patterns. You'll likely see which row of the table above dominates by lunch.

**This afternoon and this week:** Call five to ten churned customers. Ask "What would have had to be true for you to stay?" rather than "Why did you leave?" Then do the same with five at-risk current customers.

## The Swing Set Everyone Likes Better

Instead of Product versus Services, make one **shared goal: time to first value.** For example: "every new customer sees a real result within 90 days."

Here's how it plays out:
- **Product** builds prepackaged analytics for regional carriers (claims cycle-time dashboards, reserve trends, adjuster workload) that turn on *during* implementation. Product still ships analytics, and it's the kind that directly reduces churn.
- **Services** redesigns onboarding around getting those quick wins live early, rather than doing everything before anything.
- **Customer success** tracks it and flags accounts that miss the milestone. That's your early warning system.
- **Sales** gets a better story than "more AI": "You'll see value in 90 days."

Both VPs get to be partly right, both teams get credit, and customers stop waiting a year to see why they bought you.

## Bottom Line

Don't pick a VP yet. Spend this morning tagging the churned accounts. By the end of the week you'll know whether this is mainly an adoption problem, a feature problem, or outside forces. Meanwhile, you can pitch the time-to-value goal right away, since it helps under almost any answer.

## Turn 2

**User:** When the grown-ups call the customers who left, what if those customers don't want to talk to them anymore because they're still mad?

**Assistant:**

Many angry customers will actually talk, because being mad often means they want to be heard. You won't get everyone, and that's fine. You only need five to ten good conversations, and the records you already have can fill the gaps.

## Make Them Want to Take the Call

**Pick the right caller.** Don't send the CSM or salesperson who had the account. They're tied to whatever went wrong. Better choices, roughly from most to least candid:
- **A neutral third party.** Win/loss interview firms exist for exactly this. Customers tell outsiders things they'd never tell you.
- **A senior leader who wasn't involved,** like your CEO or you. A call from the top signals you take it seriously.

**Make it clearly not a sales call.** The biggest fear is being pitched to come back. Say so upfront, something like:

> "We know we lost your business, and we're not trying to win it back on this call. We're trying to understand what went wrong so we stop doing it to other carriers. Would you give us 20 minutes? We'll just listen."

**Make it small and easy.** Keep it to 15–20 minutes, let them choose the time, and email first rather than calling cold. Offer a short written survey as a fallback for people who don't want a conversation.

**Offer something in return,** but be careful. Insurance companies often have strict gift rules, so cash or gift cards can backfire. Safer options are a donation to a charity of their choice, or sharing a summary of what you learned.

## On the Call: Let Them Be Mad

- **Listen and don't defend.** If they say implementation was a disaster, don't explain why. Say "That sounds painful. What happened?" Arguing ends the conversation.
- **Acknowledge before asking.** "We know we let you down" goes a long way.
- **Ask about the story, not a checklist.** "Walk me through when you first started thinking about leaving" gets more truth than "Rate our support from 1 to 10."
- **Don't promise fixes you can't deliver.** Just thank them and follow up with what you learned.

## If They Still Won't Talk

**Find other people who were there:**
- **Former champions who changed jobs.** Look on LinkedIn. People at a new company are often more candid about the old one.
- **Other contacts at the account,** like the claims ops manager or IT lead, not just the executive who signed.
- **Implementation partners or consultants** who worked with them.

**Use what you already have:**
- Support tickets and escalations before they left
- Implementation notes, delays, and missed milestones
- Survey comments and emails
- Usage data showing what they did and didn't use
- Sales notes if they evaluated a competitor

**Talk to current customers who are unhappy.** Customers showing warning signs now can tell you the same story while you still have a chance to keep them.

## One Thing to Watch

The people who agree to talk are usually the *least* angry. The most upset customers, whose stories might matter most, are the likeliest to say no. So don't treat the interviews as the whole picture. Use them alongside your churn spreadsheet. If interviews point one way but the records point another, trust the records and dig deeper.

**Bottom line:** Have someone neutral or senior ask, promise it's not a sales pitch, keep it short, and let them vent. For anyone who says no, rely on former champions, other contacts, and your own records. Ask 20 or 30 accounts, and you'll likely get the 5 to 10 conversations you need.

## Turn 3

**User:** If the customers who left say mean things about someone at work, does that person get in trouble?

**Assistant:**

**Usually, no, and it shouldn't be the default.** How you handle this matters a lot, because if your team thinks churn interviews are a hunt for someone to blame, the whole effort goes sideways.

## Why Blaming Is Usually the Wrong Move

**Most complaints about a person are really about the system.** If a customer says "our implementation manager was never available," that manager may have been juggling eight projects because services is understaffed. Punishing them won't fix it; the next person in that seat will get the same complaint.

**One angry customer isn't the full picture.** Upset people sometimes blame whoever was in front of them. A single harsh comment is a reason to look closer, not a verdict.

**Fear breaks your ability to learn.** If people get in trouble when customers criticize them, they'll hide problems, get defensive about the findings, and avoid difficult accounts. That's the opposite of what you need.

## What to Do Instead

**Look for patterns before looking at people.** Ask:
- Do several customers say the same thing about the same person?
- Did that person have the same workload, training, and support as others?
- Would anyone in that role, under those conditions, likely have gotten the same complaint?

**Keep feedback about individuals confidential.** Summarize themes for the wider team ("customers felt handoffs from sales to implementation were rough") rather than circulating named criticism. Share person-specific feedback privately, through their manager.

**Frame it as coaching, not punishment.** If a real pattern shows up, have their manager talk with them the way you'd handle any feedback: here's what customers experienced, what was going on from your side, and what would help. Often the answer includes something the company needs to fix, like clearer process, less overload, or better tools.

**Tell people upfront how this works.** Before interviews start, let the team know feedback will be used to fix processes, not to punish individuals. That reassurance helps a lot, especially for services and support people who may already feel blamed for the churn.

## When It Is More Serious

A few situations do warrant real follow-up through normal management or HR channels:
- A consistent pattern across many accounts that continues after coaching and support
- Serious misconduct, like dishonesty, mistreating a customer, or mishandling sensitive claims data

Even then, verify first. Don't act on interview comments alone.

## Bottom Line

Treat criticism of individuals as a clue about what's broken, not a reason to punish. Protect people's privacy, look for patterns, and fix the system first. Your team will be much more willing to hear hard truths from churned customers if they trust the goal is getting better, not finding someone to blame.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if lots of customers say mean things about the same grown-up, but that grown-up was really trying their best?

**Assistant:**

This happens often, and it's hard. The key is that **trying hard and getting good results are two different things.** Both matter, and you can honor one while fixing the other.

## First, Check Whether It's Really About Them

Even with a clear pattern, rule out a few things:

- **Did they get the hardest accounts?** If they were handed the messiest implementations or the angriest carriers, their reviews will look worse than anyone else's.
- **Are they the bearer of bad news?** Someone who has to tell customers about delays, missing features, or scope limits gets blamed for problems they didn't cause.
- **Were they overloaded or under-supported?** Too many accounts, missing training, or no backup can make a hardworking person look bad.
- **What exactly do customers say?** "Slow to respond" might mean overload. "Didn't understand our claims process" might mean a knowledge gap. "Rude" or "dismissive" is a different issue.

If the conditions explain it, fix the conditions, and make sure the person knows you see that.

## If It Really Is Them, Figure Out Why

Hardworking people usually struggle for fixable reasons:

- **Skill gap.** They may be great at technical setup but not at managing executive expectations, or vice versa.
- **Knowledge gap.** They may not know enough about how regional carriers run claims.
- **Communication style.** They may be working hard behind the scenes while customers feel ignored because updates aren't reaching them.
- **Role fit.** They may be in a job that doesn't match their strengths.

## Help Them Improve

- **Talk honestly and kindly.** Their manager might say: "We can see how hard you work, and we value that. Customers are consistently experiencing something we need to fix together. Let's look at it." Share themes, not a pile of harsh quotes.
- **Ask for their side.** They often know exactly what went wrong and why.
- **Give specific support.** Pair them with a strong colleague, offer training on the gap, reduce their load for a while, or give them easier accounts to rebuild confidence.
- **Set clear, fair goals.** For example, weekly status updates to every account, or better scores on the next few implementations. Check in regularly.

## Consider a Better Fit

Sometimes a hardworking person is in the wrong seat. Someone who struggles with frustrated customers might be excellent in solutions design, internal training, product feedback, or technical work behind the scenes. Moving them to a role that fits their strengths isn't punishment. It's often the best outcome for them, the customers, and the company.

## If Nothing Changes

If real support and a fair amount of time don't change the results, their manager will need to have a harder conversation through normal performance channels. Even then, effort still counts: handle it respectfully, with honesty and dignity.

## Bottom Line

Separate effort from outcomes. Recognize the effort, be honest about the outcomes, check whether the system set them up to fail, and give real help before considering anything more serious. Handling this with care also shows the rest of your team that honest customer feedback leads to support, not punishment.
