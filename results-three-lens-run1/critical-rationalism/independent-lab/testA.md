## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Neither, in its pure form. And the framing itself may be the bigger risk to your success. Here's why, and what I'd do instead.

## Why each pure strategy tends to fail

**Working entirely within existing systems (the CEO's view):**
- Established processes are optimized for the current business. They're good at incremental improvement and bad at anything that looks different.
- New ideas get "antibodied": starved of resources, slowed by approval layers, or reshaped until they resemble what already exists.
- If the current systems and talent could turn things around on their own, they probably would have by now.

**A fully independent innovation lab (your view):**
- This is one of the most common failure patterns in corporate innovation. Labs often become "innovation theater": interesting prototypes, demo days, and nothing that reaches customers.
- The hardest part isn't generating ideas. It's getting them adopted by the core business (sales, implementation, support, the platform). Independence makes that handoff harder, not easier.
- The core organization often resents the lab ("they get the fun work, we keep the lights on"), which kills adoption.
- In insurance software specifically, value is tied to integration, compliance, data, and trust. A lab building apart from the core platform risks building things customers can't actually deploy.

The research on this (O'Reilly & Tushman on "ambidextrous organizations," Govindarajan & Trimble's *The Other Side of Innovation*) broadly supports a middle path: **structural protection for new work, combined with tight linkage to the core at the leadership level.**

## Diagnose the problem before picking the structure

You were brought in because of churn and lost deals. Ask what's actually causing them:

- **If customers are leaving over reliability, implementation pain, slow feature delivery, poor support, or outdated UX,** that's a core execution problem. An innovation lab won't fix it and could distract from it. The CEO may be more right than you think.
- **If customers are leaving for fundamentally different offerings** (cloud-native competitors, AI-driven underwriting or claims, embedded insurance, new pricing models), that's a disruption problem. The existing org likely *can't* respond fast enough alone, and your instinct for separation has merit.
- **Most likely it's both,** in which case you need a structure that handles both.

Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, the sales team, and customer success before you commit. That evidence will also be your best tool for persuading the CEO.

## What I'd recommend: a protected but connected model

1. **A small, dedicated team with its own operating rules**: faster decision rights, lighter process, different metrics (learning velocity and customer validation, not quarterly revenue at first).
2. **Firmly tethered to the core**: shared access to the platform and data, rotating engineers or product people from the core business, and a clear path for handing off validated ideas.
3. **Focused on real customer problems tied to churn and win rates**, not blue-sky exploration. Early on, credibility matters more than ambition.
4. **Direct CEO sponsorship**, with you reporting to the top so the lab isn't smothered by middle-management priorities.
5. **Explicit graduation criteria** for when an initiative moves into the core, spins out, or gets killed.

This is a way to get the benefits of both positions while avoiding the failure modes of each.

## Handling the CEO

You're new, and your boss has stated a belief that runs counter to yours. Leading with "we need to be independent of the organization" can read as "I think your company is the problem," which is a tough position in your first months.

Instead:
- **Frame it as his strategy, executed well.** "Using our existing talent and systems" is compatible with a protected team staffed partly by internal people.
- **Start with a pilot, not a reorganization.** One or two high-visibility projects tied directly to churn or a winnable deal. Results will earn you the autonomy to expand.
- **Let data make the argument.** If customer research shows the problem requires a different approach, that's far more persuasive than organizational theory.
- **Name the tradeoffs openly.** Ask him: "What would stop a good idea from shipping here today?" His answer will tell you how much protection the work actually needs.

## First 90 days, roughly

- **Weeks 1–4:** Customer and internal discovery. Map why customers leave and deals are lost. Identify internal allies and blockers.
- **Weeks 5–8:** Pick one or two initiatives with clear business impact. Assemble a small team, including respected insiders.
- **Weeks 9–12:** Ship something real to customers, even if small. Report results to the CEO and propose the operating model based on what you learned.

The question that decides your success isn't "inside or outside?" It's "how do new ideas reach customers faster than they do today?" Design around that, and the structure question mostly answers itself.

## Turn 2

**User:** What if the CEO says no to even a small protected team? How do I push back without damaging the relationship this early?

**Assistant:**

A "no" this early usually isn't the end of the conversation. It's information. How you respond to it will shape the relationship more than the decision itself.

## First, understand the "no" before pushing back

CEOs rarely reject an idea for the reason you'd guess. Before arguing, find out what he's actually objecting to. Common underlying concerns:

- **Past failures:** "We tried an innovation group before and it went nowhere."
- **Culture:** "Creating an elite team will demoralize everyone else."
- **Money and optics:** "We're losing customers. I can't justify new headcount right now."
- **Focus:** "We need everyone fixing the core, not exploring."
- **Trust:** "I don't know you well enough yet to hand you autonomy."
- **His own conviction:** He may genuinely believe the org can do this, and a separate team feels like a vote of no confidence in his people.

Ask with curiosity, not as a setup for rebuttal:

> "That's helpful. Can you tell me more about what concerns you? I want to make sure I understand what's worked and not worked here before."

Often you'll discover his objection is to the *form* (a separate team, new headcount, a "lab" label), not the *function* (moving faster on specific problems). That gives you room.

## Don't push back in the same meeting

Take the no gracefully. Something like:

> "Understood. Let me think about how to get the speed we need within that. I'll come back with some options."

This signals you're a partner, not an advocate for your own pet structure. Pushing hard in the moment, especially as a new hire, reads as not listening.

## Then reframe around what he already wants

Come back with options that achieve the function without triggering his objection:

- **No new headcount:** "Could I borrow three or four people from existing teams for 90 days on one churn problem, then return them?" Temporary and reversible is much easier to approve.
- **No separate team:** "Instead of a new group, can I get decision rights on one project, so it doesn't need to go through the normal approval cycle?" Often the real bottleneck is process, not org structure.
- **No 'lab' label:** Call it a "tiger team," "customer retention sprint," or name it after the problem. Labels carry baggage.
- **Tie it to his priority:** "Our biggest at-risk account is X. I'd like to put a small group on keeping them for the next quarter." It's hard to say no to saving revenue.

The key move is making it a **small, time-boxed, reversible experiment** with a clear success measure. You're not asking him to change his philosophy, just to test something.

## If it's still no, work within it, visibly and well

Sometimes the answer stays no, at least for now. That's okay. You can still make progress:

- **Find an ally inside the core.** A product or engineering leader who's frustrated with the status quo can often give you informal capacity and cover.
- **Deliver a win through the existing system.** Pick something achievable, push it through, and document where the process slowed you down. Specific friction ("this took 11 weeks, 7 of which were approvals") is more persuasive than any argument about structure.
- **Bring him customer evidence.** Recordings or quotes from churned customers can shift a CEO's view in a way internal proposals can't.
- **Revisit in a quarter.** "Here's what we accomplished, here's what slowed us down, here's what I'd suggest next." Earned credibility changes the conversation.

## Keep an honest check on yourself

Two things worth holding in mind:

1. **He might be partly right.** If your discovery shows churn is driven by core execution problems, pushing for a separate team could genuinely be the wrong call. Being willing to update will earn you a lot of trust.
2. **Watch whether the role is viable.** One "no" early is normal. But if over six months you're repeatedly blocked from changing *anything* about how work gets done, it's worth a direct conversation about expectations: "You hired me to produce outsized results. Here's what I believe that requires. How do you see it?" That's not a threat. It's clarifying whether you both mean the same thing by "lead innovation."

## A simple way to think about it

Early on, you're not trying to win the argument. You're trying to earn the right to be trusted with a bigger bet. Accept the no gracefully, understand the real concern, propose something small and reversible, deliver, and let results make your case.

## Turn 3

**User:** How do I figure out who the potential allies and blockers are when I've only been here a few weeks?

**Assistant:**

In your first few weeks, you have an advantage you'll never have again: it's completely normal to ask everyone questions. Use it deliberately.

## Run a structured listening tour

Aim for 20–30 conversations over your first month or two, across levels and functions. Don't just meet executives. Include:

- **Direct reports of the CEO**, especially product, engineering, sales, customer success, and finance
- **Frontline people who touch customers**: account managers, implementation consultants, support leads. They know why customers leave.
- **Long-tenured employees**: they know the history, including past innovation efforts and why they died
- **Recent hires**: they still see the organization with fresh eyes, like you
- **The CEO's executive assistant or chief of staff**: they understand how decisions actually get made and who has the CEO's ear
- **Technical architects and platform owners**: in insurance software, these people can quietly make or break anything new
- **Compliance, legal, and security**: often seen as blockers, but powerful allies if brought in early

Ask each person: *"Who else should I talk to?"* When the same names come up repeatedly, you've found the informal influencers, who often matter more than the org chart suggests.

## Ask questions that reveal stance without putting people on the spot

Good questions:

- "What's the biggest thing holding us back with customers right now?"
- "If you could change one thing about how we build or ship, what would it be?"
- "What's been tried before to fix this? What happened?"
- "Who here gets things done when it really matters?"
- "What would make my role a success from your perspective? What would make it a problem for you?"

That last one is especially revealing. People will often tell you directly what they're protecting.

## Read the signals

**Likely allies:**
- They describe problems candidly and specifically, including their own area's
- They're frustrated with the status quo and have ideas
- They offer help, introductions, or data without being asked
- They follow up after your conversation
- They talk about customers more than internal politics

**Likely blockers (or at least cautious stakeholders):**
- They emphasize why things are the way they are, or how complex everything is
- They ask a lot about scope, reporting lines, and who "owns" what
- They're polite in meetings but slow to share information or resources
- Their team, budget, or status could be affected by change
- They reference past failed initiatives as proof that change won't work

**Watch behavior over words.** Almost everyone will say they support innovation. Notice who actually returns calls, shares data, lends people, and shows up.

## Map formal and informal power

A simple two-by-two helps: **influence** (low to high) against **stance** (resistant to supportive).

- **High influence, supportive:** your core coalition. Invest heavily here.
- **High influence, resistant:** your most important relationships to understand and win over, or at least neutralize. Don't avoid them.
- **Low influence, supportive:** great sources of insight and energy. They can also become future team members.
- **Low influence, resistant:** keep them informed, but don't spend much energy here.

Remember influence isn't just title. It includes who the CEO trusts, who controls critical systems or budgets, and who others follow.

## Be careful not to label people too early

A few cautions:

- **"Blockers" usually have legitimate reasons.** The platform architect who resists new tools may be protecting system stability. The sales leader skeptical of experiments may be burned by promises that didn't ship. Understanding their concerns often turns them into your most credible supporters.
- **Early enthusiasm isn't always real alliance.** Some people attach themselves to new leaders to advance their own agendas or settle old scores. Be friendly with everyone, but be slow to align with any faction.
- **Stances shift.** People move once they see results, or once you've addressed what they were worried about. Revisit your map every month or so.
- **Keep your map private.** A document labeling colleagues as "blockers" is a political disaster if it circulates. Keep notes discreet and factual.

## Turn early relationships into real alliances

Once you've identified potential allies:

- **Solve a problem for them.** The fastest way to build an alliance is to help someone with something they care about.
- **Give them credit.** Make your early wins partly theirs, especially in front of the CEO.
- **Involve skeptics early.** Asking a cautious senior leader for advice on your first project often converts resistance into ownership.

In a few weeks, you won't have a perfect picture, and you don't need one. You need a working hypothesis about who matters and where they stand, which you'll keep refining as you go.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if one of the high-influence people I'd consider a blocker is someone the CEO trusts a lot, like his longtime CTO?

**Assistant:**

This is probably the single most important relationship to get right in your first year. A longtime, trusted CTO can make your role work or make it quietly impossible, and the CEO will likely side with them in any open conflict, at least early on.

The good news: this is also one of the most winnable relationships, if you approach it right.

## Start by assuming the CTO is reacting reasonably

From their seat, your arrival may look like this:

- **A threat to their territory.** "Innovation" in a software company overlaps heavily with technology strategy. Your role may feel like an implicit criticism: the CEO brought someone in because the CTO wasn't delivering.
- **Risk to the platform.** They've likely spent years keeping a complex, regulated system stable. New initiatives that bypass architecture standards create security, compliance, and maintenance problems that *they* will own.
- **Déjà vu.** Long-tenured leaders have often seen innovation initiatives come and go, leaving technical debt behind.
- **Resource competition.** Their teams are probably stretched already. Anything you do may pull on their people.

None of this makes them wrong. And their trust with the CEO exists for a reason: they've likely delivered for years.

## The golden rule: never go around them

The fastest way to lose both the CTO *and* the CEO is to position yourself against the CTO. Avoid:

- Criticizing the CTO or their technology to the CEO, even subtly ("the platform is really holding us back")
- Launching technical work without their knowledge
- Recruiting their engineers informally without asking
- Letting others pull you into complaints about the CTO

Assume anything you say about the CTO will reach them, because with a longtime partner of the CEO, it often will.

## Make them a partner, not an obstacle

**1. Meet one-on-one early, and lead with curiosity.**

> "You know this platform and this business better than almost anyone. I'd really value your view on where we're losing customers and what's realistically possible. And honestly, what worries you about a role like mine?"

Asking what worries them signals respect and surfaces the real concern.

**2. Clarify boundaries explicitly.** Ambiguity breeds territorial conflict. Try to agree on something like: you focus on customer problems, discovery, and validating new offerings; they own architecture, platform standards, and engineering. Put it in writing if possible, even informally.

**3. Give them real influence over your work.** Ask them to help shape your first project, choose where it touches the platform, or nominate an engineer. People rarely block what they helped design.

**4. Solve a problem for them.** Ask what they've wanted to do but couldn't get prioritized or funded, like modernizing an integration layer or building a capability customers keep asking for. If your work can advance *their* agenda, you're allies.

**5. Share credit publicly, especially with the CEO.** "This only worked because [CTO]'s team figured out how to integrate it safely" makes them look good in front of the person whose opinion matters most to them.

## If you genuinely disagree

You will at some point. When you do:

- **Disagree with them directly first,** in private, before it ever reaches the CEO.
- **Seek the underlying concern.** "Help me understand what could go wrong" often reveals a solvable issue (security, maintainability, staffing) rather than opposition.
- **Offer safeguards.** Architecture review, a sandbox environment, a time-box, a rollback plan. Show you take their risks seriously.
- **If you must escalate, do it together and transparently:** "We see this differently. Can we take it to [CEO] together and lay out both views?" Joint escalation preserves trust. Going alone destroys it.

## Watch for signs of how it's going

**Encouraging:** they share information, lend people, give candid feedback, and mention your work positively to others.

**Concerning:** repeated delays, requests getting lost, sudden new requirements, or hearing indirectly that they're skeptical of you.

If it's concerning, address it directly before involving the CEO:

> "I get the sense we're not quite aligned, and I'd rather talk about it openly than guess. What would make this work better for you?"

## A realistic perspective

- **The CTO might be right about some things.** They may understand constraints you don't yet. Being visibly willing to learn from them is a strength, not a concession.
- **You may not fully win them over, and that's okay.** Neutral and non-obstructive is a fine outcome. You don't need them to champion you, just not to block you.
- **If the relationship becomes truly unworkable** after sustained good-faith effort, that's a conversation to have with the CEO, framed around outcomes and the mandate he gave you, never around the CTO's character. But that's a months-from-now scenario, not a first-quarter one.

In your position, the CTO's trust is worth more than any single initiative. Earn it first, and many of your other obstacles will get smaller.
