# Two Toys and a Swing Set

## Toy #1: The CEO's Idea ("Fix it from inside")

**What it does:** It's like adding new pieces to the Lego set everyone already has. You work through existing teams, processes, and people.

**Who plays with it:** Everyone, in theory. In practice, the people who already run things.

**Who's happy:** The CEO, middle managers, and long-tenured staff. Nobody's turf is threatened.

**Who cries:** You, eventually. Big organizations have an immune system. New ideas get stuck in approval queues, starved of engineers who are busy with the roadmap, and sanded down until they look like everything else. "Outsized results" rarely come out of normal-sized processes.

## Toy #2: Your Idea ("Independent lab")

**What it does:** It's a fort in the backyard with its own rules. There's a small team with its own budget, speed, and freedom from legacy process.

**Who plays with it:** A handful of hand-picked people, often new hires.

**Who's happy:** The people in the lab, and maybe a few restless high performers who want in.

**Who cries:** Everyone left outside the fort. Labs often create resentment ("the cool kids get the fun toys while we keep the lights on"). The bigger risk is that labs are famous for building great prototypes that never get adopted, because the core business never felt ownership. There's also a political risk: your new boss told you what he believes, and your first move would be to tell him he's wrong.

## What Everybody Forgot

**The snacks (budget and staffing):** Who pays, and for how long? A lab without a protected 12–18 month budget gets cut in the first bad quarter. An internal effort without dedicated people gets "10% of Bob's time," which means none.

**The place to play (customers):** This is the big one. **Your company is losing customers.** That's usually not an innovation problem first. It's often a product quality, service, pricing, or integration problem. A shiny lab building next-gen features won't stop churn if customers are leaving because implementation takes 14 months or support is slow. Neither toy works unless someone knows *why* customers are leaving and why deals are lost.

**The way home (the handoff):** How does a lab idea become a real product the sales team sells and the core team supports? Most labs fail here, not at invention.

## What to Try First (Before Lunch)

**Call a customer who left.** Or ask sales ops for the last 10 lost deals and churn reasons, and actually read the notes. Then book 5 conversations this week with churned customers and lost prospects.

This is fast, costs nothing, threatens nobody, and makes you the person with the facts. It also tells you which toy you need. If the problems are fixable basics, work inside. If customers want something your current platform fundamentally can't do, you need a protected team.

## The Swing Set: A Tethered Team

Research on how established companies innovate, often called "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly & Tushman), points to a middle path that tends to beat both extremes:

- **Separate enough to move fast:** a small, dedicated team (5–8 people) with its own lightweight process and a protected budget.
- **Connected enough to land:** staffed partly with respected insiders, not just outsiders. A core business leader co-sponsors each project, and the CEO acts as the bridge.
- **Aimed at the pain:** the first project attacks a real churn or lost-deal problem, not a moonshot. An early visible win buys you permission for bigger bets later.
- **Rotating seats:** people from the core business cycle through the team, so everyone gets a turn on the swings and brings the habits back.

This also solves your political problem. You can pitch it to the CEO as honoring his instinct ("we're using our existing talent") while getting the protection you think is necessary ("but giving them room to move fast").

## Bottom Line

You're probably right that pure "work within the system" won't produce outsized results. But a fully independent lab is a risky first move for a new leader and often fails at the handoff. Start with customer truth this week, run one tethered-team pilot on a real churn problem for about 90 days, and let the results tell you whether to make the fort bigger or tear down more walls.

# Will They Be Mad?

Usually not. Most former customers are fine being asked, and many are quietly flattered. What makes people mad is **a disguised sales pitch.** If your "feedback call" turns into "have you seen our new release?", you've burned the bridge. Asking "we want to understand what we got wrong" rarely upsets anyone.

Expect maybe 1 in 4 or 5 to say yes. That's normal, and 5 good conversations will teach you a lot.

## How to Ask So They Say Yes

- **Keep it small:** "20 minutes," not "an hour."
- **Say why:** "I'm new, I lead innovation, and I want to hear what we got wrong. No sales pitch, I promise."
- **Pick the right messenger:** A note from you (new, no baggage) often works better than one from their old account rep. If they left on bad terms with a specific person, definitely don't send that person.
- **Offer something back:** A short summary of what you learned, or a small thank-you like a gift card or donation, if your company allows it.
- **Don't argue:** If they say "your support was terrible," say "thank you, tell me more," not "actually, we improved that."

## If They Won't Talk, Try Other Doors

1. **Your own people:** Sales reps, account managers, support staff, and implementation teams know why customers left. Buy them lunch and ask. They're often dying to tell someone.
2. **Customers who are about to leave:** Talk to at-risk current customers. They're still invested in making it work, so they're often more willing, and you might save them.
3. **Lost prospects:** People who picked a competitor over you are often more candid than churned customers.
4. **The paper trail:** Support tickets, CRM loss notes, cancellation emails, and survey scores. These are less rich than a conversation, but you can read them today.
5. **A neutral third party:** "Win/loss analysis" firms interview churned customers for you. People are often more honest with an outsider.
6. **Public reviews:** Sites like G2 or industry forums, if your products are listed there.

## The Big Idea

You're not looking for one perfect interview. You're looking for **the same complaint showing up from several directions.** If support tickets, sales reps, and two former customers all mention slow implementation, you've found your first problem to solve, and a strong starting point for your pilot.

# When Nothing Matches

This happens a lot, and it's usually not as messy as it looks. Different complaints often hide a common cause, and even real messiness is itself a finding.

## 1. Look Under the Complaints

People describe **symptoms**, not causes. Group them one level deeper:

- "Setup took forever"
- "Your team never got back to us"
- "We couldn't get our data migrated"

These sound different, but they might all be **"we're slow and hard to work with."** Try sorting every complaint into a few buckets, such as *product*, *price*, *service*, *speed*, and *fit*, and see if one fills up.

## 2. Split the Customers into Groups

Sometimes nothing matches because you're mixing different kinds of customers. Small insurers might leave over price, while big carriers leave over missing features. Sort by **size, product line, region, or how long they were customers,** and patterns often appear inside each group.

## 3. Look at *When* They Left

Timing can reveal what words don't:

- **Left in year one?** Likely onboarding or implementation.
- **Left after many years?** Likely falling behind competitors.
- **Left right after a price increase or a release?** That's your clue.

## 4. Count the Money, Not Just the Votes

One complaint from your three biggest lost accounts may matter more than ten complaints from tiny ones. Weight what you hear by **revenue lost**.

## 5. If It's Truly Scattered, That Means Something Too

If there's still no pattern, a few explanations are likely:

- **Death by a thousand cuts:** There's no single broken part. The whole experience is just a bit worse than competitors'. The fix is usually overall quality and responsiveness, not one big feature.
- **Reasons outside your control:** Mergers, budget cuts, or a new executive who brought their favorite vendor. Worth knowing, so you don't chase ghosts.
- **Not enough conversations yet:** With 5 interviews, noise is normal. Patterns usually appear somewhere around 10–15.
- **The questions are too narrow:** Try asking "Walk me through the moment you decided to leave" instead of "What went wrong?" Stories reveal more than opinions.

## 6. Pick One Anyway

You said you want something to try and fix if it's wrong. So don't wait for perfect agreement. Choose the problem that is:

- **Mentioned by your biggest lost customers**, and
- **Small enough to test in 30–90 days**

Run your pilot on that. If it doesn't move the numbers, you've learned something and you pick the next one. Scattered answers don't stop you. They just mean your first experiment is a smart guess instead of a sure thing.

# Will a Miss Hurt You?

**It depends much less on whether the first try works and much more on how you set it up.** CEOs rarely lose faith in someone over one failed experiment. They lose faith when they're surprised, when money disappears with nothing learned, or when someone seems to be guessing.

Being new cuts both ways. You haven't built up much trust yet, but you also get a grace period, and people expect you to be learning.

## Set It Up as an Experiment Before You Start

The same result reads very differently depending on what came before:

- **Without setup:** "We tried to fix churn and it didn't work." That sounds like failure.
- **With setup:** "We ran a 60-day test on our top hypothesis. It didn't move the numbers, so we ruled it out and we're moving to the next one." That sounds like a leader doing their job.

Before launching, agree with the CEO on:

- **What you're testing, and why you picked it**
- **What success looks like**, such as "cut onboarding time by 30% for new customers"
- **When you'll decide**, such as "we'll review at 60 days"
- **What happens if it fails**: you stop, share what you learned, and try the next idea

Once he's agreed to those terms, a miss is part of the plan he approved, not a surprise.

## Other Ways to Protect Yourself

- **Keep the first bet small.** Cheap failures are forgivable. Expensive ones are remembered.
- **Don't let him be surprised.** Send short updates every couple of weeks, especially when things look shaky. Bad news delivered early builds trust.
- **Run two or three small bets instead of one big one.** Then one miss is just part of the mix.
- **Tie it to what he cares about.** He wants to turn the ship around with existing people. Show how the experiment uses and grows his team.
- **Show what you learned.** "We found out implementation isn't the main issue, but pricing clearly is" is real value, even if the fix didn't work.

## Something You Could Say to Him

> "I want to move fast, which means some of what we try won't work. I'd rather find that out in 60 days for a small cost than in a year for a big one. Here's my first bet, how we'll measure it, and when we'll decide. If it misses, you'll hear it from me first, along with what we learned and what's next."

Most CEOs hear that as confidence, not weakness.

## One Honest Caveat

A few leaders really do punish any failure. If you run a well-framed, cheap, transparent experiment and he still treats a miss as incompetence, that tells you something important about whether "outsized results" are actually possible there. That would be worth knowing early too.
