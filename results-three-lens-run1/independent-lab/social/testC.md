## Turn 1

**User:** Use impression management to answer the following.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

# Short Answer

**Both, sequenced carefully.** Your instinct toward an independent lab is often right on substance. But you're new, and the CEO has publicly committed to the opposite view. Impression management suggests you shouldn't frame this as "your strategy vs. his." Instead, build credibility inside the system first, then earn the autonomy to create a protected innovation unit that he sees as *his* strategy working.

---

## Why Impression Management Matters Here

Impression management is how people shape others' perceptions of them to gain trust, influence, and resources. Right now you have three perception problems:

1. **You're an unknown.** You haven't banked credibility (what Hollander calls "idiosyncrasy credits") that lets you deviate from norms.
2. **Your idea implicitly criticizes the CEO.** "We need to get away from the existing organization" can sound like "your belief in our people and systems is wrong."
3. **You may be seen as a threat by the organization.** Separate labs often trigger an "us vs. them" dynamic, with the core business resenting the "cool kids" who get freedom and funding.

Being right isn't enough if you're perceived as arrogant, disloyal, or naive about the company.

---

## The Substance: Your Instinct Has Merit

Research on organizational ambidexterity (O'Reilly & Tushman) and disruptive innovation (Christensen) shows that established organizations often smother new ideas. Existing processes, metrics, and incentives are optimized for the current business. So an independent structure is frequently necessary for *breakthrough* innovation.

However, fully isolated labs often fail too. They produce ideas the core business rejects or can't scale. The most successful model is usually **structurally separate but strategically integrated**: its own processes and metrics, while staying connected to leadership, customers, and the core organization's resources.

So the real question isn't *which* strategy. It's **how to get to the right structure without losing the CEO's support.**

---

## Impression Management Tactics to Apply

Using Jones & Pittman's framework of self-presentation strategies:

| Tactic | How to Use It | Risk to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Ingratiation** (genuine alignment) | Echo the CEO's language: "turn the ship around," "leverage our talent." Sincerely acknowledge what the organization does well. | Flattery that looks hollow |
| **Exemplification** (demonstrate commitment) | Spend early weeks listening to customers, sales, and engineers. Show you respect the institution. | Appearing to be a "yes person" |
| **Self-promotion** (competence) | Deliver a visible quick win using existing teams. Prove you can produce results inside the system. | Overclaiming credit for others' work |
| **Avoid intimidation** | Don't imply "the old way is broken and I'm here to fix it." | Alienating the CEO and middle managers |
| **Avoid supplication** | Don't appear helpless or overly dependent on getting your way. | Looking weak or unsure |

---

## A Phased Approach

### Phase 1: Earn Credibility (First 60–90 days)
- Conduct a listening tour: churned customers, lost deals, frontline staff.
- Identify *specifically* why innovation is failing (approval cycles, risk aversion, legacy tech, incentives).
- Launch one or two small pilots **within existing systems** to show respect for the CEO's view.
- Document the friction you encounter. This becomes your evidence.

### Phase 2: Reframe the Lab as the CEO's Idea Extended
Don't pitch "an independent lab." Pitch something like:
> *"You're right that our talent is our advantage. To unlock it, I'd like to create a protected space where our best people can move at startup speed, without being slowed by processes designed for our core business."*

This frames the lab as **using existing talent**, which is consistent with his view, not a rejection of it. Label it with terms he'll embrace: "accelerator," "tiger team," "customer innovation studio."

### Phase 3: Build a Bridge, Not a Wall
- Staff the lab partly with respected internal people, not only outside hires.
- Give the CEO visible ownership: he sponsors it, and he presents the wins.
- Create formal handoff paths so ideas return to the core business.
- Share credit generously with core teams to reduce resentment.

---

## A Note on Authenticity

Impression management works best when it's **strategic framing of genuine beliefs**, not manipulation. If you truly believe the CEO is wrong, don't pretend to agree indefinitely. Instead, *earn the standing to disagree effectively*. If he rejects a protected unit even after you've built credibility and presented evidence, that's important information about whether you can succeed in this role.

---

## Bottom Line

- **On substance:** A semi-independent innovation unit is likely necessary for outsized results.
- **On perception:** Pushing it immediately risks your credibility and the CEO's support.
- **The winning move:** Show respect for the existing organization, deliver early results within it, gather evidence of the constraints, and then introduce the lab as a natural evolution of the CEO's own vision.

You want the CEO to leave the conversation thinking, *"This is how we turn the ship around, and I made it happen,"* not *"The new hire told me I was wrong."*

# Managing Middle Managers Through Impression Management

Middle managers can quietly kill an innovation lab. They control the people, budgets, data, and customer access you'll need, and they can slow-walk requests without ever openly opposing you. The goal is for them to see the lab as **an asset to their careers**, not a threat to their status.

---

## First, Understand the Threat From Their Perspective

Impression management starts with knowing how *you* are perceived. To a middle manager, you and your lab may signal:

| What They May Fear | What They're Thinking |
|---|---|
| **Talent loss** | "You're going to poach my best people." |
| **Implied criticism** | "The lab exists because we failed." |
| **Status loss** | "The exciting work goes to the lab. I get maintenance." |
| **Resource competition** | "Your budget comes out of mine." |
| **Being bypassed** | "Decisions about my customers and products will happen without me." |
| **Extra work** | "I'll be stuck implementing your ideas with no added resources." |
| **Career risk** | "If the lab succeeds, I look bad. If it fails, I get blamed for not supporting it." |

Your job is to change what the lab *means* to them.

---

## Applying the Impression Management Tactics

### 1. Ingratiation: Show Genuine Respect for Their Expertise
Middle managers know where the bodies are buried: why customers churn, which systems are fragile, what's been tried before.
- Seek their advice *before* you have a plan. People support what they help create.
- Credit their insights publicly: "Maria's team flagged this churn pattern two years ago."
- Acknowledge the constraints they've been operating under. Many have wanted to innovate but were blocked by the same systems you're trying to escape.

**Avoid:** Flattery without follow-through. If you ask for input and ignore it, you'll be seen as manipulative.

### 2. Exemplification: Demonstrate That You're Not Above the Organization
- Sit in on their team meetings, customer calls, and support escalations.
- Show that you understand the day-to-day pressures of running the core business.
- Model the collaboration you want: share information freely and respond quickly to their requests.

**Avoid:** Acting like the "special" executive who doesn't deal with operational reality.

### 3. Self-Promotion, Redirected: Promote *Them*, Not Yourself
With peers and subordinates, self-promotion often backfires. Make the lab a platform for *their* visibility.
- Let managers present joint wins to the CEO.
- Frame successes as "the lab and the claims team" rather than "the lab."
- Tie lab outcomes to metrics they're already measured on, such as retention, renewal rates, and deal velocity.

### 4. Avoid Intimidation
Even subtle signals of "I have the CEO's backing" create compliance, not commitment.
- Never say "the CEO wants this" to force cooperation, except as a last resort.
- Don't describe the core business as "legacy," "slow," or "the old way." Those words spread fast.

### 5. Avoid Supplication
Don't appear to be begging for support. It signals the lab is weak and optional. Position collaboration as a mutual opportunity, not a favor.

---

## Structural Moves That Reinforce the Impression

Perception has to be backed by real design choices, or the framing will collapse.

**Talent rotation instead of poaching**
- Create 6–12 month rotations where people join the lab and then *return* to their teams with new skills.
- Managers get credit for developing talent, and their people come back more capable.
- Ask managers to *nominate* candidates rather than recruiting around them.

**A "customer" relationship, not a rivalry**
- Treat business-unit managers as the lab's clients. Ask them: "What problem keeps you up at night that you've never had the resources to solve?"
- Work on *their* problems first. This makes the lab a service to them, not a competitor.

**Shared ownership of outcomes**
- Give managers a seat on a lab advisory group or project review.
- When an idea moves into production, the receiving manager should own and present it.

**Resource clarity**
- Be transparent about where the lab's funding comes from. Ambiguity breeds suspicion that budgets are being quietly raided.
- Where possible, bring resources *to* managers when lab projects transfer into their areas, so adoption isn't just extra work.

---

## A Phased Approach

### Phase 1: Listen Before You Launch
- Meet one-on-one with key managers before the lab is announced.
- Ask: *"What have you tried that didn't get traction? What would you do with a small team and fewer approval layers?"*
- Identify **potential allies** (frustrated innovators), **neutral pragmatists**, and **likely resisters**.

### Phase 2: Co-Create With Early Allies
- Pick one or two respected managers for the first pilot projects.
- Solve a real problem for them quickly and visibly.
- Let *them* tell the story to their peers. Peer endorsement is far more credible than yours.

### Phase 3: Expand Through Demonstrated Value
- Share results in terms managers care about: fewer escalations, retained accounts, faster sales cycles.
- Invite neutral managers in once there's proof.
- Address resisters privately and directly (see below).

---

## Handling the Resisters

Some managers will stay skeptical. Useful approaches:

- **Surface the concern privately.** "I get the sense the lab might create problems for your team. I'd rather hear it directly and fix it."
- **Separate legitimate concerns from territorial ones.** A manager worried about losing a key engineer during a critical release has a valid point. Accommodate it.
- **Offer a low-risk entry point.** A small, bounded collaboration lets them test you without committing.
- **Escalate only as a last resort.** If someone actively obstructs after good-faith efforts, involve the CEO, but understand you'll spend political capital and may damage the relationship.

---

## Sample Framing

**Instead of:**
> "The lab will move faster because it won't have to deal with the bureaucracy in your area."

**Try:**
> "Your team knows our customers better than anyone. The lab gives you a way to test the ideas you've never had room for, with a small team that can move fast. When something works, it comes back to your team, and you get the credit for it."

**Instead of:**
> "I'd like to pull Sam onto the lab team."

**Try:**
> "Sam came up in several conversations as someone with great ideas. Would you consider nominating Sam for a six-month rotation? Sam would come back with new skills, and your team would have a direct line into the lab's work."

---

## A Note on Authenticity

Middle managers are often very good at detecting insincerity, having watched many "transformation initiatives" come and go. The framing only works if it's true:
- If you promise rotations, bring people back.
- If you promise credit, give it publicly, especially in front of the CEO.
- If you ask for input, show how it changed your plans.

Every broken promise confirms their suspicion that the lab is a threat dressed up as a partnership.

---

## Bottom Line

- Middle managers resist what threatens their **talent, status, resources, and credibility**.
- Use **ingratiation** (respect their expertise), **exemplification** (engage with operational reality), and **redirected self-promotion** (make them look good).
- Avoid **intimidation** (leaning on CEO authority) and **supplication** (begging for support).
- Back your framing with real structures: rotations, shared ownership, and solving their problems first.

The goal is that when the lab succeeds, middle managers say *"we did that,"* not *"they did that without us."*

# Managing Executive Peers Through Impression Management

Executive peers present a different challenge than middle managers. They're your **equals in status**, they have **their own direct line to the CEO**, and they're often competing, openly or quietly, for influence, budget, and future succession. A new executive brought in to "produce outsized results" can easily look like the CEO's new favorite, or like an implied judgment that the existing team hasn't delivered.

Unlike middle managers, peers can't be won over mainly by helping them look good to *you*. You need them to see you as an **ally who increases their standing**, not a rival who diminishes it.

---

## First, Understand How You're Being Perceived

| What Peers May Fear | What They're Thinking |
|---|---|
| **Loss of CEO attention** | "The new person gets the CEO's time, and I get less." |
| **Implied failure** | "They hired an innovation chief because the rest of us didn't innovate." |
| **Turf overlap** | "Innovation sounds a lot like product, technology, or strategy, which is *my* job." |
| **Budget competition** | "Every dollar for the lab is a dollar I don't get." |
| **Credit theft** | "When my team delivers, the lab will claim it was their innovation." |
| **Succession threat** | "Are they being groomed for a bigger role?" |
| **Being made to look slow** | "The lab moves fast by skipping the controls my function is responsible for." |

The more the CEO praises you publicly, the more acute these fears become. Early favor is an asset with the CEO and a liability with peers.

---

## Role-Specific Concerns

Each executive sees you through their own lens. Tailor your approach accordingly.

| Peer | Likely Concern | How to Position the Lab |
|---|---|---|
| **CTO / CIO** | "You'll build shadow IT, create security risks, and hand me systems to maintain." | Involve them in architecture standards. Make the lab a proving ground for technologies they've wanted to adopt. |
| **Chief Product Officer** | "Innovation is product strategy. You're taking my job." | Clearly divide scope: the lab explores and validates, product scales and owns the roadmap. Make them the landing place for successful ideas. |
| **Chief Revenue Officer / Sales** | "You'll distract customers or promise things we can't sell." | Build around the deals they're losing and the objections they hear. Give them new stories to tell customers. |
| **CFO** | "This is an unaccountable cost center." | Show disciplined stage-gate funding, clear metrics, and willingness to kill projects. Speak in terms of ROI and risk. |
| **COO / Customer Success** | "You'll create operational chaos and more work for my teams." | Target churn drivers and operational pain first. Plan handoffs and support models in advance. |
| **Chief Legal / Compliance** | "Insurance is regulated. You'll create liability." | Bring them in early as design partners, not late-stage approvers. |
| **CHRO** | "You'll disrupt compensation equity and poach talent." | Partner on rotation programs and career-development framing. |

---

## Applying the Impression Management Tactics

### 1. Ingratiation: Build Genuine Relationships, Not Alliances of Convenience
With peers, ingratiation must feel **collegial, not political**. Obvious alliance-building will be read as maneuvering.
- Meet each peer one-on-one early, ideally before your strategy is finalized.
- Ask what they need, what's worked, and what they'd do differently.
- Learn their priorities and pressures from the CEO and board.
- Find real common ground. Most of them are also under pressure from churn and lost deals.

**Avoid:** Agreeing with everyone. Peers compare notes, and inconsistency will destroy your credibility quickly.

### 2. Exemplification: Be the Team Player, Visibly
Exemplification is especially powerful with peers because it counters the "golden child" narrative.
- Support peers' initiatives in executive meetings, not just your own.
- Volunteer the lab's help on *their* problems.
- Accept the same constraints they do: budget discipline, reporting cadence, governance.
- Don't ask for exceptions you can't justify.

**Avoid:** Visible perks or special treatment that signal you're playing by different rules.

### 3. Self-Promotion: Promote Shared Results
With peers, self-promotion is the highest-risk tactic. Overt claims of credit breed resentment fast.
- Report outcomes as joint achievements: "The CRO's team and the lab closed..."
- Let peers present lab-supported wins that fall in their domain.
- Save your own self-promotion for areas that are unambiguously yours, and keep it factual.

Paradoxically, the CEO usually notices who *enables* others' success. Generosity with credit is itself a signal of leadership.

### 4. Supplication, Used Carefully: Ask for Help
Supplication is usually risky, but a limited version works well with peers.
- Ask experienced peers for advice on navigating the company: "You've been here eight years. What am I missing about how decisions get made?"
- This leverages the **Ben Franklin effect**: people who help you tend to like you more.
- It also signals humility, which directly counters the "arrogant new hire" impression.

**Avoid:** Appearing incapable or dependent. Ask for *insight*, not rescue.

### 5. Avoid Intimidation: Don't Weaponize Your CEO Access
This is the most important rule with peers.
- Never say "the CEO and I discussed this" to settle a disagreement.
- Don't take disputes to the CEO before trying to resolve them directly.
- Don't let the CEO become your go-between with a peer.
- If the CEO asks you about a peer's area, be fair and constructive. Assume everything will get back to them.

Being seen as someone who "goes around" peers is extremely hard to recover from.

---

## Managing the CEO Relationship With Peers in Mind

Your peers are watching how you interact with the CEO. Some practical guidelines:

- **Don't monopolize the CEO's time.** Be efficient and structured in your one-on-ones.
- **Be transparent about what you discuss.** If a topic affects a peer, tell them before or right after.
- **Encourage the CEO to frame the lab as a team asset.** Ask him to present it as serving the entire leadership team, not as a separate power center.
- **Push back on excessive public praise.** A quiet request helps: "I appreciate it, but I'd rather the credit go to the team. It'll make the collaboration easier."
- **Deflect comparisons.** If the CEO says "Why can't everyone move as fast as the lab?", immediately contextualize: "We have different mandates. The lab can move fast because the core teams are keeping the business running."

That last move matters enormously. One unchallenged comparison can create a lasting rival.

---

## A Phased Approach

### Phase 1: Listen and Map (First 30–60 days)
- One-on-ones with every peer before finalizing your plan.
- Understand each person's goals, pressures, informal influence, and relationship with the CEO.
- Identify overlaps in scope and potential friction points early.

### Phase 2: Clarify Boundaries and Co-Design
- Propose explicit scope boundaries, especially with product and technology leaders.
- Share your draft lab plan with peers **before** presenting it to the CEO. No one likes being surprised in an executive meeting.
- Incorporate their feedback visibly.

### Phase 3: Create Shared Wins
- Choose early lab projects that directly help one or two peers hit their goals.
- Let those peers champion the results.
- Consider a **lab steering committee** that includes executive peers, giving them real influence and shared ownership.

### Phase 4: Maintain the Relationships
- Regular check-ins, even when nothing is urgent.
- Proactively flag anything touching their domains.
- Keep delivering help, not just asking for it.

---

## Handling a Peer Who Sees You as a Rival

Despite your best efforts, one or two peers may remain competitive. Approaches:

- **Address it directly and privately.** "I sense some tension about the lab's role. I'd like to understand your concerns and figure out how we work together."
- **Clarify your intentions.** If you're not angling for their role, or for succession, say so, but only if it's true.
- **Formalize boundaries.** Written scope agreements or RACI charts can reduce territorial disputes.
- **Continue to be generous.** Consistent good faith often wins over skeptics with time. It also makes any continued obstruction visible to others.
- **Escalate only after documented good-faith efforts,** and frame it as a problem to solve together, not a complaint: "We're struggling to align on scope. Could you help us clarify?"

---

## Sample Framing

**Instead of:**
> "The CEO brought me in to drive innovation across the company."

**Try:**
> "The CEO asked me to help build some new capabilities, but the core business is where the value is. I'd like to understand what you're trying to achieve and how the lab could actually help you get there."

**Instead of (to the CPO):**
> "The lab will handle new product ideas."

**Try:**
> "I see the lab as an extension of your team's capacity to explore. We test ideas quickly, and the ones that work land on your roadmap for you to own and scale. How should we set up that handoff?"

**Instead of (in an executive meeting):**
> "The lab delivered a prototype in six weeks."

**Try:**
> "Working with the CRO's team, we validated a prototype in six weeks. Her team's customer insight made that possible."

---

## A Note on Authenticity

Executive peers are sophisticated observers of political behavior. They've seen new leaders arrive with charm offensives that disappear once they've secured the CEO's favor.
- Be consistent in what you say to each person.
- Keep your commitments, especially about credit and scope.
- Never privately undermine a peer to the CEO. It almost always surfaces eventually.

Over time, your reputation among peers will come less from what you say and more from **whether they're better off because you joined.**

---

## Bottom Line

- Peers fear losing **CEO attention, turf, budget, credit, and status**.
- Use **ingratiation** (real relationships), **exemplification** (visible team play), and **limited supplication** (asking for their advice).
- Use **self-promotion** sparingly and focus on shared results.
- Above all, **never weaponize your access to the CEO**, and help him frame the lab as a team asset rather than a rival power center.

The goal is for your peers to see you not as competition for the CEO's attention, but as the executive who helps *them* win that attention.

# Presenting the Lab to Customers Through Impression Management

Customers are a very different audience from the CEO, middle managers, or executive peers. Internally, you're managing perceptions of *yourself*. With customers, you're managing perceptions of the *entire company*, and at-risk customers have already formed a negative impression. They've likely concluded your company is **slow, unresponsive, or falling behind**.

That changes the task. You're not creating an impression from scratch. You're **repairing a damaged one**. And the biggest danger is that announcing an innovation lab confirms their suspicion that you're more interested in shiny new things than in fixing the problems they already have.

---

## First, Understand How Customers Will Perceive the Lab

| Customer Perception Risk | What They're Thinking |
|---|---|
| **Innovation theater** | "Another announcement. They still haven't fixed the bugs I reported last year." |
| **Misplaced priorities** | "They're funding a lab while my support tickets sit for weeks." |
| **Empty promises** | "We've heard roadmap promises before. None of them shipped." |
| **Disruption risk** | "Insurance is regulated. I don't want to be forced into an unproven platform or a painful migration." |
| **Guinea pig fears** | "Are they going to experiment on my business?" |
| **Cost concerns** | "Will this mean price increases or paid upgrades?" |
| **Desperation signal** | "They're losing customers and scrambling. Should we get out before it gets worse?" |

That last point matters. Customers talk to each other, especially in a concentrated industry like insurance. If the lab looks like a panic move, it can *accelerate* churn.

---

## The Core Principle: Earn the Right to Talk About the Future

At-risk customers generally won't believe claims about tomorrow until you've demonstrated change today. The sequencing is:

1. **Acknowledge** what's gone wrong.
2. **Fix** visible problems.
3. **Involve** customers in shaping what comes next.
4. **Demonstrate** results before announcing them broadly.

In impression management terms, this is the difference between **assertive tactics** (claiming a new identity) and **defensive tactics** (repairing a damaged one). With at-risk customers, repair comes first.

---

## Applying the Impression Management Tactics

### 1. Accounts and Apologies: Acknowledge the Problem
Research on organizational impression management (such as Elsbach's work on verbal accounts) shows that when an organization's reputation is damaged, **how it explains the problem** strongly shapes whether stakeholders trust it again.
- **Acknowledge specific failures** rather than speaking vaguely: "We've been too slow to deliver the integrations you asked for."
- **Take responsibility** rather than making excuses about market conditions or technical debt.
- **Describe concrete changes**, not just good intentions.

Denial or deflection with a customer who's already frustrated usually destroys whatever credibility remains.

**Avoid:** Over-apologizing in ways that create legal exposure or suggest systemic incompetence. Coordinate with Legal and Customer Success on language.

### 2. Ingratiation: Listen Seriously
With customers, ingratiation means **demonstrating that you value their perspective**, not flattery.
- Visit at-risk accounts personally, alongside their account team.
- Ask what would make them stay, what competitors offer that you don't, and what problems they're solving with workarounds.
- Follow up in writing with what you heard and what you'll do.

**Avoid:** Listening tours with no visible follow-through. That's worse than not asking.

### 3. Exemplification: Show Commitment Through Action
Before talking about the lab, show that you're committed to the basics.
- Partner with Customer Success and Product to resolve one or two long-standing pain points for key at-risk accounts.
- Assign senior attention, including your own, to escalations.
- Deliver something small and fast. Speed of response is itself a message.

This is where your internal relationships pay off. You'll need the CRO, COO, CPO, and CTO to deliver these fixes. The lab's credibility with customers depends on the core business's credibility.

### 4. Supplication, Reframed: Invite Them to Help Shape the Future
Careful supplication works well with customers when framed as **partnership rather than need**.
- Invite select customers to become **design partners** or join a **customer innovation council**.
- Position it as privileged access: "We want our most experienced customers shaping what we build."
- Involve them in defining problems, testing prototypes, and prioritizing ideas.

This turns at-risk customers from critics into co-owners. It's much harder to leave a product you helped design.

**Avoid:** Framing that sounds like "please don't leave, we'll do anything." That signals desperation and weakens your negotiating position.

### 5. Self-Promotion: Let Evidence Speak
Customers are skeptical of vendor claims. Self-promotion works only when it's specific and verifiable.
- Share **results**, not plans: "We reduced claims processing time by 30% for a pilot customer."
- Use customer voices through case studies and references rather than your own marketing.
- Demonstrate working prototypes, not slide decks.

**Avoid:** Buzzwords like "AI-powered transformation" or "reimagining insurance." At-risk customers hear those as signs you're out of touch with their actual problems.

### 6. Avoid Intimidation: No Fear-Based Selling
- Don't imply they'll fall behind competitors if they don't adopt your new ideas.
- Don't suggest older versions will lose support unless that's confirmed and handled carefully.
- Don't use contract terms or switching costs as implicit threats.

Pressure might delay a departure, but it guarantees resentment and eventual churn.

---

## Segment Your Customers

Not every customer should hear the same message at the same time.

| Segment | Approach |
|---|---|
| **At-risk customers** | Lead with acknowledgment and fixes. Offer design-partner roles only once you've shown progress. Keep the lab message quiet and tied to *their* problems. |
| **Stable, satisfied customers** | Share the lab as a sign of continued investment. Invite interested ones into pilots. |
| **Strategic or advocate customers** | Involve them early as co-creators and future references. Their endorsement helps reach at-risk peers. |
| **Recently lost customers** | Consider win-back conversations later, once you have proof points. Lead with what has changed, not what's planned. |
| **Prospects** | Use lab results as evidence of momentum, but only verified outcomes. |

---

## A Phased Approach

### Phase 1: Listen and Fix (First 60–90 days)
- Personally meet key at-risk accounts with their account teams.
- Identify the top two or three pain points driving churn.
- Work with core teams to deliver visible fixes quickly.
- Don't announce the lab publicly yet.

### Phase 2: Invite Co-Creation
- Form a small customer advisory group or design-partner program, including a mix of at-risk and advocate customers.
- Frame lab projects around the problems customers identified.
- Set clear expectations about timelines, what's experimental, and what's committed.

### Phase 3: Demonstrate Results
- Pilot solutions with design partners.
- Measure outcomes that matter to customers: time saved, errors reduced, compliance simplified.
- Build case studies with customer permission.

### Phase 4: Communicate More Broadly
- Once you have proof, share the lab's work with the wider customer base, at user conferences, in account reviews, and through case studies.
- Frame it as "what we built with our customers," not "what our lab invented."

---

## Coordinating With Internal Stakeholders

Your customer message must be consistent with what account teams say and what the company can deliver.
- **Align with the CRO and Customer Success** before contacting any customer. Showing up at an account without them undermines everything you did in the previous phase with peers.
- **Brief account managers** so they can speak confidently about the lab and don't feel bypassed.
- **Work with Legal and Compliance** on what you can promise, especially in a regulated industry. Avoid forward-looking commitments that could create contractual or reputational exposure.
- **Keep the CEO aligned.** If he's meeting customers, make sure he's delivering the same message.

---

## Sample Framing

**Instead of:**
> "We've launched an innovation lab to transform the future of insurance software."

**Try:**
> "We know we haven't moved fast enough on the issues you've raised. We've made changes to fix that, starting with the integration problems your team flagged. We've also created a small team that works directly with customers to solve problems faster. We'd value your input on what they should focus on first."

**Instead of (to an at-risk customer):**
> "Our lab is working on some exciting new capabilities that will address your concerns."

**Try:**
> "You told us the renewal workflow is costing your team hours every week. We've fixed the two most urgent issues. We'd also like to work with you on a better long-term solution, and you'd see and shape it before anyone else."

**Instead of (describing results):**
> "Our lab has developed a revolutionary new claims tool."

**Try:**
> "We built a new claims tool with three of our customers. In the pilot, they cut processing time by about a third. One of them would be glad to talk with you about their experience."

---

## Handling a Skeptical, At-Risk Customer

When a customer says "We've heard this before":
- **Agree with them.** "You're right, and you have good reason to be skeptical."
- **Don't argue with their experience.** Arguing reinforces the perception that you don't listen.
- **Offer a small, verifiable commitment.** "Let me show you progress on one specific issue in the next 30 days. If we don't deliver, you'll know where we stand."
- **Follow through exactly as promised.** One kept commitment does more than any presentation.

If they're already leaving, stay gracious. A respectful exit keeps the door open for a win-back, and protects your reputation with the other customers they talk to.

---

## A Note on Authenticity

Customers who are close to leaving are highly attuned to spin. The framing only works if:
- You're **genuinely fixing** the problems they raised, not just talking about them.
- Design-partner programs **actually influence** what gets built.
- Results are **real and verifiable**, not selectively presented.
- You **never promise capabilities** the company can't deliver.

In a regulated industry built on trust, the damage from one broken promise can outweigh the benefit of many announcements.

---

## Bottom Line

- At-risk customers already have a **negative impression**. Your job is repair before promotion.
- Use **accounts and apologies** (acknowledge problems), **ingratiation** (listen seriously), and **exemplification** (fix things first).
- Use **supplication as partnership** by inviting them to co-create, and **self-promotion only through evidence**.
- Avoid **intimidation** and anything that signals **desperation**.
- Coordinate tightly with account teams, Legal, and the CEO so every message is consistent.

The goal is for at-risk customers to see the lab not as a distraction from their problems, but as **proof that the company is finally listening, and is building the future with them.**
