# Short answer

Neither pure version wins the friendship contest. The **within-the-system** idea makes the grown-ups at the big table like you but may not change anything. The **independent lab** makes a few kids think you're amazing and makes most of the class suspicious. There's a third idea (below) that gets you most of the friends *and* the results. The biggest risk right now is that **you and the CEO disagree**, and he's the one who hands out gold stars.

---

## Idea 1: Play inside the big classroom (the CEO's way)

**Who gives you a gold star:**
- **The boss (CEO):** He picked this plan, so you look like a team player.
- **Longtime helpers (middle managers, engineering leads):** Nobody takes their toys away. They feel respected.
- **Sales and support:** They like improvements to what they already sell.

**Who gets in trouble or has to say sorry:**
- **You, later.** If churn keeps happening, the story becomes "we hired an innovation leader and nothing changed." You were hired for *outsized* results, and incremental ones won't look outsized.
- **Frustrated rebels inside the company:** The people who've been waiting for change may quietly give up or leave.

**Customers:** They'll like small fixes. But if they're leaving because the product feels outdated, polishing it won't bring them back.

**Everybody's moms (board, employees' families, the market):** It feels safe with no scary headlines. It's also forgettable.

**Cape vs. chores:** This is almost all chores. It's real work, but it's invisible. Few people will notice or credit it.

---

## Idea 2: Build a separate clubhouse (your instinct)

**Who gives you a gold star:**
- **You and the lab kids:** Speed, freedom, and exciting work. It's great for recruiting.
- **Some customers:** Especially prospects who want something new and modern.
- **The press and the industry:** A shiny lab gets attention.

**Who gets in trouble or has to say sorry:**
- **The CEO, in a way.** Your plan implies his plan was wrong. That's a hard message to deliver in your first months.
- **The core organization** feels judged: "The cool kids got a clubhouse, and we got told we're the problem."
- **You, if the lab builds things the main company can't absorb.** This is the classic failure. The lab ships a clever prototype, the core org can't sell, support, or integrate it, and it dies. In insurance software, compliance, integrations, and carrier relationships make this especially likely.

**Customers:** Existing customers may worry the old product is being abandoned, which can *increase* churn.

**Everybody's moms:** It looks exciting for about 12–18 months. Then everyone asks what it has earned.

**Cape vs. chores:** This is mostly cape. Here, being loud and visible matters a lot, and that's the danger. "Innovation theater" can win friends early and lose them all at budget time.

## Who sits together at lunch

| Idea | Lunch table forms around... | Who eats alone |
|---|---|---|
| Inside the system | CEO + existing leadership + ops | Change-hungry employees, you (eventually) |
| Independent lab | You + new hires + a few bold customers | Core engineering, sales, support, and possibly the CEO |
| Third idea (below) | CEO + selected core leaders + lab team + key customers | Hopefully just the people who resist *everything* |

The pattern: whoever gets to **own the win** joins your table. Whoever gets **blamed for the problem** sits elsewhere.

---

## The third idea: a clubhouse with a door that opens into the classroom

Researchers call this "ambidextrous" organization. Practically, it looks like this:

1. **Start from the CEO's frame, not against it.** Present it as "using our existing talent and systems, with a protected fast lane." He gets to be right, and you get your lab.
2. **Separate the *way of working*, not the people.** Give a small team its own rules (speed, budget, fewer approvals), but staff it partly with **respected insiders on rotation**. When they go back, they bring the new ways with them, and they become your ambassadors.
3. **Aim it at the churn, not at "the future."** Pick 2–3 problems customers are literally leaving over. That keeps the lab tied to revenue and makes sales and support want to sit with you.
4. **Give each project a core-org "adopter" from day one.** A named leader in the main business commits to taking it over if it works. This solves the problem of lab ideas dying on hand-off.
5. **Share the gold stars loudly.** When something works, credit the core team and the CEO publicly. Wear the cape for them, not for yourself.
6. **Earn more independence with results.** Begin small and connected. If early wins land, you'll have the credibility to make the lab more separate later.

---

## Your first 90 days

- **Weeks 1–4:** Talk to 15–20 churned and at-risk customers, plus sales and support. Learn *why* they're leaving. That gives you facts the CEO can't wave away.
- **Weeks 4–8:** Find 3–5 internal allies who are already trying to change things. They're your first lunch table.
- **Weeks 8–12:** Propose one or two small, fast, customer-facing bets with a clear adopter in the core business and a 90-day scoreboard.

**Bottom line:** You're probably right that real innovation needs protection from the big organization's habits. But if you build a wall, you lose the friends you need to scale anything. Build a clubhouse with a door, invite the CEO in first, and let early results earn you a bigger clubhouse.

## Turn 2

**User:** Why are the customers leaving? Is it because the software is old and boring, or because something else is broken?

**Assistant:**

I don't know, and nobody at your company knows for sure either until you ask the customers who left. But I can tell you what's most likely in insurance software, how to tell "old and boring" apart from "something is broken," and how to find the real answer quickly.

## The usual suspects in insurance software

Customers rarely leave because software is *boring*. They leave because it's **painful, slow, or expensive**. Here are the common causes, grouped by type:

**1. The product itself is behind**
- On-premise or hosted systems while competitors offer true cloud/SaaS
- Weak APIs, which make connecting to portals, data providers, and insurtech tools hard
- Launching a new insurance product or changing rates requires the vendor's developers instead of business users configuring it themselves
- Clunky screens for agents, underwriters, or claims staff
- Poor data and analytics

**2. The experience of working with you is broken**
- Implementations that run late, cost too much, or never quite finish
- Upgrades so painful that customers stay stuck on old versions
- Slow or unhelpful support
- Regulatory and compliance updates that arrive late
- Customers don't believe your roadmap or have stopped trusting you

**3. The business math doesn't work**
- Total cost of ownership is too high, especially when you add consultants and internal IT staff
- Pricing that doesn't fit how they grow
- A competitor made an aggressive offer

**4. Things outside your control**
- The customer was acquired, merged, or exited a line of business
- A new executive arrived with a preferred vendor

## How to tell which one it is

| If you hear... | It's probably... | Would an innovation lab fix it? |
|---|---|---|
| "We couldn't launch products fast enough" | Configurability / architecture | Partly, but it needs core platform work |
| "It didn't connect to anything" | APIs / integrations | Yes, a good lab target |
| "Our users hated it" | User experience | Yes, a good lab target |
| "The implementation was a nightmare" | Delivery / services | No, this is an operations fix |
| "Support never answered" | Service | No |
| "Upgrades were impossible" | Architecture *and* delivery | Partly |
| "It cost too much for what we got" | Value / pricing | Rarely |
| "We just stopped trusting you" | Relationship / credibility | No, and it's the hardest to fix |

This matters for your strategy. **If the real problems are implementation, support, and trust, a shiny innovation lab won't stop churn.** It might even annoy customers who feel you're chasing new toys while their basics stay broken. If the problems are product speed, integrations, and usability, a lab can help a lot.

My hunch: in legacy insurance software, it's usually a mix. The product is aging, *and* delivery and support have eroded. Often it's the second one that finally pushes customers out the door, while the first one is the reason they give.

## How to find the real answer

1. **Interview churned customers directly.** Aim for 10–15 conversations. Ask what triggered the decision to look elsewhere, not just why they chose the competitor. If candor is a concern, use a neutral third party, because former customers are often more honest with an outsider.
2. **Talk to at-risk customers.** They'll tell you what's broken while there's still time to fix it.
3. **Run win/loss analysis on deals you lost.** The reasons you lose new business may differ from the reasons you lose existing customers. Lost deals point to product and positioning gaps. Churn often points to delivery and relationship gaps.
4. **Check the data you already have:**
   - Support ticket volume and themes
   - Implementation timelines versus plan
   - Which version each customer runs (large numbers stuck on old releases is a warning sign)
   - Usage patterns before a customer left
5. **Ask frontline staff.** Account managers, support engineers, and implementation consultants usually know exactly why customers leave. Often nobody has asked them.
6. **Compare the story inside the company with what customers say.** A gap between the two is itself an important finding, and a useful one to bring to the CEO.

## Why this helps you politically

Coming back to the CEO with "here's why customers are actually leaving, in their own words" is far more persuasive than arguing about lab structure. The customers' answers will tell you which parts of your plan belong inside the existing organization, like fixing delivery and support, and which need a protected fast lane, like building modern integrations or new user experiences. That points toward the "clubhouse with a door" approach we discussed, and the evidence will justify it for you.

## Turn 3

**User:** If the customers who left are mad at you, why would they even want to talk to you?

**Assistant:**

Many of them will talk, and often more willingly than you'd expect. Here's why, and how to improve your odds.

## Why angry former customers often agree to talk

- **They want to be heard.** People who had a bad experience often wished someone had listened while they were still customers. Being asked afterward is validating.
- **Relationships in insurance software last a long time.** It's a small industry. The IT director who left you might be at another carrier next year, or might come back if the new vendor disappoints. Many people keep doors open.
- **Switching is often harder than they expected.** Some are months into a painful migration with the new vendor and may have mixed feelings.
- **You're not selling anything.** A conversation framed as "help us understand what went wrong" feels very different from a win-back pitch.
- **Some aren't actually angry.** They may have left for budget, merger, or leadership reasons and have no hard feelings.

You won't get everyone. A reasonable expectation is that somewhere between a quarter and half of the people you ask will say yes. That's enough, because 10–15 good conversations usually reveal the main patterns.

## How to make them more willing

1. **Have the right person ask.** A request from a senior leader (you, or even the CEO) signals that you take it seriously. A request from their old sales rep feels like a sales call.
2. **Be explicit that it's not a sales pitch.** For example: "We're not trying to win you back. We want to understand what we got wrong so we can fix it." Then keep that promise.
3. **Keep it short and easy.** Ask for 20–30 minutes by phone or video, scheduled around their calendar.
4. **Say something honest up front.** "It sounds like we let you down" goes a long way. Getting defensive or explaining your side will end the conversation fast.
5. **Consider a neutral third party.** Win/loss research firms or independent consultants often get more candid answers, because people are less guarded with someone who didn't sell them the software.
6. **Offer something small, if appropriate.** A thank-you note, a summary of what you learned, or a donation to a charity of their choice. Check company and customer policies first, because some insurers restrict gifts.
7. **Follow up afterward.** Tell them what you changed because of feedback like theirs. It builds goodwill, and occasionally reopens doors.

## Pick the right people to ask

- **Users as well as executives.** The claims manager or underwriter who used the system daily often gives the most specific, useful answers.
- **People who were champions for you.** Someone who originally fought to buy your software and then felt let down usually has the most detailed story.
- **Recent departures.** Memories are fresher and details are sharper within the last 6–18 months.

## If most of them won't talk

That tells you something too. A widespread refusal often signals a broken relationship or lost trust, not just product problems. In that case, lean on the other sources:

- **At-risk current customers** who are still invested enough to complain
- **Your own frontline staff** in account management, support, and implementation
- **Lost sales deals**, since prospects who chose a competitor are usually less emotional than former customers
- **Industry analysts and consultants** who advise insurers on vendor selection and hear candid opinions about every vendor
- **Former employees** who worked on those accounts

Putting several of these sources together usually gives you a clear picture, even if some former customers never pick up the phone.

## Turn 4

**User:** Would the grown-ups inside the company get upset if you find out the customers left because of stuff they did wrong?

**Assistant:**

Yes, some of them probably will, especially if you're not careful about how you gather and share what you find. That's normal, and it's manageable.

## Who is most likely to feel defensive

- **Leaders who own the broken areas.** If customers say "implementations were a nightmare," the head of professional services will feel it's about them.
- **People who approved past decisions.** Whoever chose the architecture, set the pricing, or delayed the cloud move may feel blamed.
- **Long-tenured staff.** People who built the product over many years can take criticism of it personally.
- **Possibly the CEO.** He believes the existing systems and talent can turn things around. Findings that say "the systems are the problem" can feel like a challenge to his judgment.

## Why they'd get upset

- **Fear.** Layoffs, reorganizations, and demotions often follow bad news, so people protect themselves.
- **Feeling judged by the new person.** You just arrived. Findings from you can sound like "the outsider says we failed."
- **Unfairness.** Many problems come from underfunding or earlier leadership decisions, not from the people currently in the roles. Being blamed for constraints they didn't choose feels unjust.
- **Pride.** Many of them worked hard and care about the customers.

## How to reduce the blow-up

**1. Focus on systems, not individuals.**
Say "our implementation process runs late 70% of the time," not "the services team keeps failing." Most customer problems really are system problems: incentives, handoffs, staffing, and tools.

**2. Involve them before you have findings.**
Ask department leaders to help design the questions and suggest which customers to call. Invite some to sit in on interviews. It's hard to dismiss findings you helped gather, and hearing a customer's frustration directly changes people more than any report.

**3. Preview findings privately.**
Before anything goes to the CEO or a big meeting, walk each affected leader through what customers said about their area. No one should be surprised in public.

**4. Use the customers' own words.**
Quotes and recordings, used with permission, make it clear that the findings come from customers rather than from your opinion.

**5. Balance the bad with the good.**
Customers almost always mention things they liked, such as a great support engineer or a strong feature. Include those, and name individuals who got praised.

**6. Pair each problem with an owner and resources.**
"Customers struggled with upgrades, and here's how we'll help the team fix it" lands very differently from "customers struggled with upgrades." Let the people closest to each problem lead its fix.

**7. Get the CEO's cover up front.**
Before you start, agree with him that the goal is learning, not assigning blame. If he says so publicly, people will feel safer being honest, and so will you.

**8. Be careful with timing.**
Don't lead with a big "here's everything that's broken" presentation in your first few months. Share insights gradually, alongside early wins.

## Be honest with yourself

Some discomfort is unavoidable, and a little is healthy. If nobody feels any sting, the findings probably weren't honest. And sometimes the research will reveal a genuine performance or leadership problem, not just a system one. When that happens, handle it privately with the CEO and HR, never in a findings presentation.

## Why handling this well matters for you

How you deliver bad news in your first months will shape whether the organization sees you as someone who helps them win or someone who makes them look bad. If leaders come away thinking "that research helped me get the resources I've needed for years," they become your allies. That's the lunch table you need for any innovation effort to succeed.

## The core idea

**Find out why customers are leaving, fix the basics inside the company, and build a small protected team to create what's new. Grow its independence as it earns results.**

This respects the CEO's belief in the existing organization, gives you the room to innovate that you need, and bases everything on what customers actually say.

## Phase 1: Listen and build allies (first 90 days)

**Step 1: Align with the CEO before anything else.**
Meet with him and propose a listening effort, not a lab. For example: "Before I recommend anything, I want to hear directly from customers why we're losing them. Can I count on your support, and will you tell the organization this is about learning, not blame?" This gets you his backing without an early fight over structure.

**Step 2: Run the customer research.**
- Interview 10–15 former customers, 10 at-risk current customers, and several lost prospects.
- Talk to frontline staff in support, account management, implementation, and sales.
- Pull the data: support tickets, implementation timelines, version adoption, and churn history.
- Invite department leaders to help shape the questions and sit in on some calls.

**Step 3: Find your first allies.**
Identify 3–5 respected insiders who already want change. They'll help with the research and become your core team later.

**Step 4: Sort the findings into two buckets.**
- **Things the existing organization should fix:** implementation, support, upgrades, and trust
- **Things that need a new, faster approach:** modern integrations, new user experiences, and faster product configuration

Preview the findings privately with each affected leader, then present them to the CEO with customer quotes, clear owners, and proposed fixes.

## Phase 2: Fix the basics and start the fast lane (months 4–9)

**Inside the existing organization:**
- Help the owners of broken areas get resources to fix them. Let them lead, and give them credit.
- Treat this as the CEO's "turn the ship around" plan, because it is.

**Launch a small protected team, framed as a pilot rather than a lab:**
- 6–12 people, mixing a few outside hires with respected insiders on rotation
- Its own working rules: faster decisions, fewer approvals, and a separate budget
- 2–3 projects aimed directly at reasons customers leave or deals are lost
- A named leader in the core business committed to adopting each project if it works
- A 90-day scoreboard for each project, measured by customer outcomes, not demos

**Share wins publicly and generously.**
Credit the core teams and the CEO. Bring customers in to show what's changing.

## Phase 3: Earn independence (months 9–18)

If the pilot delivers, such as saved accounts, won deals, or faster launches, you'll have the credibility to:
- Make the team more permanent and more independent
- Add more projects and grow the team
- Rotate more insiders through it so new ways of working spread

If it doesn't deliver, you'll have learned cheaply and still fixed the basics, which may be what matters most for churn.

## What to avoid

- **Don't announce an independent innovation lab in your first few months.** It sets up a fight with the CEO and alienates the organization before you've earned trust.
- **Don't build shiny things while basics stay broken.** If customers are leaving over implementation and support, fix those first.
- **Don't let the pilot build things nobody will adopt.** Every project needs a home in the core business from day one.
- **Don't deliver findings as blame.** Frame them around systems, not individuals, and never surprise anyone in public.
- **Don't measure success by attention.** Measure it by retained customers, won deals, and revenue.

## Your first week

1. Schedule a one-on-one with the CEO to propose the listening effort.
2. Ask sales and account management for a list of customers who left in the last 18 months, plus those at risk.
3. Have coffee with 5–10 frontline people and ask, "Why do you think customers leave?"
4. Start a running list of potential allies.

## The bottom line

Your instinct that innovation needs protection is right. But the fastest way to get a truly independent lab is not to ask for one now. Earn it by showing that you understand the customers, respect the people already there, and can deliver results. Start with listening, fix what's broken, prove the fast lane works, and let the independence follow.
