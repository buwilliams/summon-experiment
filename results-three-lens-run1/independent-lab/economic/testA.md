## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Neither strategy is right on its own. You and the CEO are each partly correct, and the bigger risk is that you're solving different problems.

## First, diagnose what's actually broken

Churn and low win rates are usually symptoms of **core business problems**: product gaps, poor implementation experience, slow release cycles, pricing, service quality, or a competitor that simply does the basics better. Before deciding on structure, find out:

- **Why are customers leaving?** Talk to churned customers directly, not just internal explanations.
- **Why are you losing deals?** Review recent losses with sales. Who won, and why?
- **Is this a sustaining problem or a disruptive one?** Are competitors doing the same thing better, or doing something fundamentally different, like AI-native underwriting, embedded insurance, or a radically cheaper deployment model?

If the answer is mostly "we're slow, clunky, and hard to work with," a separate lab won't fix it. The core business has to improve, and the CEO's instinct is right.

If the answer includes "the market is shifting toward something our current architecture, business model, or culture can't produce," you need some separation, because the core organization will almost certainly smother it.

## What the evidence says about each approach

**Independent innovation labs** have a mixed track record. Common failure modes:
- "Innovation theater": impressive demos that never ship
- The core organization rejects what the lab produces (not invented here, can't integrate, threatens existing budgets)
- Resentment toward the "cool kids" with special rules
- Labs get cut in the first budget squeeze because they have no revenue and no internal allies

**Innovating within the core** fails differently:
- New ideas get judged by mature-business metrics and look bad
- Resources flow to the loudest current customers, not future ones
- Risk-averse processes (compliance, architecture review, release cycles) kill experiments slowly
- Your best people get pulled back to firefighting

The research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly and Tushman) points to a hybrid: **explore separately, integrate at the top.** Exploratory teams get their own processes, metrics, and talent, but they're tightly linked to senior leadership and have a deliberate path to scale through the core business.

## A practical approach for your situation

1. **Don't open by telling the CEO he's wrong.** You're new, and he hired you with a clear thesis. Leading with "we need to escape the organization" will likely cost you credibility and allies.

2. **Spend 60–90 days learning.** Talk to customers, lost prospects, sales, support, and engineering. Find out where innovation is already happening quietly, and who the internal talent is.

3. **Get early wins inside the core.** Pick one or two visible problems tied to churn or win rate and fix them fast using existing people. This proves you can deliver, builds trust, and teaches you where the organizational friction really lives.

4. **Then propose a portfolio**, framed as extending the CEO's strategy rather than replacing it:
   - **Core improvement (most effort):** within existing systems, aimed at retention and competitiveness
   - **Adjacent bets:** small cross-functional teams with some process exemptions
   - **Transformational bets (small and protected):** a separate team with its own rules, but with explicit criteria for what success looks like and how it transfers back

5. **If you do build a separate unit, design the reintegration from day one.** Have core business leaders co-own it, staff it partly with respected insiders, and agree in advance on how a successful experiment gets resourced to scale.

## The bottom line

The CEO is likely right that turning around churn requires fixing the core with the people you have. You're likely right that genuinely new things rarely survive inside a struggling incumbent's processes. The winning move is to earn trust by improving the core first, then use that credibility to carve out protected space for the bets the core can't make.

## Turn 2

**User:** The CEO expects outsized results quickly, so what if he won't give me 60–90 days just to learn before I show anything?

**Assistant:**

Then don't make learning a separate phase. Learn *through* delivering. The 60–90 days was never meant as quiet observation. It's how long it takes to understand the business well enough to make big bets safely. You can show results within weeks while building that understanding.

## Reframe what "learning" looks like to the CEO

Instead of "I'm assessing," the message becomes "I'm acting." The same activities, positioned differently:

- **Churn interviews become a customer rescue effort.** Personally call at-risk and recently lost accounts. You'll learn the real problems, and you may save a few renewals, which is a result the CEO can see.
- **Lost-deal reviews become a win-rate sprint.** Join active deals, identify the top two or three objections costing you business, and fix what can be fixed fast (demo quality, proposal speed, pricing packaging, a missing integration story).
- **Internal conversations become talent scouting.** Find the frustrated high performers who already know what's broken and have ideas. They'll become your first team.

## Aim for visible wins in the first 2–4 weeks

Look for problems that are painful, visible, and fixable without major architecture changes. In insurance software, common candidates include:

- Slow or painful implementation and onboarding
- A long-standing bug or workflow irritant customers complain about constantly
- Weak sales demos or proposals compared with newer competitors
- Slow response times on support escalations for key accounts
- An obvious, low-risk AI application, like document summarization or claims triage assistance, that can be piloted with one friendly customer

Pick one or two, assign a small hand-picked team, and ship something in weeks. Speed and visibility matter more than size at this stage.

## Negotiate what "outsized" and "quickly" mean

This is the most important conversation to have early. Ask the CEO directly:

- What would a great first 30 days look like to you? First 6 months?
- Which metrics matter most: retention, pipeline, win rate, new product revenue?
- What would make you feel this hire was a mistake?

Then commit to a **cadence of visible progress**, such as a short weekly update and a 30-day checkpoint with concrete outcomes. Frequent small proof points buy you the credibility and time to pursue the larger bets. Executives who expect quick results are usually reassured by momentum, not by a finished strategy.

## Avoid the trap of the flashy early bet

The biggest risk under time pressure is launching something splashy, like a big lab announcement or an ambitious new product, before you understand the business. If it stumbles, you lose credibility you can't easily regain. Quick wins should be **low-risk and high-visibility**. Save the bold bets for when you know which ones are worth making.

## A compressed timeline

- **Week 1–2:** Meet the CEO on success metrics. Start customer and lost-deal calls. Identify one or two quick-win targets and a few strong internal allies.
- **Week 3–6:** Ship the first quick wins. Share weekly progress. Start forming your view of the core versus the transformational opportunities.
- **Week 6–10:** Present a portfolio recommendation backed by real customer evidence and early results. Now a proposal for protected exploratory work comes from someone who has already delivered.

This way the CEO sees results almost immediately, and you still get the understanding you need to avoid betting big on the wrong thing.

## Turn 3

**User:** How do I get people to work on these quick wins when they don't report to me and their managers already have them fully booked?

**Assistant:**

This is the central challenge of your role, and it's worth solving deliberately because the same pattern will repeat for every initiative you run. You have three main levers: **executive backing, mutual benefit, and making the ask small.**

## 1. Get explicit CEO sponsorship, and use it carefully

The CEO wants fast results, so he should help clear the path. Ask him for specific support, not general endorsement:

- A clear statement to his leadership team that these initiatives are priorities and that you'll need some of their people's time
- Agreement on how conflicts get resolved. If a manager says no, what happens?
- Ideally, a small dedicated budget for contractors or backfill

Then use this backing sparingly. If every request arrives with "the CEO says," you'll create resentment and managers will comply minimally. The sponsorship is your safety net, not your opening line.

## 2. Make the quick wins solve their problems too

The easiest way to get resources is to choose work the managers already want done but can't prioritize. Before picking targets, ask department heads:

- "What's the problem your team keeps getting pulled into that never gets fixed?"
- "What customer complaint frustrates you most?"
- "What would make your quarter easier?"

If a quick win reduces support tickets, helps close a deal, or removes a recurring fire drill, the manager becomes a co-owner instead of a donor. Give them and their people **public credit** for results. Managers lend people more readily to leaders who make their teams look good.

## 3. Make the ask small and specific

"Can I borrow two engineers?" gets refused. This gets considered:

- "I need one person for four hours a week for three weeks, on a problem that's costing your team X."
- A clear end date, deliverables, and a promise to return people on time

Keep your word on these commitments. Your reputation for returning people on schedule will determine whether you get them next time.

## 4. Tap discretionary energy

In every large organization, some people are frustrated by the status quo and eager to work on something meaningful. Find them through your conversations, skip-level meetings, or by asking managers who their most restless high performers are. Often they'll contribute beyond their normal work if the project is interesting and visible. Always involve their managers, though. Recruiting around them is a fast way to make enemies.

## 5. Force the trade-off into the open when needed

If people are truly 100% booked, adding your work means something else slips. Don't let that happen silently. Make the trade-off explicit:

- "To do this, your team would need to delay X by two weeks. Is that acceptable?"
- If the manager disagrees, bring the decision to whoever sets priorities, framed neutrally: "We need a call on which matters more."

This protects both you and the manager, and it shows the CEO the real cost of his priorities instead of hiding it in overtime.

## 6. Bring in outside capacity where possible

A modest budget for contractors, an agency, or a technical partner lets you deliver quick wins without fighting over internal people at all. It's often the fastest path, and it shows momentum while you build internal relationships.

## Watch for this pattern

If you consistently can't get people despite CEO support, that's valuable information. It suggests the organization's resource allocation will strangle any innovation effort, which strengthens your case for eventually having a dedicated team. Keep track of these friction points. They'll make your later proposal for protected capacity concrete rather than theoretical.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if the managers say yes in meetings but then quietly drag their feet or pull their people back?

**Assistant:**

This is very common, and it's usually not malice. Quiet resistance typically means a manager doesn't feel safe saying no, or the incentives they're measured on haven't changed. Treat it as a signal to diagnose, not just a behavior to stop.

## 1. Understand why it's happening

Foot-dragging usually comes from one of these:

- **Overload:** They agreed under social pressure but truly can't spare the people.
- **Incentives:** Their bonus, reviews, and boss still reward their original goals, not your project.
- **Disagreement:** They think the project is wrong or a waste of time but didn't want to say so.
- **Threat:** Your work implicitly criticizes their area or could shift budget and status away from them.
- **Their own boss:** Someone above them is quietly telling them to protect their team's work.

Each has a different fix, so find out which it is before reacting.

## 2. Make it safe to say no

Many managers say yes because refusing a CEO priority feels risky. Tell them directly: "I'd rather hear a real no than a yes that doesn't hold. If this doesn't work for your team, tell me and I'll find another way." A clear no is much easier to work with than a false yes, and people respect leaders who offer this.

## 3. Turn verbal agreements into concrete commitments

Vague agreement is easy to walk back. After each meeting, send a short note: who is contributing, how many hours, for how long, and what they'll deliver. Keep a simple shared tracker for your quick wins showing owners, progress, and blockers. This isn't about policing people. Visibility naturally encourages follow-through and makes slippage obvious early.

## 4. Address slippage directly and privately first

When someone gets pulled back, talk to the manager one-on-one right away, and be curious rather than accusatory:

- "I noticed Priya hasn't been able to join the last two sessions. What's going on?"
- "Has something changed on your side? Should we adjust the plan?"

Often you'll uncover a real constraint you can solve, like shifting timing, reducing scope, or finding backfill.

## 5. Fix the incentives

If managers are rewarded only for their existing goals, your project will always lose. Work with the CEO and HR to make contributions to these initiatives count: mention them in leadership meetings, include them in performance conversations, and credit managers publicly when their teams help deliver results. When supporting you becomes a visible way to look good, behavior changes.

## 6. Escalate cleanly and as a last resort

If private conversations don't work, raise it with the person who sets priorities, framed as a resourcing decision rather than a complaint:

- "We agreed on X, but the team hasn't been able to commit the time. Either we adjust the priority of their other work, or we delay this. I need a decision."

Bring facts, not blame. Escalating too quickly or too often will label you as someone who runs to the CEO, and that damages trust across the organization.

## 7. Reduce your dependence on reluctant partners

- Spread the work so no single manager can stall a project.
- Lean on your enthusiastic volunteers and allies.
- Use contractors or outside partners for critical-path work.
- Prioritize quick wins in areas where leaders are genuinely supportive. Early successes there create momentum and social proof that bring others along.

## Use it as evidence

Keep track of how often committed resources disappear and why. If the pattern persists even with CEO backing, it's hard evidence that the current structure can't sustain innovation work. That makes your eventual case for a small dedicated team concrete: not "I think we need independence," but "here's what happened each time we relied on borrowed capacity."
