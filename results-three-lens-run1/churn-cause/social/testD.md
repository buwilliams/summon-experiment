- **Get sign-off in writing,** even an email. This protects you and binds the VPs.

# 3. Separate who runs the analysis from who has a stake

- **Have a neutral party do the number-crunching:** RevOps, finance, BI, or a data analyst who doesn't report to any of the three leaders. If that isn't possible, bring in a short outside engagement.
- **Your role is sponsor and convener, not analyst.** You bring people together and move it forward. You don't touch the spreadsheets.
- **Give both VPs the same raw data and working sessions.** If they can see the cuts as they come in, they're far less likely to challenge the final result.

# 4. Balance the evidence sources

Exit interviews and CSM notes are CS-generated, and critics will discount them. Weight the evidence toward sources no one can easily shade:

- Product usage logs
- Implementation timelines from project records
- Support ticket data
- Contract and billing history
- Win-loss results from sales

Where you do use interviews, have someone outside CS conduct them. A third party or a sales or product leader works, and customers often speak more candidly to someone other than their CSM.

# 5. If the data points at CS, get there first

This feels like the risky outcome, but handled well it's the best one for your standing.

- **Disclose early and privately.** Tell your boss before the readout: "The data shows a meaningful share of churn traces to gaps in our coverage and early warning. Here's what I'm doing about it." Leaders who surface their own problems gain trust. Those who get caught lose it.
- **Bring a fix with the finding.** Examples: coverage changes, redesigned health scoring, renewal playbooks started earlier, clearer handoffs from implementation to CS. Owning the problem and the solution turns blame into credibility.
- **Be precise, not self-flagellating.** The likely result is shared causes, such as long implementations handing CS accounts that never reached value. Say exactly which part is yours, fix it, and don't absorb blame that belongs elsewhere.

# 6. Show CS isn't favored in the outcome

- Report CS-related findings with the same prominence and detail as the others.
- Avoid framing that conveniently lands on "more CS headcount" unless the data clearly supports it.
- Share the full readout with both VPs before it goes upward, so no one is surprised in front of the CEO.

The underlying principle is that the diagnostic's credibility comes from your willingness to be implicated by it. If people see you designed it so your own team could be found at fault, they'll trust the conclusions wherever they land. That trust is worth more to your standing than any single finding.

The key difference between "throwing them under the bus" and "protecting them" is whether *you* stand in front of them or behind them. If the message is "leadership might blame us, so I'm offering you up," they'll feel betrayed. # 1. Tell them first, and in person

They should hear this from you before it appears in any document, meeting, or hallway conversation. Learning secondhand that CS is "a hypothesis" is what makes it feel like betrayal. Do it in a team meeting, and give your managers a heads-up an hour or a day before so they can help carry the message.

# 2. Make yourself the one on the list

Frame it as your accountability, not theirs:

- "CS is on the list" means *my* strategy, coverage model, playbooks, and tooling are being tested.
- If the data shows gaps, those are gaps in how I've set the team up. They are not individual failures.

Say this explicitly. It's also true: most CS failures are structural, such as coverage ratios, bad health signals, and poor handoffs.

# 3. Explain why this protects them

Your team probably already senses that CS gets blamed for churn by default. Name that directly:

- Without data, the default story is "CS dropped the ball." With data, CS is judged on evidence like everyone else.

Be specific about what you will and won't do:

- **Systems, not individuals.** The diagnostic won't be used for individual performance judgments. If patterns show up at the CSM level, you'll treat them as coverage or support problems first.
- **No surprises.** They'll see findings about CS before anyone outside the team does.
- **You'll own it upward.** Any CS findings go to leadership as "here's what I'm fixing," not "here's who failed."
- **Fixes will be resourced.** If the data shows they're stretched too thin, you'll use it to argue for help.

Only promise what you can deliver. If you can't guarantee that no individual data will ever surface, don't claim it. Say what you'll do with it instead.

People feel less like suspects when they're helping investigate:

Assume it *will* get back to them, probably in distorted form ("CS is collecting dirt on us"). The fix isn't secrecy. Make the evidence-gathering so open and symmetrical that there's nothing hidden to discover.

# 1. Tell the VPs before, or right after, you tell your team

Don't let them hear about it secondhand. A short note or conversation works:

> "Heads-up: I'm briefing my team on the diagnostic this week. I'm telling them CS is on the list, and I'm asking them to contribute account histories and observations. That includes anything touching implementation or product, just as I'd expect your teams to flag anything touching CS. Everything goes to [neutral analyst], and you'll see all of it."

Once they've heard it from you, a secondhand version sounds like old news rather than a revelation.

# 2. Make the invitation symmetrical

The problem isn't CSMs sharing evidence about other teams. It's CS being the *only* team doing it. So:

- **Invite Product and Services teams to submit evidence too,** including evidence about CS. Their implementation leads and product managers have views on handoffs, account management, and escalations.
- **Use the same intake for everyone:** one template covering the account, what happened, when, and what data supports it. It should have neutral prompts like "Where did this account get stuck?", not "Who's to blame?"
- **Send every submission to the neutral analyst,** not to you or any VP.

When every team feeds the same process, no team is "building a case." They're all contributing to one shared investigation.

# 3. Don't let CS hold a private file

This is the line that matters most:

- No CS-only spreadsheet or folder of grievances.
- No holding evidence back to use later in a meeting.
- Everything your team submits goes into the shared pool at the same time as everyone else's, visible to both VPs.

If CSMs have strong opinions about another team, the right place for them is the shared intake, where they'll be tested against data like every other claim.

# 4. Treat anecdotes as leads, not conclusions

Tell your team, and say it publicly too, that individual stories are hypotheses for the analyst to check against usage, timelines, and ticket data. They aren't findings. This reassures the VPs that a CSM's frustration with one implementation won't become the headline. It also holds CS's own anecdotes to the same standard.

# 5. Adjust how you phrase it to your team

Instead of "share evidence that implicates other teams," say something like:

> "Share what you saw on accounts that churned: where they got stuck, what they told you, what you flagged. Wherever it points, CS, implementation, or product, it goes into the same process everyone else is feeding."

It's the same request, but framed around accounts rather than teams, and it will sound very different if repeated.

# 6. If a distorted version reaches them anyway

Address it directly and quickly:

- Name it: "I heard there's a concern that CS is building a case. Here's exactly what I asked my team to do."
- Show the mechanics: the shared template, the neutral analyst, their own teams' invitation to contribute.
- Offer them visibility: "You're welcome to see every CS submission, and I'd like your teams' input on CS too."

Don't get defensive. The openness of the process is your defense.

# 7. Model it yourself

Make one of your early submissions about CS: a churned account where coverage lapsed or a health score missed the warning. When the VPs see CS contributing evidence against itself, it becomes hard to believe you're quietly targeting them.

The underlying principle is that transparency and symmetry replace trust you haven't yet built. You don't need the VPs to believe your intentions. You need a process where your intentions don't matter because everything is visible and everyone plays by the same rules.
