**Assistant:**

# Your Lemonade Stand Problem

**Short answer:** Neither idea wins on its own. The CEO's plan is cheap but gets you few cookies. Your lab could get lots of cookies, but it's expensive and slow, and it might break the piggy bank before the first batch comes out of the oven. The best path is a third idea (below) that starts small, earns trust fast, and gets more money only when it proves it can make cookies.

*The numbers below are made up to show the shape of each idea, not real forecasts. Let's say **1 cookie = $1M in revenue you keep or win each year.***

---

## Idea 1: Fix the Lemonade Stand You Already Have (CEO's plan)

**Allowance spent first:** Small. Maybe 1–3 coins. You mostly use people and tools you already have.

**Cookies you'd get:** A few, and pretty soon. Fixing why customers leave (slow updates, bad onboarding, missing features they keep asking for) can save real money. Keeping a customer is usually much cheaper than finding a new one.

**How long until treats come:** 3–9 months for the first ones.

**Could a big dog eat them?** Yes, two dogs:
- **The house dog:** The company's own habits. Big companies naturally squash new ideas with approval steps, "we tried that before," and roadmaps that are full for 18 months. Your new ideas may get chewed up before they reach a customer.
- **The neighbor's dog:** Newer competitors who build faster. Fixing the old stand keeps you in the game, but it rarely helps you win new customers against someone with a shinier stand.

**Where you'd keep spending and not get more candy:** Hackathons, idea contests, and "innovation weeks" where nothing ever ships. This is called *innovation theater*. It feels busy but makes zero cookies.

---

## Idea 2: Build a Brand-New Stand Down the Street (your lab)

**Allowance spent first:** Big. A separate team, new tools, maybe new hires at startup-style pay. Think 5–15+ coins over 18–24 months before you know if it works.

**Cookies you'd get:** Could be the most cookies of any idea, *if* it works. Most lab bets won't. A few might be big.

**How long until treats come:** 18–36 months for real money. That's the problem: **customers are leaving now.** The lab can't stop the leak in time.

**Could a big dog eat them?** Yes, and these dogs are bigger:
- **The "your food smells weird" dog:** Labs often build great things the main company refuses to sell, support, or connect to its systems. The cookies get baked and then nobody delivers them. This is the most common way labs die.
- **The impatient-parent dog:** If the CEO or CFO doesn't see cookies within a year, they take the piggy bank back. Budget cuts kill most labs, not bad ideas.
- **The "new kid vs. boss" dog:** Your CEO already said he wants to work inside the current system. Starting your job by building the opposite of what he asked for puts *you* at risk, not just the lab.

**Where you'd keep spending and not get more candy:** Building polished prototypes before proving customers will pay. Hiring a big team before you know what problem you're solving. Fancy office, brand, and "lab culture" spending.

## Idea 3: The Test Stand with a Grown-Up Deal

Take the best parts of both. Build a **small, protected team** that is separate in *how it works* but tightly connected to *who sells and delivers*.

**How it works:**

1. **Start by stopping the leak (months 0–6).** Pick your 10–20 most at-risk or recently lost customers. Talk to them. Have a small team of 5–8 people (some from inside, some new) ship fixes that keep them from leaving. This makes cookies fast and earns you trust with the CEO.

2. **Give the team its own rules, not its own island.** They get fast approvals, their own budget line, and permission to skip the normal roadmap. But they share the company's customers, sales team, and core systems. This way, what they build can actually be delivered.

3. **Borrow, don't steal, people.** Rotate engineers and product people from the main company through the team for 3–6 months. They bring knowledge in and carry new habits back. This tames the house dog because the core team feels like part of the win.

4. **Pay out allowance in steps, like a venture capitalist.** Each bet gets a small amount to prove one thing. If it proves it, it gets more. If not, it stops. No big up-front check.

5. **Once you've earned trust (months 9–18), add bigger bets.** Now you can fund one or two more independent "new stand" ideas, like a new product for a customer segment you're losing. You'll have proof that the approach works, and the CEO will be on your side.

**Why this gets more treats:** It makes cookies early (retention), keeps the door open for big cookies later (new products), and never risks the whole piggy bank on one bet.

## Who Holds the Piggy Bank?

| Job | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Holds the piggy bank** | CEO + CFO as sponsors | Set a yearly innovation budget that is protected from normal budget cuts for at least 18 months |
| **Counts the coins** | You, reporting monthly | Track a few numbers that matter (see below), not activity like "ideas submitted" |
| **Says when to stop buying** | A small group: you, CFO or delegate, head of sales, head of product | Meets every quarter. Every bet has stop rules agreed **before** money is spent |

**Coins worth counting:**
- Customers saved and revenue kept (net revenue retention)
- Win rate on new deals where your work was part of the pitch
- Time from idea to something in a customer's hands
- Money spent per bet vs. what it returned

**Good stop rules:** "If no customer agrees to pilot this within 90 days, we stop." "If this doesn't reduce churn in the test group within 2 quarters, we stop."

---

## What to Say to Your CEO

Don't frame it as "you're wrong, we need a separate lab." Frame it as:

> "I agree we should use our people and systems. I want to set up a small team with special rules to move faster, starting with our at-risk customers. We'll fund it in stages and stop anything that doesn't work. If it proves itself, we expand."

He gets what he wanted, you get the independence you need, and the piggy bank stays in one piece.

## Turn 2

**User:** What if the grown-ups on the regular team get mad that the test stand gets special rules and they don't?

**Assistant:**

# When the Regular Team Gets Mad

This will happen, and it's one of the most common ways test stands fail. The regular team usually doesn't sabotage it on purpose. They just stop helping: requests sit in a queue, handoffs get "not my job," and the test stand slowly starves. So plan for it from day one.

---

## Why They Get Mad

It's usually not really about the rules. It's about what the rules *seem* to say:

| What they see | What they hear |
|---|---|
| Test stand skips approvals | "You're the slow ones" |
| Test stand gets new tools and budget | "Your work matters less" |
| Test stand ships something flashy | "They get the credit" |
| Test stand hands over what it built | "And you get to clean up after them" |
| Good people move to the test stand | "We're losing our best players" |

If you fix what they *hear*, most of the anger goes away.

---

## How to Keep Them From Getting Mad

### 1. Tie the special rules to the *job*, not the *people*
A fire truck can run red lights because of what it's doing, not because firefighters are better than everyone else. Say it plainly: "These rules exist because this team runs small experiments that might fail. Most of what the regular team does, like keeping customers' systems safe and working, *should* have more checks."

Keep the special rules few and specific. "Can release to 3 pilot customers without the full review board" is fine. "Doesn't have to follow process" is a recipe for resentment.

### 2. Make the test stand a test kitchen for rules, too
This is the most important move. Any special rule that works gets offered to the regular team. If faster approvals work for the test stand without breaking anything, roll them out company-wide.

Now the test stand isn't a club that gets perks. It's the place that figures out how to make *everyone's* job easier. This also delivers exactly what your CEO asked for: improving the existing system and people.

### 3. Fix the regular team's problems first
Ask the regular team: "What slows you down the most?" Make one or two of those among the test stand's first projects, like a painful release process, a customer complaint they're tired of fielding, or a tool they hate. When the test stand's first win makes the regular team's life better, they become fans.

### 4. Give away the credit, loudly
When something works, announce it as a joint win and name the regular-team people who helped. If a core engineer answered one question that unblocked the test stand, thank them in front of their boss. Credit is cheap for you and valuable to them.

### 5. Don't leave dishes in their sink
The test stand owns what it builds until handoff. The regular team gets a real say in what "ready to hand over" means, including documentation, support, and security standards. Nothing gets thrown over the wall.

### 6. Open the door
- Anyone can apply for a rotation.
- Rotations have an end date, and people return with a clear path to promotion, not a step backward.
- Agree with regular-team managers on who rotates and when, so they don't feel raided.

### 7. Bring the grumpiest grown-ups inside
Find the two or three respected regular-team leaders most likely to be skeptical. Give them a seat on the group that decides what gets funded and what gets stopped. It's hard to resent something you help steer, and their skepticism will make your bets better.

### 8. Keep it boring on the outside
No fancy separate office, no special swag, no secret meetings. Share what the test stand is working on, what it costs, and what failed. Being open about failures matters most. People resent a team that seems to only ever win.

---

## Warning Signs It's Going Wrong

- Requests from the test stand sit much longer than other requests
- Regular-team managers "can't spare anyone" for rotations
- Jokes or nicknames about the test stand (e.g., "the playground")
- Handoffs keep bouncing back
- Lots of your best regular-team people ask to transfer. That sounds good, but it means the regular team feels drained and will push back harder.

If you see these, talk to the regular-team leaders one-on-one before it spreads. Ask what's bothering them, and assume at least part of their complaint is fair.

---

## When Someone Is Still Mad

Sometimes they'll be right. If they say "why do *they* get to skip that review and we don't?", ask yourself honestly whether the review is useful for anyone. If not, you just found your next rule to fix company-wide. If it is useful, explain why the test stand's situation is different and what guardrails replace it.

**One honest note:** Some grumbling is normal and even healthy. If nobody notices the test stand is doing anything different, it probably isn't. The goal isn't zero complaints. The goal is that the regular team feels the test stand is *for* them, not *instead of* them.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if the test stand team starts liking their special rules so much that they don't want to give them back when their turn is over?

**Assistant:**

# When the Test Stand Doesn't Want to Give the Rules Back

This is a good problem in one way: it means the special rules feel better than the old ones. But if you don't plan for it, you get one of two bad outcomes. Either the test stand turns into a permanent club with perks (and the regular team's anger from last time comes roaring back), or people quit instead of going back to the old way.

---

## Two Different Problems

| Problem | What it looks like | Real risk |
|---|---|---|
| **People don't want to go back** | Rotators ask to extend, stall their return, or start job hunting | You lose good people, and the rotation idea falls apart |
| **The team won't give up perks** | Rules that were meant for experiments slowly apply to everything the team does, forever | The test stand becomes a second bureaucracy with better snacks |

They need different fixes.

---

## Fixing "I Don't Want to Go Back"

### 1. Say the end date on day one, and put it in writing
Before anyone joins, they agree on how long the rotation lasts, what they'll return to, and what they'll carry back. It's much easier to keep a promise both sides made up front than to take something away later.

### 2. Make coming back a promotion, not a demotion
Returning rotators should go back with a new job attached: "You're now the person who brings faster releases to your team." Give them visible ownership of spreading one practice they learned. Their manager and the CEO should recognize it. If coming back feels like being sent to your room, people will fight it. If it feels like graduating, they'll want it.

### 3. Treat the complaint as a clue
If someone says "I can't go back to waiting three weeks for approvals," they've just told you which regular-team rule to fix next. This is the test kitchen idea from last time. The more rules you spread, the less there is to "give back," because the regular team gets them too.

### 4. Let a few people stay
Not everyone should rotate. Keep a small permanent core, maybe two or three people, who hold the know-how and culture. Everyone else rotates. Be clear from the start about which role each person has, and pick the permanent people for skills, not for who complains loudest.

### 5. Watch for flight risk
If a strong person would rather quit than return, talk before it gets that far. Options include a longer stay, a different team, or leading the spread of new practices. Losing them is worse than bending the rotation plan once.

---

## Fixing "We're Keeping Our Perks"

### 1. Attach rules to the bet, not the team
A rule like "release to pilot customers without the full review" belongs to a specific experiment. When that experiment ends, or grows into a real product with real customers, the rule ends too. Graduated products follow the regular grown-up rules, because now real customers depend on them.

### 2. Give every rule an expiration date
Each quarter, every special rule has to earn its place again. Your decision group asks three questions:
- Is this rule still helping us learn faster?
- Has it caused any problems like security issues, customer complaints, or messes for the regular team?
- Should it end, continue, or spread to everyone?

No answer means the rule expires. This stops rules from quietly turning into permanent privileges.

### 3. Watch for rules becoming badges
Warning signs:
- The team uses the special rules for routine work that isn't an experiment
- People describe the rules as something they "earned" or "deserve"
- The test stand starts adding its *own* approval steps and meetings
- Budget grows while the number of experiments shrinks

That last one is the sneakiest. Test stands can grow their own slow habits over time. If yours starts looking like a small copy of the big company, shrink it back down.

### 4. Make you the rule-breaker who also enforces the rules
The team needs to see that you protect the special rules fiercely *and* take them away when they stop making sense. If you only ever defend them, the team will assume they're permanent.

---

## The Big Idea

The healthiest outcome isn't the test stand keeping its rules or giving them back. It's the special rules **running out because everyone has the good ones.**

Picture it like this:
- **Year 1:** The test stand has 10 special rules. The regular team has none.
- **Year 2:** 4 of those rules have spread to everyone. 3 were dropped because they didn't work. The test stand is trying 3 new ones.
- **Year 3:** The regular team moves noticeably faster, and nobody's jealous, because the gap is small and keeps shrinking.

That's also the story you tell your CEO: the test stand didn't replace the existing system. It upgraded it, one rule at a time.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if the test stand's rule gets shared with everybody, and then something breaks because the regular team isn't used to going that fast?

**Assistant:**

# When a Shared Rule Breaks Something

This will probably happen at least once, so plan for it now. The goal isn't "never break anything." The goal is **small breaks you can fix quickly, and never a break that hurts customers badly.** In insurance software, where your customers are paying claims, pricing policies, and answering to regulators, that second part matters a lot.

---

## Why a Rule That Worked Can Break

A rule that works on the test stand often works because of **everything around it**, not the rule itself.

| On the test stand | On the regular team |
|---|---|
| Changes go to 3 pilot customers who agreed to try new things | Changes go to every customer, including ones running live claims |
| Small, new code with few connections | Big, old systems where one change can ripple everywhere |
| People chosen for the job, trained in the new way | People used to the old way, with old safety habits |
| Someone watching closely and ready to undo fast | Nobody specifically watching |

So when you share a rule, you're not just handing over a rule. You also need to hand over **the safety net that made it work.**

---

## Before Sharing a Rule

### 1. Share the rule with its safety gear attached
Every shared rule comes with the guardrails that made it safe. Examples:
- "Faster releases" comes with automated testing, small change sizes, and a way to switch new features off instantly.
- "Fewer approvals" comes with a clear list of what still needs approval.

If the regular team doesn't have the safety gear yet, build that first.

### 2. Try it with one team first
Don't roll it out to everyone at once. Pick one regular team that's willing and has lower-risk work. Run it for a month or two. Then expand to a few more teams, then everyone. Each step is a chance to find problems while they're small.

### 3. Have an undo button ready
Before anyone uses a new rule, answer this question: **"If this goes wrong, how fast can we go back?"** If the answer is "we can't" or "it would take weeks," the rule isn't ready to share.

### 4. Draw a "never go fast here" line
Some areas should stay slow and careful no matter what, like customer data and privacy, regulatory reports, claims payments, billing, and security. Write this list down and agree on it with engineering, compliance, and your CEO *before* anything is shared. Speed rules don't apply inside that line, or apply only with extra checks.

### 5. Train people, don't just announce
Pair regular-team members with test stand people for the first few uses. Former rotators are perfect for this. They know both worlds.

---

## When Something Breaks

### Step 1: Fix it first, talk later
Use the undo button. Protect customers. Tell affected customers quickly and honestly. This is not the moment to argue about whether the rule was a good idea.

### Step 2: Look at what happened without blaming anyone
Hold a short review focused on *what happened*, not *who messed up*. Ask:
- Was the rule wrong, or was it missing safety gear?
- Did we skip a step in the rollout?
- Did something fall inside the "never go fast here" line that we didn't notice?

If the person who made the mistake gets punished, everyone learns to hide mistakes, and hidden mistakes are the dangerous ones.

### Step 3: Decide one of three things

| Decision | When |
|---|---|
| **Adjust** | The rule is good but needs more guardrails, training, or a slower rollout |
| **Narrow** | The rule works for some kinds of work but not others. Limit where it applies. |
| **Pull back** | The rule doesn't work outside the test stand. Return it, and say so openly. |

### Step 4: Share what you learned
Tell the rest of the company what broke, what you changed, and why. This builds trust. People are much more willing to try new things when they see problems handled openly and calmly.

---

## Don't Let One Break Stop Everything

After a break, someone will say, "See? Going fast is dangerous. Let's go back to the old way." That's the old habits fighting back.

Two things protect you:

**1. Set expectations ahead of time.** Tell your CEO and leadership up front: "Some of these changes will cause small problems. Here's how we'll keep them small, and here's what we'll never risk." If you warn them first, a break looks like the plan working. If you don't, it looks like a failure.

**2. Keep score honestly.** Track both sides: how many problems the new rules caused *and* how much faster or better things got. The old way also had problems. They were just slower and more familiar. Compare fairly.

---

## Who's Responsible

- **The team using the rule** owns fixing problems in their work.
- **You and the test stand** own the rollout plan, the safety gear, and the training. If a shared rule breaks because it was shared too fast, that's on you, not the regular team.
- **Your decision group** decides whether to adjust, narrow, or pull back, and whether any "never go fast here" areas need updating.

Owning rollout problems yourself is also the best way to keep the regular team's trust. They took a risk by trying your rule. If it breaks and they get blamed, they won't try the next one.

# What You Should Do

**Don't build a separate lab yet.** Build a small test stand with special rules, point it at your churning customers first, and earn the right to make bigger bets later. This matches what your CEO asked for, stops the leak while it's still urgent, and keeps the big-cookie option open.

Here's the plan, step by step.

---

## Days 1–30: Listen Before You Build

You're new. Your biggest advantage right now is that people will tell you things they won't tell the CEO. Use it.

**Talk to:**
- **10–15 customers who left or are about to leave.** Why? What would have kept them? Who did they switch to?
- **Sales, about lost deals.** Why did prospects pick a competitor?
- **3–5 respected regular-team leaders.** What slows them down most? What have they tried that got killed? Some of these people will become your allies. Some are the skeptics you'll want on your decision group later.

**Get these numbers:**
- How much revenue left last year, and which customers are at risk this year
- The top 3–5 reasons customers leave
- Win rate on new deals, and the top reasons you lose

By day 30 you should be able to say: "Here's where the piggy bank is leaking, and here's why."

---

## Days 30–60: Make the Deal with the CEO

Bring your CEO a **one-page plan**. Frame it in his language: working within existing systems and people, with a faster lane to prove what works.

**What to ask for:**

| Ask | Why |
|---|---|
| A small team of 5–8, mostly rotators from inside plus 1–2 outside hires | Cheap, trusted, and connected to the core |
| A protected budget for 18 months, released in stages | Survives budget cuts, but only grows when it earns results |
| 3–5 specific special rules, written down | Speed where it matters, clear limits everywhere else |
| A decision group: you, CFO or delegate, heads of sales and product, and 1–2 skeptical regular-team leaders | Someone counts the coins and says when to stop |
| A written "never go fast here" list | Protects customer data, claims, billing, and regulatory work |
| Agreement that some experiments will fail and that's expected | So the first failure doesn't end the program |

**Pick your first 3 bets:**
1. **Churn fix #1:** The biggest reason customers are leaving
2. **Churn fix #2:** Something one at-risk major customer badly needs
3. **Regular-team fix:** One thing that slows the regular team down, which wins you friends

---

## Days 60–90: Ship Something Real

- Get your first changes in front of a few pilot customers, even if they're small.
- Send your first monthly report: what shipped, what you learned, what you stopped, and what it cost.
- Thank the regular-team people who helped, by name, in front of their bosses.

Small and real beats big and planned. The point of the first 90 days is proving the test stand can deliver.

---

## Months 4–12: Grow Only When It Works

| When | What |
|---|---|
| **Month 4–6** | Measure whether churn fixes are saving customers. Stop anything that isn't working. Start the first rotation swap. |
| **Month 6** | First quarterly rule review. Pick one rule that worked and try it with **one** willing regular team, with its safety gear attached. |
| **Month 6–9** | Returning rotators lead the spread of what they learned. Expand the first shared rule if it holds up. |
| **Month 9–12** | If you've earned trust, propose one bigger bet: a new product or offering aimed at the customers you're losing or deals you keep losing. This is where the separate-lab thinking comes back, now with proof and a CEO who backs you. |

---

## Your 12-Month Scorecard

Agree on these with the CEO up front. Targets depend on your real numbers, so set them after your first 30 days.

- **Revenue saved** from at-risk customers
- **Win rate** on new deals where your work was part of the pitch
- **Speed:** time from idea to something in a customer's hands
- **Rules spread:** how many test stand practices the regular team adopted
- **Cost per bet** vs. what each bet returned
- **Problems caused**, tracked honestly and compared with the old way

---

## What Not to Do

- **Don't call it an "innovation lab."** Call it something plain, tied to the goal, like a "customer retention team." Fancy names invite resentment and make the program an easy budget cut.
- **Don't hire a big team up front.** Grow only when bets earn it.
- **Don't fight your CEO's framing.** Build inside it and stretch it.
- **Don't spread rules faster than you can build the safety gear.**
- **Don't promise big cookies in year one.** Promise saved customers and faster delivery. Deliver those, then ask for more.
