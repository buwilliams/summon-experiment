**Assistant:**

# Innovation Strategy: Following the Money

**Short answer:** Neither pure strategy is the best use of capital. The CEO is largely right about *where the money is*: stopping churn in the existing base is the biggest, fastest, most certain profit lever. You are largely right about *how innovation dies inside incumbents*: standard budgeting, approvals, and priorities will smother anything new. The better approach combines the two. Fund a small team with real decision rights to attack the revenue leaks first, then fund new bets in stages, releasing money only when evidence justifies it.

Below is the financial reasoning. The numbers are **illustrative**, built on a hypothetical $500M insurance software company. Replace them with your company's actual figures before you take any of this to the CEO or CFO.

---

## 1. First, Where Is the Money Actually Going?

**Illustrative baseline:**
- $500M revenue: $350M recurring (subscription/maintenance) and $150M services
- Gross annual churn of 10%, about **$35M of recurring revenue lost per year**. Healthy vertical enterprise software is often 5–7%.
- About $200M of new-business pipeline per year at a 20% win rate, or **$40M new annual contract value**
- Net result: the company is running hard to stand still.

**Three facts about insurance software shape every strategy:**
1. **Churn is a lagging indicator.** Contracts run 3–7 years. Today's churn reflects dissatisfaction from years ago, and the next wave of at-risk renewals is already visible in the contract calendar.
2. **Every churned customer takes services revenue with them**, including implementations, upgrades, and custom work. The true loss is larger than the recurring revenue alone.
3. **Insurers buy slowly.** Procurement, security review, regulatory sign-off, and integration with core policy, billing, and claims systems mean 9–18-month sales cycles. New products take years to reach meaningful revenue.

**The math that matters most:** each point of churn is worth about $3.5M in recurring revenue per year, and that loss compounds. Cutting churn by 3 points protects roughly $10M in year one, $21M cumulatively in year two, and $31M+ by year three, at 70–80% gross margins. Very few new products reach that scale in three years.

## 2. Strategy A: Work Within Existing Systems (the CEO's Approach)

**Revenue generated or protected**
- Mostly *protected* revenue: fixing product gaps, implementation pain, and service issues that drive churn.
- Some win-rate improvement from better demos, packaging, and roadmap credibility.
- **Realistic range: 1–2 points of churn reduction and 1–3 points of win-rate improvement.** It is modest because the same systems that produced the problem are being asked to fix it.
- At 1.5 points of churn reduction and 2 points of win rate: about **$5M protected plus $4M new recurring revenue per year**, compounding.

**Investment and operating costs**
- Mostly reallocated headcount, so incremental cost looks low: roughly $3–8M/year for tooling, consultants, and a small program office.
- **Hidden cost:** reallocated engineers stop doing other work. Opportunity cost is real but invisible on the budget.

**When returns arrive**
- Quick product and service fixes: 6–12 months.
- Visible churn reduction: 12–24 months, because renewals cycle slowly.

**Risks that could wipe out returns**
- **Dilution.** Initiatives get absorbed into quarterly roadmaps and lose to urgent customer escalations and existing commitments.
- **Structural causes go untouched.** If customers are leaving for modern cloud-native competitors because of architecture, incremental fixes only slow the bleeding.
- **No outsized results.** The CEO hired you for step-change outcomes. This path produces incremental ones, and you will be judged on the gap.

**Where spending grows without profit**
- "Transformation programs": consultants, frameworks, steering committees, and dashboards that produce activity reports rather than retention.
- Tooling purchases, such as innovation platforms and agile tools, that don't change decisions.

**Budget control, ownership, and stopping**
- Budget sits with existing business-unit (BU) and functional leaders. **You have influence but no authority.**
- Results are diffuse. Everyone and no one owns them.
- **Nobody decides when to stop.** These programs rarely get killed. They fade, which makes spending hard to track.

## 3. Strategy B: Independent Innovation Lab (Your Approach)

**Revenue generated or protected**
- Mostly *new* revenue from new products, such as AI-driven claims or underwriting tools, embedded insurance APIs, or a modern cloud module.
- **It protects almost nothing in the near term.** The $35M/year churn problem continues while the lab builds.
- Plausible outcome if a bet succeeds: $2–5M recurring revenue by year 3, and $20–50M by years 5–7 for a real winner. Most lab bets don't succeed. Assume 20–30% odds for any given product.

**Investment and operating costs**
- Team of 25–40 at about $250K fully loaded: **$6–10M/year**.
- Plus cloud, facilities, design, and compliance/security work (insurers will demand SOC 2 and similar certifications even for pilots): **$1–3M/year**.
- **Plus the cost people forget: go-to-market.** Enterprise insurance sales teams, solution engineers, and implementation staff can double the budget once products are ready to sell. Alternatively, you fight the core sales force for attention.
- **Three-year total: roughly $30–45M, before meaningful revenue.**

**When returns arrive**
- First pilot revenue: 18–30 months.
- Meaningful revenue: 3–5 years.
- Payback: years 5–7, if a winner emerges.

**Risks that could wipe out returns**
- **Sponsor risk.** Labs typically die when their executive sponsor leaves or the company has a bad quarter. With revenue shrinking, the lab is the first cut, likely before it has proof points.
- **Distribution risk.** The core sales team controls the customer relationships. If they see the lab's products as distraction or cannibalization, they won't sell them.
- **Integration risk.** Insurance products must plug into legacy policy, billing, and claims systems. A lab working apart from the core often builds things the core can't absorb.
- **Rejection by the core.** Isolation breeds resentment ("the cool kids"), and the core organization pushes back.
- **Timing mismatch.** The company may lose enough customers during the build phase that the investment becomes unaffordable.

**Where spending grows without profit**
- Headcount creep ("we need our own data team, our own DevOps...").
- Building features instead of testing demand.
- Pilots that never convert. Insurers love free pilots and rarely sign contracts that follow from them.
- Rebuilding capabilities the core already has.

**Budget control, ownership, and stopping**
- You control the budget, which is attractive but also concentrates career risk on you.
- The financial results you own are small and late. You can't show P&L impact for 2–3 years.
- If stopping criteria aren't defined up front, the decision gets made politically, usually at the worst moment.

**A candid note:** part of the appeal of Strategy B is control and freedom from the organization. That is a legitimate need, but make sure the structure serves the returns and not just the autonomy.

## 4. Strategy C: Revenue-Anchored, Stage-Gated Innovation (Recommended)

The core idea: **give innovation independence in *how* it works, but tie it tightly to *where* the money is**, and release capital only as evidence arrives.

### Phase 0 (days 0–90): Find the leaks. Cost: under $500K.
- **Churn autopsy.** Analyze lost customers and at-risk renewals over the next 24 months by revenue. Interview churned and at-risk customers.
- **Lost-deal analysis.** Why do you lose, to whom, and at what stage?
- **Output:** a ranked list of the specific product, service, and architecture gaps costing the most revenue, plus the renewal calendar showing which revenue is at risk and when.
- This gives the CEO a data-driven reason to fund you. It also tells you whether the problem is fixable incrementally (the CEO's view) or structural (yours).

### Phase 1 (months 3–12): Retention and win-rate strike team. Cost: $4–6M/year.
- **8–15 people** (product, engineering, design, customer success, a sales engineer), reporting to you.
- **Protected from standard processes.** They get their own release path, a fast-track procurement exception, and dedicated time from core architects.
- **Mission:** fix the top churn drivers for the largest at-risk accounts, and remove the top reasons deals are lost.
- **Target: 2–3 points of churn reduction and 3–5 points of win-rate improvement.** That means $7–10M protected plus $6–10M new recurring revenue per year, compounding.
- This phase funds the rest. Early wins earn you the credibility and capital for bigger bets.

### Phase 2 (months 6–36): Portfolio of new bets, funded in tranches.
- **3–5 bets, each starting at $250K–$1M.** Each bet gets more money only when it hits predefined evidence gates:
  - **Gate 1 (about $250K):** problem validated with 10+ customer interviews, and 3 insurers willing to sign a letter of intent.
  - **Gate 2 (about $1M):** paid pilot with at least one customer. **Require customer co-funding**, even if it's small. Insurers that pay are real signals. Free pilots are not.
  - **Gate 3 ($3–5M+):** repeatable sales. Two or more paying customers, a credible path to $10M+ recurring revenue, and a defined integration path into the core.
- Expect 1–2 of 5 bets to survive. Killing the others is the system working, not failing.
- **Weigh build, partner, and buy options at every gate.** Partnering with or acquiring an insurtech startup may be cheaper and faster than building. The insurtech valuation reset has made many capable teams affordable.

### Phase 3: Scale winners into the core, or spin them out.
- Winners move to a core BU with a sales quota attached, or become a separate unit if they truly conflict with the core business model.

**Total capital at risk:** about $5–7M in year 1. Years 2–3 grow to $10–15M/year **only if gates are hit**, for roughly $20–35M over three years. Most of that is contingent, not committed.

**Budget control, ownership, and stopping**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Who controls the budget?** | A separate innovation fund, ring-fenced from BU budget cuts, allocated by a small **investment committee**: CEO, CFO, you, and one core BU leader. It meets quarterly, like a venture capital firm. |
| **Who owns financial results?** | **Shared ownership.** You own delivery and evidence. A named BU leader co-owns revenue outcomes and has them in their compensation. That solves the distribution problem. |
| **Who decides when to stop?** | The committee, using **kill criteria written before money is spent**. Missed gate = default stop unless the committee explicitly overrides with a written rationale. |
| **How do you prevent spending creep?** | Headcount caps per phase, no GTM hiring until Gate 3, and a CFO-owned monthly report on cost per validated learning and cost per dollar of revenue protected. |

## 6. What to Do Next

1. **Get the real numbers from finance:** recurring vs. services mix, gross and net revenue retention by segment, the renewal calendar for the next 24 months, win/loss data, and the gross margin on each revenue line. Rebuild this model with them.
2. **Reframe the conversation with the CEO.** This isn't "internal vs. independent." It's "fix the revenue leaks fast, with a team that has the freedom to actually do it, then earn the right to bigger bets." That respects his instinct and protects yours.
3. **Propose the investment committee and kill criteria in your first pitch.** CFOs fund discipline. Pre-committing to stop failing work is the fastest way to get capital for succeeding work.
4. **Secure one early, visible retention win** within 6–9 months, ideally saving a large at-risk renewal. That becomes your political capital for everything else.

**Final thought:** The biggest risk to your mandate isn't choosing the wrong structure. It's spending 18 months building something new while the existing customer base keeps leaving, and running out of time and funding before your results arrive. Start where the money is leaking, prove you can stop it, and use that credibility to fund the future.

# How Much of the Churn Savings Would Concessions Give Back?

**Short answer:** Probably a lot more than most retention plans assume. In the illustrative model below, a typical concession package gives back **roughly half to two-thirds of the gross profit from saved accounts over the first three years**. Light, disciplined concessions keep about 70% of the value. Heavy, undisciplined ones can make the save program **lose money**.

Even so, a well-run save program usually beats the alternative of losing the customer and buying replacement revenue through new sales. But my earlier projections were gross figures, and they overstated the net benefit. Here's the corrected math.

---

## 1. Why the Give-Back Is Bigger Than It Looks

Four mechanics inflate the true cost of concessions:

1. **You pay concessions to the whole at-risk pool, not just the customers you actually save.** Some would have renewed anyway (deadweight), and some take the discount and leave later anyway. You can't perfectly tell them apart in advance.
2. **Discounts cost 100% margin; saved revenue earns about 75% margin.** A dollar of discount is a dollar of lost profit. A dollar of saved revenue is only about 75 cents of gross profit. Measured in revenue, concessions look smaller than they really are.
3. **Discounts persist, and custom work creates ongoing costs.** A renewal discount typically carries through the full contract term and often anchors the next renewal. Free custom work becomes code you maintain forever.
4. **Concessions leak.** The insurance industry is small, and pricing gets shared through procurement consultants, analysts, and peer networks. Customers learn that threatening to leave works, and discounts spread to healthy accounts.

---

## 2. Illustrative Model: One Year's Cohort of Saved Accounts

**Starting assumptions** (continuing the earlier $500M example, 3-point churn reduction):
- At-risk pool: **$25M** in recurring revenue, roughly 25–30 accounts.
- Without intervention, 60% churns ($15M). With the program, only 18% churns ($4.5M).
- **Revenue saved: $10.5M/year**, worth about **$7.9M in gross profit** at 75% margin.
- **Revenue renewing with concessions: $20.5M.** About $10M of that would likely have renewed anyway.

**Concession scenarios, per year for this cohort ($M):**

| Cost item | Light | Typical | Heavy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Renewal discount on $20.5M renewing (8% / 15% / 25%) | 1.6 | 3.1 | 5.1 |
| Free custom work + waived services, one-time cost in year 1 | 1.5 | 3.0 | 8.0 |
| Ongoing maintenance of custom work (years 2+) | 0.2 | 0.5 | 1.0 |
| Discount leakage to healthy renewals | 0.0 | 0.8 | 2.0 |

**Net gross profit retained from the cohort ($M):**

| | Light | Typical | Heavy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 4.8 | 1.0 | –7.2 |
| Year 2 | 6.1 | 3.5 | –0.2 |
| Year 3 | 6.1 | 3.5 | –0.2 |
| **3-year total (vs. $23.7M gross)** | **17.0 (~72% kept)** | **8.0 (~34% kept)** | **–7.6 (value destroyed)** |

**At the program level**, a new cohort is added each year, and each cohort carries its heavy year-1 costs. In the typical case, the program keeps roughly **$13–14M of about $47M in gross profit** over three years, less than 30%. The ratio improves after year 3 as one-time costs roll off, but persistent discounts cap it at around 45% unless prices are restored.

A note on the save team itself: it costs roughly $1–2M/year. That is already inside the Phase 1 strike team budget from my earlier plan, so don't count it twice.

## 3. Why It's Usually Still Worth It: The Real Comparison

The right comparison isn't "save with concessions" against "keep the revenue for free." It's "save with concessions" against **"lose the customer and replace the revenue."**

Replacing $10.5M of recurring revenue through new business typically means:
- **$12–18M in sales and marketing spend**, at a common $1.20–1.70 of acquisition cost per dollar of new annual recurring revenue in enterprise vertical software.
- **12–24 months of delay**, given insurance sales cycles.
- **Implementation costs**, often underwater for new logos.
- **Services revenue lost** from the departing customers, which I excluded from the model above.
- **Lost references.** Departing insurers become negative references in a small market.

So even the typical scenario, keeping about a third of the gross profit, generally beats replacement. **The heavy scenario doesn't.** At that point you're paying customers to stay, and some of them should be allowed to leave.

---

## 4. How to Shrink the Give-Back

The goal is to move from the typical scenario toward the light one. The biggest levers:

**Trade, don't give.** Every concession should buy something back:
- Discount in exchange for a **longer term** (5 years instead of 3) or **annual price escalators**
- Discount in exchange for **multi-year prepayment** or **expansion commitments** (additional lines of business, modules, or users)
- Free work in exchange for **migration to standard configuration**, which reduces your future support cost
- Concessions in exchange for **reference rights, case studies, or co-development partnerships** that feed the innovation pipeline

**Make discounts temporary.** Use ramped pricing, such as 20% off in year 1, 10% in year 2, and list price in year 3. This alone can cut the three-year discount cost by 40–50%.

**Productize, never fork.** Only build custom work that goes into the core product and benefits multiple customers. Fund it from the roadmap budget, not as a giveaway. One-off custom code for a single account is the most expensive concession because its costs never end.

**Triage the at-risk pool.** Segment by lifetime value, profitability, and save probability. Concentrate concessions where they change outcomes. Some accounts are unprofitable at current pricing, and losing them may improve margins.

**Intervene earlier.** Concessions are far cheaper 12–18 months before renewal, when you're fixing problems, than 60 days before, when you're negotiating under a competitor's deadline. The renewal calendar from Phase 0 is your early-warning system.

**Fix root causes instead of paying for symptoms.** If ten accounts are at risk because of the same product gap, fixing that gap once benefits the whole customer base. Discounting ten times leaves the gap open for the next wave. This is the strongest argument for the strike team: every root cause it fixes reduces future concession costs.

**Limit leakage contractually.** Avoid most-favored-customer clauses, use confidentiality terms around pricing, and apply discounts to specific bundles rather than list price.

---

## 5. Governance: Who Approves Concessions and What Gets Measured

- **Deal desk with an approval matrix.** Account teams approve small concessions. Larger ones go to a VP, and anything above roughly 15% discount or $250K in free work requires CFO sign-off.
- **Measure net retained gross profit**, not "logos saved" or "revenue retained." Revenue-based metrics reward exactly the behavior that destroys value.
- **Compensate account and customer success teams on net figures**, after concessions and service costs.
- **Review each cohort six months after renewal.** Did saved accounts stabilize, expand, or start drifting toward churn again? This tells you whether concessions bought loyalty or just time.

---

## 6. Data to Get Before You Trust Any of This

Replace my assumptions with your company's actuals:

1. **Historical renewal discounts**, at-risk vs. healthy accounts, over the last 2–3 years.
2. **Save rates.** How many flagged accounts renewed, and how many of those churned within the following contract term?
3. **Free work hidden in the books.** Services write-offs, non-billable hours logged against renewing accounts, and custom code maintained for single customers. This is often the largest unmeasured cost.
4. **Discount drift.** Are average renewal prices for healthy accounts declining? That signals leakage.
5. **Acquisition cost per dollar of new recurring revenue.** This sets the replacement benchmark your save program has to beat.

---

## 7. Implications for the Strategy

- **Revise the earlier projections downward.** On a net-profit basis, the retention component of Strategy C is likely worth roughly **30–70% of the gross figures** I gave, depending on concession discipline.
- **The recommendation still holds, and gets stronger in one respect.** Concession costs make root-cause fixes more valuable relative to negotiated saves. The strike team's job is to make the save program progressively cheaper by removing the reasons customers want to leave.
- **Present net numbers to the CEO and CFO from day one.** Leaders who show gross savings and then quietly absorb concession costs lose credibility fast. Leaders who show the give-back upfront, along with a plan to shrink it, earn the budget.

## 2. Why Profit Falls Faster Than Saves

It's operating leverage working in reverse. The at-risk accounts that would have renewed anyway (roughly $10M of revenue in the pool) still receive concessions, because you can't reliably identify them in advance. That concession cost is essentially **fixed**, regardless of how many accounts you actually save.

When saves drop from $10.5M to $5M, that fixed cost is spread over half as many wins. Each saved dollar now carries about $3 of discounted revenue instead of $2. Halve the saves and you can wipe out nearly all the profit.

## 3. Break-Even: How Good Does the Save Rate Need to Be?

Over three years, one cohort breaks even at these levels of churn within the at-risk pool:

| Concession level | Break-even churn in at-risk pool | Minimum revenue saved |
|---|---|---|
| **Light** | ~53% | ~$1.7M |
| **Typical** | **~40%** | ~$5.1M |
| **Heavy** | Never within 3 years | Not achievable |

**40% sits almost exactly at the typical break-even point.** That makes your scenario the line between a program that pays for itself and one that doesn't.

**The biggest lever is triage.** If you withhold concessions from even half of the accounts likely to renew anyway, typical-concession break-even improves from 40% churn to roughly 50%. In other words, identifying who doesn't need a discount is worth more than almost any negotiating tactic.

## 4. Is 40% More Realistic?

Probably, at least for the first year or two. Cutting churn from 60% to 18% means reversing about 70% of expected losses, which is exceptional for any company. For one that has been steadily losing these accounts, it would require fixing the underlying reasons customers leave, not just negotiating better.

**The achievable save rate depends on why customers are leaving:**

| Churn reason | Can concessions fix it? | Realistic within 12–24 months? |
|---|---|---|
| **Price or value perception** | Yes | High |
| **Service, implementation, or relationship failures** | Partly; mostly needs operational fixes | Moderate–high |
| **Product gaps** (missing features, lines of business, integrations) | Temporarily at best | Low–moderate; depends on roadmap speed |
| **Architecture or strategy** (board-level cloud or modernization mandate, already evaluating modern core platforms) | Rarely | Low |
| **Structural** (mergers, exiting a line of business, vendor consolidation) | No | Near zero |

If most of your at-risk revenue sits in the bottom three rows, even 40% could be optimistic in year one. The Phase 0 churn autopsy answers this question, and it should set the base case.

**A more credible trajectory:** 60% → 45–50% in year 1 (early saves, limited product fixes), → around 40% in year 2, → 30% or better in year 3, but **only if** the strike team ships fixes for the top churn causes. Improvement after year 1 depends on product fixes, not negotiation.

## 5. What Falls Apart, and What Holds

**What falls apart:**
- **Concession-based saves as a profit driver.** With typical concessions, they roughly break even. With heavy ones, they destroy value.
- **Phase 1 paying for itself through retention alone.** At a $5M save rate, retention doesn't cover the strike team's $4–6M/year cost on a profit basis.
- **The $50–80M three-year impact figure from my first answer.** That should be revised well down.
- **Much of Strategy C's retention advantage over Strategy A.** A 1.4-point reduction in total-company churn falls inside the 1–2 points I estimated for the internal approach. Strategy C now has to justify itself through faster root-cause fixes, concession discipline, win-rate gains, and the new-venture pipeline, not through bigger saves.

**What holds:**
- **Saving still beats replacement.** Replacing $5M of recurring revenue would cost roughly $6–8.5M in sales and marketing plus 12–24 months of delay. A break-even save is worth about that much compared with replacement.
- **Revenue base and valuation.** Enterprise software is valued largely on recurring revenue. Letting accounts go spreads fixed R&D and overhead across a smaller base. A profit-neutral save still protects valuation and operating leverage, though that argument is weaker than profit and shouldn't carry the case alone.
- **Excluded upside.** The model leaves out services revenue from saved accounts, which could add meaningful profit per account.
- **Staged funding protects you.** Because Strategy C releases capital in tranches, a weaker save rate means less money lost, not a stranded investment.
- **Strategy B doesn't look better.** An independent lab does nothing for churn. Slower retention improvement means continued revenue decline, which makes long-horizon lab funding even more vulnerable to cuts.

---

## 6. How to Revise the Plan

1. **Make 40–50% churn in the at-risk pool the base case** and 30% or below the upside case, contingent on product fixes shipping. Present it that way to the CEO and CFO.
2. **Make light concessions and triage mandatory, not aspirational.** At realistic save rates, typical concessions are break-even and heavy ones are value-destroying. The deal desk rules from my last answer become essential.
3. **Put a gate on the save program itself.** Measure the first two quarterly renewal cohorts before scaling. If churn in the pool stays above about 45% while average discounts exceed about 12%, **stop concession-led saves** and redirect that budget to root-cause product fixes.
4. **Shift the strike team's weight toward root causes and win rate.** Fixing a product gap that drives churn across ten accounts beats negotiating ten discounts. Win-rate gains carry no concession cost, so their value rises in relative terms.
5. **Plan for a slower financial ramp.** Expect the program to be roughly profit-neutral for 18–24 months, with value building as root-cause fixes reduce the need for concessions. Say so upfront. A credible, modest case survives contact with reality. An inflated one ends your mandate at the first quarterly review.

**The bottom line:** At a realistic save rate, retention is a defensive necessity rather than a growth engine. The innovation mandate's outsized returns have to come from fixing why customers leave and from winning more new business, not from paying at-risk customers to stay.

The value grows later, to roughly $7M/year by year 4.

Win-rate gains are genuinely valuable over a customer's lifetime, but they follow a J-curve: costs come first and profits arrive later. Combined with the revised retention numbers, **the strike team doesn't pay for itself within three years on base-case assumptions.** That's the uncomfortable conclusion.

The more hopeful finding is that the modeling points to a lever that may be worth more than win rate: **fixing implementation losses across all new business**, not just the incremental wins.

## 1. Is a 3–5 Point Gain Realistic?

**Baseline:** $200M qualified pipeline per year, 20% win rate, $40M in new annual contract value. Each win-rate point is worth about **$2M in new annual contract value**.

**What you can influence depends on why deals are lost.** Your win/loss data will give the real mix, but enterprise software losses typically fall into these buckets:

| Loss reason | Addressable by | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| **No decision / status quo** (often the largest bucket) | Rarely; the buyer chose not to act | Mostly out of reach |
| **Product or architecture gaps** vs. modern competitors | Product fixes | 12–36 months, including the time for the market to notice |
| **References and reputation** (churn, analyst ratings, failed implementations) | Retention and implementation quality | Slow; reputations lag reality by 1–2 years |
| **Sales execution** (qualification, demos, deal strategy) | Sales enablement, deal reviews, better presales | 3–9 months, the fastest lever |
| **Price** | Discounting, which buys win rate by giving up deal value | Immediate, but expensive |

**Three reasons early gains are small:**
1. **Sales cycles of 9–18 months** mean most deals closing in year 1 were shaped before any product fixes existed.
2. **The churn problem feeds the win-rate problem.** Prospects call references and read analyst reports. While you're still losing customers, you're still losing deals because of it.
3. **The easiest gains are often fake.** Win rate rises if the sales team disqualifies weak deals earlier or discounts harder. The first shrinks the denominator; the second shrinks revenue per win.

**Realistic trajectory (points above the 20% baseline):**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Downside** | +0.5 | +1.0 | +1.5 |
| **Base** | +1.0 | +2.0 | +2.5 |
| **Upside** | +1.5 | +3.0 | +4.0 |

The original 3–5 point range belongs in the upside case for year 3 and beyond, not in the base case.

## 2. The Economics of $1M of Incremental New Business

One helpful fact: **incremental wins from an existing pipeline don't carry full acquisition cost.** The sales team, marketing, and pipeline are already paid for. The marginal costs are commissions, presales effort, implementation, and any discount needed to win. That's far cheaper than the $1.20–1.70 per dollar of new revenue I used earlier to estimate the cost of replacing churned revenue.

**Assumptions per scenario:**

| Assumption | Light | Typical | Heavy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commissions + presales (% of first-year contract value) | 15% | 20% | 25% |
| Implementation profit/loss (× contract value) | Break-even | –0.3× (billed 1.0×, cost 1.3×) | –0.8× (fixed-price overruns, discounted services) |
| Subscription revenue in year 1 (ramps with go-live) | 50% | 40% | 25% |
| Customers lost by year 3 (failed or troubled implementations) | 5% | 10% | 20% |

**Net gross profit per $1M of incremental annual contract value ($M):**

| | Light | Typical | Heavy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | +0.23 | –0.10 | –0.66 |
| Year 2 | +0.73 | +0.61 | +0.48 |
| Year 3 | +0.71 | +0.68 | +0.60 |
| **3-year total** | **1.67** | **1.19** | **0.41** |
| Annual run-rate after year 3 | ~0.71 | ~0.68 | ~0.60 |

Each new insurance customer typically stays 7–10 years and often expands to more lines of business. So **lifetime value per $1M is strong even in the typical case: roughly $5M+ in gross profit, undiscounted.** The problem is timing, not value.

## 5. The Lever the Model Uncovered: Implementation Losses

In the typical scenario, implementations lose 0.3× contract value. That loss applies to **all $40M of baseline new business every year, not just incremental wins.** That's about **$12M/year in implementation losses** already embedded in the business.

Cutting those losses in half would be worth roughly **$6M/year**. This lever has several advantages over win rate:
- **No sales-cycle lag.** It applies to new contracts and in-flight projects within 6–18 months.
- **No concessions or discounts required.**
- **It reduces future churn.** Troubled implementations create tomorrow's at-risk accounts.
- **It improves win rate.** Better implementations produce better references.

**How to capture it:**
- **Packaged implementations:** preconfigured templates by line of business and state, fixed-scope offerings
- **Commercial terms:** stop open-ended fixed-price commitments, bill by milestone, and start subscription revenue at signing rather than go-live
- **Systems integrator partners:** shift overrun risk to partners where they can deliver more efficiently
- **Deal desk gate on services margin:** no new deal signed below a minimum implementation margin without executive sign-off
- **Disqualify bad-fit deals.** Win rate on paper may dip, but profit per win rises.

**Caveat:** this depends entirely on your actual services profitability. If implementations are already profitable, this lever disappears. Pull project-level services profitability for the last 2–3 years before building a case on it. It's one of the most important data requests on your list.

## 6. Risks and Where Spending Can Grow Without Profit

- **Implementation capacity.** Another $5M of new contract value at 1.3× implementation cost means about $6.5M of implementation work, roughly 30–40 more consultants or partner capacity. If you don't add capacity, quality drops, and **this year's new customers become the at-risk cohort two years from now.**
- **Discounting to win.** Winning marginal deals on price inflates win rate while lowering revenue per win and training the market to wait for discounts.
- **Sales enablement programs** that generate training hours and new tools without changing outcomes.
- **Metric gaming.** Measure win rate consistently:
  - From a fixed pipeline stage
  - In dollars, not deal counts
  - Including "no decision" outcomes
  - Alongside average deal size, discount levels, and pipeline volume

  The best single metric is **new-business gross profit per dollar of qualified pipeline.** It captures win rate, pricing, and implementation economics together.

---

## 7. What This Means for the Plan

1. **Reframe the strike team's case as a 5-year value case, not a 3-year payback.** On base-case numbers, it's value-creating over the life of the customers it wins and saves, but it doesn't pay back quickly. Say so plainly before the CFO discovers it.
2. **Resize or re-sequence the strike team.** Start smaller, around $2–3M/year, focused on the few product fixes that drive both churn and losses. Scale only when the first two quarters of renewal and win/loss data support it.
3. **Add implementation economics as a primary workstream**, pending the services profitability data. It may be the fastest and largest profit lever available.
4. **Set clear ownership.** The Chief Revenue Officer owns win rate and deal-level pricing. The services leader owns implementation margins. You own the product fixes and the combined metric: gross profit per dollar of pipeline and net retained gross profit. A shared deal-level profitability view keeps anyone from winning a number by damaging someone else's.
5. **Be honest about "outsized."** On realistic assumptions, retention and win rate stabilize the business and create solid long-term value, but they don't produce outsized short-term returns. Outsized results would have to come from:
   - Implementation economics, if the losses are as large as they may be
   - Structural changes, such as a packaged cloud offering or a partner-led delivery model
   - The staged Phase 2 new bets

   That's another argument for keeping those bets cheap, fast, and gated. They're the part of the portfolio with real upside.

**Bottom line:** The core business improvements are worth doing, but they're a slow, disciplined recovery, not a turnaround in three years. If you present them as the source of outsized returns, the numbers will eventually contradict you. If you present them as the foundation, you can point to implementation economics and the staged bets as where the upside lives, and that pitch will survive the first quarterly review.
