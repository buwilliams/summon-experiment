# Neither Pure Strategy Is Your Best Move

As a new hire, openly contradicting the CEO's stated thesis is the fastest way to lose standing. But fully adopting his thesis makes you the likely scapegoat if churn continues. A hybrid, framed as *his* idea executed well, is almost certainly the winning play. Here's the reasoning.

## The Core Political Fact

The CEO has publicly committed, probably to the board, to "turning the ship around" with existing people and systems. An independent lab implicitly says **"the existing organization can't do this,"** which indicts his judgment and every executive who runs that organization. You'd be asking the people you most need to approve your plan to endorse a verdict against themselves.

But notice the tension in his own position: he hired an outsider to produce "outsized results." Part of him knows business-as-usual isn't working. Your job is to resolve that tension for him without making him look wrong.

## Proposal A: Work Within Existing Systems

**Who gains:** The CEO is vindicated. Incumbent executives (CTO, product, operations) keep their turf. Long-tenured employees feel trusted.

**Who's at risk:** You. If results are slow, and they likely will be, you become the innovation leader who didn't innovate. You risk capture: absorbed into existing processes, blamed for their failures, credited for nothing.

**Signals:**
- **Board:** Continuity and stability, but possibly "more of the same" if they're already skeptical.
- **Employees:** Reassuring. "We're not being replaced."
- **Customers:** Reassuring for conservative insurance carriers who fear platform disruption. Uninspiring for prospects comparing you with nimbler competitors.
- **Public/analysts:** Barely registers.

## Proposal B: Independent Innovation Lab

**Who gains:** You get visibility, autonomy, and a clear "you own this" narrative. Sales may like having something new to pitch. Board members pushing for bold change may champion you.

**Who's at risk:** The CEO loses face if he reverses his position. Heads of engineering and product see a rival power center draining budget and talent. The CFO sees cost without near-term revenue.

**Signals:**
- **Board:** Decisive, but possibly "the CEO's plan was wrong" or "a new hire is freelancing."
- **Employees:** "The real work happens elsewhere, and we're the legacy." This breeds the resentment that kills lab outputs when they need the core org to adopt them.
- **Customers:** Existing carriers may worry their platform is being deprioritized, which could accelerate churn. New prospects may be intrigued.
- **Public/analysts:** A splashy headline, but corporate innovation labs have a reputation for "innovation theater," and many are quietly shut down within a few years.

**A substantive problem too:** Independent labs are best at creating *new* markets. Churn is usually a *core* problem, such as product gaps, implementation pain, service quality, or pricing. A lab building the future won't stop customers leaving today, and the retention numbers are what everyone will watch.

## Where Looking Decisive Matters More Than Results

- **Board meetings in the next 1–2 quarters:** A credible, confident plan buys time before results exist.
- **At-risk customer accounts:** Carriers renewing contracts care more about feeling heard and supported than about your roadmap. Visible attention can save accounts before any product changes ship.
- **Employee morale during decline:** A narrative that the existing team can win matters for retention of key talent.

**The caveat:** In a churn situation the scoreboard is visible and fast. Optics buy you time, not survival. Your standing in 12–18 months will rest on whether retention and win rates actually move.

## The Third Approach: A Core-Anchored Acceleration Team

Structure it as **"protected, not separate"**:

1. **Framing:** Pitch it as the operational expression of the CEO's thesis: "unlocking the talent we already have by removing the friction that slows them down." He gets to be right. Avoid the word "lab." Something like "Customer Acceleration Team" signals urgency and core relevance.

2. **Mission:** Charter it first on the churn drivers, not moonshots. Early wins on retention are what the board, sales, and customers all care about, and they earn you credibility for bolder bets later.

3. **Staffing:** Mix in respected internal people nominated by existing leaders, plus a few outside hires. Insiders give it legitimacy and create advocates back in their home teams. It's also the CEO's "existing talent" story in action.

4. **Autonomy:** Seek exemptions only where they matter, such as faster procurement, lighter approval gates, and the freedom to talk directly to customers. That's enough independence to move quickly without creating a rival kingdom.

5. **Shared credit:** Give each project an operating executive as co-sponsor, with a defined path for handing results back to the business units. Their wins become their wins, which turns potential blockers into owners.

6. **Staged autonomy:** Propose a 90-day proof period with clear metrics. Success earns more budget and independence. This lowers the risk of approval for the CEO and CFO, and it lets you graduate toward a more independent structure *later* if the evidence supports it.

This is essentially the "ambidextrous organization" model (O'Reilly and Tushman): exploit the core while exploring new ground, with leadership integration between the two.

## How to Win Approval

- **Diagnose before you prescribe.** Spend your first 60–90 days on a listening tour: at-risk customers, lost deals, frontline staff, each executive. Newcomers who arrive with a solution look arrogant. Those who arrive with data look credible, and you'll know whether churn is truly a core-product problem.
- **Pre-wire one-on-one.** Never debut this in a group meeting. Walk the CEO through it first and let him shape it, so it becomes his plan. Then take it to the key executives individually and incorporate their input.
- **Identify your board-facing ally.** Learn which board members are driving the pressure for change. Make sure the CEO can present your plan to them as evidence of his decisiveness.
- **Put customers in the story.** A few at-risk carriers visibly engaged in co-designing fixes is the most persuasive signal to every audience at once.

**In short:** Proposal B would likely lose you the CEO. Proposal A would likely lose you your reputation. The hybrid lets the CEO be right, lets incumbents share credit, and lets you own the results, while keeping the option of a more independent unit once you've earned it.

## Turn 2

**User:** What if the board is already losing faith in the CEO? Would tying myself so closely to his thesis sink my standing along with his?

**Assistant:**

Yes, that risk is real. But the answer isn't to distance yourself from the CEO. It's to change *what* you're tied to. Bind your reputation to measurable outcomes and the company's customers, not to any one person's thesis. The hybrid approach still works. You just adjust how it's framed and credited.

## First, Verify the Premise

Newcomers often misread board dynamics from hallway talk. Look for concrete signals:
- The board is hiring outside advisors or consultants that report to it rather than to the CEO.
- Board committees are asking for direct access to executives below the CEO.
- The chair or lead director is visibly more active.
- A key ally of the CEO on the board has left or gone quiet.
- Compensation or strategy reviews are being accelerated.

Rumors of a CEO's decline are common in struggling companies and often premature. Acting on a false read, such as distancing yourself from a CEO who survives, would be costly.

## Why Disloyalty Is the Bigger Danger

It's tempting to hedge by quietly signaling to the board that you see things differently. Resist that, for practical and ethical reasons:

- **Boards distrust executives who undermine their CEO.** Even directors who want the CEO gone see it as a character flaw. They wonder if you'd do the same to his successor.
- **Incoming CEOs value loyalty too.** A new leader looks at how people behaved under the old regime. Someone seen as having played both sides is rarely kept.
- **The CEO will notice.** Under pressure, leaders become hypersensitive to perceived betrayal. If he survives, you're finished. If he doesn't, he may take you down on the way out.
- **Going around him is simply wrong.** You were hired to serve the company through its governance structure. Private back-channeling to directors is the kind of move that ends reputations across an industry.

## How to Hedge Legitimately

**1. Frame the work around the problem, not the person.**
In the original advice, you pitched the hybrid as the CEO's thesis in action. Now keep that as the internal framing, but make the public identity of the work about customers and metrics: "reducing churn in our top accounts" or "doubling win rates in mid-market carriers." Whoever runs the company, those goals stay valuable.

**2. Build a scoreboard early.**
Define 3–5 clear metrics, such as retention in at-risk accounts, time-to-value for new clients, and win rate against specific competitors. Report them consistently. A leader with visible numbers survives a leadership change far better than one known for a strategy.

**3. Spread credit widely.**
Credit operating executives, frontline teams, and customer co-designers more than the CEO personally. This builds alliances across the organization that outlast him. These people may also be the ones advising the board or a new CEO on who's valuable.

**4. Get board exposure through proper channels.**
Ask the CEO to have you present progress to the board or a committee. Most CEOs under pressure welcome showing that their initiatives are moving. It lets directors see your competence firsthand, legitimately.

**5. Document your diagnosis.**
Your listening tour findings, such as why customers churn and why deals are lost, should be written up and shared with the CEO. If leadership changes, a clear, candid, fact-based diagnosis shows you were seeing reality, without you ever having criticized anyone.

**6. Build design flexibility into the structure.**
The staged-autonomy model already lets the team grow more independent. If a new CEO wants bolder moves, you can scale up quickly. If the current CEO stays, you continue as planned. The same structure works under either leader.

## How Different Scenarios Play Out

| Scenario | What protects you |
|---|---|
| CEO survives | Your loyalty and the early wins you helped him show the board |
| CEO replaced by internal successor | Relationships with operating execs you shared credit with |
| Outside CEO brought in | Clear metrics and a documented diagnosis that make you look useful rather than part of the old regime |
| Activist investor or sale | Tangible results on retention and growth, which directly affect valuation |

## A Word on Timing and Self-Protection

- **The CEO may become more open to bold moves.** Leaders under pressure sometimes embrace initiatives they would have resisted, because they need a visible win. That could give you room to propose more ambitious elements of your original lab idea, framed as his decisive response.
- **He may also look for someone to blame.** A new innovation leader is an easy scapegoat. Your scoreboard and documented communications are your protection.
- **Personally, keep your options open.** Maintain your professional network and your reputation in the industry. Executives hired by a CEO under pressure sometimes find their tenure shortened through no fault of their own. The best insurance is a track record that travels with you.

**In short:** Be loyal in conduct, independent in substance. Serve the CEO faithfully through proper channels, while building your reputation on results, customers, and cross-organizational relationships. That way, you're valuable to whoever ends up running the company.

## Turn 3

**User:** If the board starts to rate me highly after those presentations, how do I keep the CEO from seeing me as a threat or a possible replacement?

**Assistant:**

You can't fully control how an insecure CEO perceives you, but you can make it very hard for him to see you as a rival. The principle is simple: **the board's regard for you should always feel like a reflection of his leadership, never a competing asset.** Every time directors think well of you, it should strengthen his standing, not dilute it.

## Make Your Success His Success

- **Present as his lieutenant.** In board presentations, open by connecting the work to his priorities: "When [CEO] brought me in, he asked us to focus on retention in our largest accounts. Here's where we are." That positions you as the executor of his agenda.
- **Let him deliver the good news first.** Share wins with him before anyone else, and encourage him to announce major results to the board himself. You can then provide the detail.
- **Credit his decisions specifically.** Not generic thanks, but concrete credit: "The decision to give the team direct access to customers was what made this possible." Directors notice when a CEO's choices produce results.

## Route Everything Through Him

- **Tell him about every director contact.** If a board member emails you, invites you to lunch, or asks for your views, tell the CEO promptly and matter-of-factly: "[Director] reached out asking about our churn data. I'm planning to send her the dashboard we shared last month. Anything you'd like me to add?" Transparency removes the suspicion that you're building a private channel.
- **Redirect strategic questions.** If a director asks your opinion on company-wide strategy or leadership, keep your answers within your remit: "That's really a question for [CEO]. On the retention side, here's what we're seeing." Never comment on his performance, even if invited to.
- **Decline private meetings where possible,** or invite him along. If a director insists on one-on-one time, keep it on your area of work and debrief him afterward.

## Avoid the Signals of a Rival

CEOs tend to feel threatened not by competence itself but by certain behaviors:

- **Widening your scope uninvited.** Stay focused on your mandate. If you see problems elsewhere, raise them privately with him, not in forums where others hear.
- **Building your own visible brand.** Be cautious about press, conference keynotes, or LinkedIn posts that make you the face of the turnaround. If external visibility is useful, ask him first, and share the stage.
- **Talking about the company's future in sweeping terms.** "Here's where this company needs to go" sounds like a CEO. "Here's what our customers need from us" sounds like a strong operator.
- **Gathering a faction.** Building cross-functional allies is good. Being seen as the center of a group of dissatisfied executives is dangerous. Keep your relationships grounded in the work, and never become the person others vent to about the CEO.

## Invest in the Relationship Directly

- **Meet regularly and candidly.** A standing one-on-one where you share bad news early, ask for his judgment, and give him private credit builds trust that survives board attention.
- **Ask for his guidance on board dynamics.** "How do you think the board will react to this?" signals that you see him as the one who manages that relationship.
- **Make him look good under pressure.** If the board is skeptical of him, offer to help him prepare: data, customer stories, proof points. Being useful to him when he's vulnerable is the strongest signal of loyalty.

## Watch for Warning Signs

Even with all this, he may become uneasy. Signs include being excluded from board meetings you previously attended, a sudden narrowing of your mandate, being asked to route all communications through him, or noticeably cooler interactions.

If you see these, **address them directly and privately:** "I've noticed some changes and want to make sure we're aligned. My goal is to make your turnaround succeed. Is there anything I should be doing differently?" Most CEOs respond well to that directness. Letting unspoken tension grow is worse.

## Be Honest With Yourself About Ambition

If you'd genuinely like to be CEO someday, here or elsewhere, that's a legitimate goal. But the way to get there is by being an outstanding executive under this CEO, not by positioning yourself against him. Boards tend to promote people who made their predecessor successful. And if he senses you're campaigning for his job, no amount of careful framing will fix it.

If he's eventually replaced and the board considers you, your record of loyalty will count in your favor. If he isn't, you'll have kept the trust of the person you report to.

**In short:** Be excellent, transparent, and generous with credit. When the board's respect for you consistently shows up as respect for his judgment in hiring and backing you, you become an asset to him rather than a threat.

## Turn 4

**User:** If the board is souring on him, won't routing everything through him make directors see me as his loyalist rather than an independent leader?

**Assistant:**

It's a fair worry, but it rests on a false choice. Directors don't judge independence by *who you route communications through*. They judge it by *whether what you tell them is true and unvarnished*. You can follow proper channels and still be seen as clearly independent-minded.

## The Distinction That Matters

- **Process loyalty:** Respecting the chain of command, keeping the CEO informed, not back-channeling. Boards expect this from every executive and would think less of you without it.
- **Loyalty of judgment:** Shading data, spinning setbacks, defending the CEO's decisions reflexively, echoing his framing when the evidence says otherwise. *This* is what makes someone look like a loyalist.

Directors who are souring on a CEO are usually looking for executives who will give them an accurate picture of the business. Give them that, through legitimate channels, and you'll be seen as independent regardless of reporting lines.

## How to Signal Independence Without Disloyalty

**1. Report bad news as plainly as good news.**
If a pilot fails or churn worsens in a segment, say so clearly in your presentations, with what you learned and what you'll change. Nothing establishes credibility faster than an executive who doesn't spin.

**2. Show that your conclusions come from evidence.**
Ground your presentations in customer data, lost-deal analysis, and your own diagnosis. When your recommendations follow visibly from facts, directors see a thinking leader, not a mouthpiece.

**3. Keep your own voice.**
Crediting the CEO for specific decisions is fine. Echoing his talking points isn't. Speak in your own terms about what you're seeing in the market and with customers.

**4. Be candid with the CEO, and let it be known you are.**
If you disagree with a direction, tell him privately and directly. If a director asks whether you've raised a concern, you can truthfully say, "Yes, I raised it with [CEO], and here's where we landed." That shows independence *within* the system, which is what boards want.

**5. Don't defend him personally.**
If a director probes about his leadership, don't praise or criticize. Stick to your remit: "I can speak to what we're seeing in retention." Staying neutral reads as professional, while jumping to his defense reads as partisan.

## Why Going Around Him Would Backfire

Some executives think that signaling independence means offering directors private views on the CEO. That usually damages them:

- **Directors wonder about your discretion.** If you'd confide in them about this CEO, you'd do the same to the next one, including whoever they appoint.
- **It can taint any future opportunity.** If the board later considers you for a bigger role, they want someone who earned it by performance, not by helping remove their predecessor.
- **Boards have their own tools.** When directors seriously doubt a CEO, they use formal mechanisms: executive sessions, outside advisors, structured interviews with senior leaders. They don't need you to volunteer.

## When Things Change

- **If the board formally asks for your input,** such as in a leadership review or during a CEO transition, answer honestly and constructively. Focus on the business, the challenges, and what the company needs, rather than on grievances. Candor through a board-initiated process is appropriate.
- **If you ever see real misconduct,** such as misrepresenting results to the board, ethical or legal violations, or retaliation, the rules change. Use formal escalation channels like the general counsel, audit committee, or whistleblower process. That's not disloyalty. It's your duty to the company.

**In short:** Directors don't need you to be disloyal to see you as independent. They need you to be accurate. Follow the chain of command in process, but be rigorously honest in substance. That combination makes you look like exactly the kind of leader a board wants to keep, whoever ends up running the company.
