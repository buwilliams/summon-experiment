## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

Neither strategy is right on its own, and framing it as a choice between them may be the biggest risk to your success. Here's why, and what I'd suggest instead.

## First, question the diagnosis

Before choosing a structure, ask what's actually driving the churn and lost deals. In enterprise insurance software, the causes are often:

- **Core product problems**: outdated UX, slow implementation, integration pain, reliability issues
- **Execution problems**: poor support, missed deadlines, painful upgrades
- **Strategic displacement**: competitors (often cloud-native insurtechs) offering a fundamentally different model

An independent lab mostly addresses the third. If customers are leaving because implementations take 18 months or the platform is hard to configure, a lab building new things won't stop the bleeding. Talk to churned customers and lost prospects in your first 60–90 days. The answer shapes everything.

## Why each pure strategy tends to fail

**Working entirely within existing systems (the CEO's view)**
- New ideas get evaluated against the metrics, margins, and timelines of the existing business, and they almost always lose.
- The org's "immune system" (budget cycles, risk reviews, sales comp tied to current products) smothers anything unfamiliar.
- If the existing systems and talent could produce outsized results, the company probably wouldn't be in this situation.

**A fully independent lab (your view)**
- Labs often produce clever prototypes that never reach customers because the core org won't adopt, sell, or support them.
- They breed resentment ("the cool kids who don't have to deal with legacy code").
- They're easy to cut in the next budget crunch because they're disconnected from revenue.
- In insurance software specifically, innovation has to handle regulatory complexity, carrier integrations, and legacy data. That knowledge lives in the core organization.

## What tends to work: separate but connected

Research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly and Tushman's work is worth reading) consistently finds the winning model is **structural separation with deliberate integration**:

- **Separate**: its own team, processes, metrics, and budget protection, so it isn't judged by core-business standards too early.
- **Connected**: shared executive sponsorship (ideally the CEO directly), rotation of people between the lab and the core, and clear pathways for how successful work gets handed off, scaled, and sold.

Practically, that might mean:

1. **A small dedicated team** that includes respected insiders, not just outside hires. Insiders give you credibility and knowledge of where the bodies are buried.
2. **A two-track portfolio**: some work aimed at near-term pain in the core business (retention, win rates) and some at longer-term bets. The near-term wins buy you political capital for the long-term ones.
3. **Different metrics by stage**: learning milestones and customer validation early, revenue and adoption later.
4. **Customers involved from day one.** Co-develop with a few carriers, including at-risk accounts. Nothing builds internal credibility faster than a customer saying "we're renewing because of this."

## On the CEO disagreement

You're new, and your boss has a stated view. Don't pitch this as "you're wrong, we need independence." Instead:

- **Find the common ground.** He's right that innovation has to leverage existing talent and ultimately flow through the core business. Say so.
- **Frame separation as a tool for his goal**, not a rejection of it: "To get outsized results from our people, we need to give a small group protection from processes designed for the current business."
- **Propose a time-boxed pilot** with clear success criteria rather than a big org redesign. Early evidence will win the argument better than debate.
- **Clarify what "outsized results" means to him**: reduced churn? New revenue? A new product line? In what timeframe? Misaligned expectations here sink more innovation leaders than structure does.

## Bottom line

Your instinct that the existing machinery will crush innovation is well-founded. So is the CEO's instinct that a disconnected lab won't turn the ship. The strongest position is to take the best of both, anchor it in a clear diagnosis of why customers are leaving, and prove it with early wins that matter to the core business.

If you share more about why customers are churning or what the competitive landscape looks like, I can help you get more specific.
