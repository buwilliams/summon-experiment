## Prompt

Use the method of conjectures and refutations (critical rationalism) to answer the following.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

# Conjectures and Refutations Applied to Your Innovation Strategy

Critical rationalism doesn't ask "which strategy is right?" as if we could prove one true. It asks which conjectures survive our best attempts to refute them, and how we can design tests that would expose our errors quickly and cheaply. So let's treat both your view and the CEO's as bold guesses and try to break them.

---

## Step 1: State the Problem Precisely

Popper insisted we start from a *problem*, not an observation or a preferred solution. Your stated question ("lab or no lab?") is about means. The actual problem situation is:

> **P1:** The company is losing existing customers (churn) *and* failing to win new ones. Current performance is unacceptable, and the CEO expects outsized improvement.

These are two distinct failures with potentially different causes:
- **Churn** is usually a verdict on the *current* product, service, implementation, and relationship.
- **Losing new deals** may reflect the product, but also price, positioning, sales execution, or a competitor with a better offering.

Keep this distinction in mind. It will become the most important refutation of both positions.

---

## Step 2: Conjecture A (The CEO's)

> **CA:** The company can turn around by working within existing systems, procedures, and talent.

**What CA predicts (its testable consequences):**
1. The capabilities needed to fix the problem already exist internally.
2. Good ideas either exist or can be generated by current staff.
3. Existing processes can move those ideas to customers fast enough to matter.
4. What's missing is focus, leadership, or priority, not structure.

**Attempted refutations:**
- **The historical record.** The existing system, procedures, and talent produced the current decline. That doesn't *prove* they can't fix it, but it shifts the burden: CA must explain what will be different this time. If the answer is "a new leader (you)," then CA quietly concedes that *something* in the system needs to change.
- **The incentive problem.** Large incumbents reliably allocate resources to existing customers and existing revenue, and starve new initiatives that look small and uncertain. This is the core of Christensen's *Innovator's Dilemma*, and it's well corroborated. If your company's budgeting, roadmap, and approval processes work this way, CA predicts innovation will happen, but the structure predicts it will be smothered.
- **Speed.** If a new idea takes 12–18 months to clear architecture review, security, and roadmap prioritization, CA may be correct in principle yet fatally slow in practice.

**Where CA survives criticism:**
- Existing employees hold deep knowledge of insurance workflows, regulatory requirements, and legacy integrations (policy admin, claims, billing). Much of that knowledge is tacit and can't be hired quickly.
- Existing staff own the customer relationships. If churn is the urgent problem, the people who touch customers daily are essential to fixing it.
- CA may be partially true: the *talent* may be adequate while the *procedures* are the bottleneck. CA bundles three things (systems, procedures, talent) that may not stand or fall together.

**What would refute CA decisively:**
- An inventory showing that good ideas have repeatedly been proposed internally and killed or delayed by process.
- Measurements showing idea-to-customer cycle time far exceeds the time available before key accounts leave.

---

## Step 3: Conjecture B (Yours)

> **CB:** Success requires an innovation lab independent of the large organization.

**What CB predicts:**
1. Independence will produce faster, better ideas and products.
2. Those products will reach customers and move churn and win rates.
3. The benefits of independence will outweigh the costs of separation.

**Attempted refutations:**

- **The churn mismatch.** This is the strongest refutation. Insurance software sales cycles often run 12–24 months, and carriers are conservative buyers. A new lab starting today might produce a sellable product in a year or two, then face a long sales cycle. Meanwhile, customers leaving *now* are leaving over the current product and service. An independent lab, almost by definition, doesn't touch the things causing today's churn. **CB may be a good answer to a different problem** (long-term growth) while leaving P1's most urgent half unaddressed.

- **The transplant-rejection problem.** Corporate innovation labs have a poor track record. A recurring pattern: the lab builds something promising, then the core business can't or won't absorb it. Sales doesn't know how to sell it, engineering won't support it, and it doesn't integrate with the platform customers already run. In insurance software, integration with the core platform often *is* the product. A lab that's truly independent may build things that can't be sold through existing channels or deployed into existing customer environments.

- **"Innovation theater."** Many labs produce demos, hackathons, and press releases rather than revenue. If the lab's success criteria aren't tied to churn and win-rate outcomes, CB becomes unfalsifiable: any result can be called "learning."

- **The political refutation.** Your CEO has explicitly stated a contrary belief. A lab created over the objection of its most important sponsor tends to be defunded at the first budget squeeze. Independence requires protection, and protection comes from the top. CB in its pure form may be self-defeating in *this* organization, even if it's sound in general.

**Where CB survives criticism:**
- The incentive and speed arguments against CA are real. Separation *is* the corroborated remedy when new initiatives need different processes, metrics, and risk tolerance.
- Some insurtech-style opportunities (new distribution models, AI-driven underwriting or claims tools, new customer segments) may genuinely require freedom from legacy architecture and legacy economics.

**What would refute CB decisively:**
- Churn and lost-deal analysis showing customers leave over service quality, implementation failures, stability, or price, none of which a separate lab would address.
- Evidence that the main barrier to new sales is integration with the existing platform, which a separate lab is worst positioned to solve.

---

## Step 4: Expose the Shared Hidden Assumption

Popper emphasized that the most productive criticism often targets an assumption both sides share without noticing. Here it is:

> **Both CA and CB assume we already know that the problem is an innovation problem, and debate only its organizational form.**

Neither conjecture has been tested against the actual causes of churn and lost deals. If customers leave because implementations run over budget, support is slow, or the platform is unstable, then *neither* "innovate within" nor "innovate outside" is the right frame. The fix might be operational excellence, not innovation at all.

This doesn't mean innovation is irrelevant. It means the question "lab or no lab?" is premature until this assumption is tested.

---

## Step 5: A Bolder, More Testable Conjecture

A better conjecture should explain everything the competitors explain, survive the refutations that damaged them, and expose itself to sharper tests.

> **CC:** The turnaround requires *two different kinds of work run under different rules*:
> 1. **A retention track embedded in the core**, targeting the specific, diagnosed causes of churn with existing talent but *exempted from normal procedures* (fast-track approvals, dedicated capacity, direct customer access).
> 2. **A small, autonomous venture team** with separate processes, metrics, and budget, but *structurally linked* to the core through a CEO-level sponsor, shared customer access, and a defined path for handing products to sales and engineering. It pursues only opportunities that diagnosis shows the core cannot address.
>
> **Both tracks are governed by pre-committed, falsifiable success criteria and kill conditions.**

This resembles what O'Reilly and Tushman call the "ambidextrous organization": separate units for exploration and exploitation, integrated at the senior leadership level. That model is better corroborated than either full integration or full independence.

**Why CC is better, in Popperian terms:**

| Criterion | CA (Within) | CB (Independent lab) | CC (Ambidextrous, problem-first) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addresses churn (urgent) | Partially | Poorly | Directly |
| Addresses new growth | Weakly (incentives) | Well | Well, if diagnosis supports it |
| Survives incentive critique | No | Yes | Yes |
| Survives transplant-rejection critique | Yes | No | Mostly (designed-in integration) |
| Survives political critique | Yes | No | Yes (CEO co-owns it) |
| Falsifiable | Vaguely | Vaguely | Explicitly (kill criteria) |

CC also takes the CEO's view seriously rather than defeating it. It concedes that existing talent and knowledge are essential, and challenges only the *procedures*, which is the weakest part of CA.

---

## Step 6: Design the Crucial Tests

Knowledge grows through attempted refutation, so you should run tests whose outcomes could prove *you* wrong, not just the CEO. Do these in your first 60–90 days:

**Test 1: Why do customers leave? (Tests the shared hidden assumption)**
- Interview recently churned and at-risk customers directly, not through account managers.
- *Refutes innovation-focused strategies* if the dominant causes are service, stability, implementation, or price.
- *Supports the need for innovation* if customers cite missing capabilities or a competitor's superior product.

**Test 2: Why do we lose deals? (Tests CB's growth rationale)**
- Run a lost-deal analysis on the last 12–24 months. Who won, and why?
- *Supports a venture track* if you're losing to fundamentally different offerings.
- *Refutes it* if you're losing on price, references, or sales execution.

**Test 3: The graveyard audit (Tests CA)**
- Catalog ideas proposed internally in recent years and what happened to them.
- *Refutes CA's "procedures" claim* if good ideas were repeatedly killed or stalled by process.
- *Refutes the "talent" critique* if many good ideas existed. That would mean the people are fine and the system is the problem.

**Test 4: The speed test (Tests CA vs. CB directly)**
- Pick one small, clearly valuable improvement for an at-risk customer. Try to ship it through normal channels, and track every blocker.
- Then ship a comparable improvement via a protected fast-track team.
- The gap in time and outcome is direct evidence on how much structure matters, and it gives the CEO data rather than your opinion.

**Pre-commit your refutation criteria.** Before running the tests, write down with the CEO what results would change each of your minds. For example: "If more than 60% of churned customers cite service rather than product gaps, the first investment goes to retention, not a lab." Agreeing in advance on what counts as refutation turns a political disagreement into a shared investigation.

---

## Step 7: Tentative Conclusion

Neither strategy can be shown to be "right," but both can be criticized, and both have serious flaws as currently stated:

- **The CEO's conjecture** is weakened by the incentive and speed problems that make incumbents poor at new work, and by the fact that the current system produced the current situation.
- **Your conjecture** is weakened by the mismatch between a lab's timeline and the urgency of churn, by the poor track record of isolated labs, and by the political reality that a lab opposed by the CEO is unlikely to survive.

The conjecture that best survives criticism is to **diagnose first, fix retention inside the core with procedural exemptions, and build a small, linked (not isolated) venture team only where evidence shows the core can't deliver**, all with falsifiable success and kill criteria.

This position is provisional. The customer and deal evidence from your first 90 days may refute it, and if so, that's the method working. You were hired to produce outsized results. The fastest route is probably not winning the argument with the CEO, but proposing experiments that could prove either of you wrong, and letting reality decide.
