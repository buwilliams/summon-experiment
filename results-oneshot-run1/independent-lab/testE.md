## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I don't want to know which idea sounds more grown-up. I want to know which one is more fun. Pretend each idea is a new toy: what does it do, and who gets to play with it? If each one happened, would the people at work be happy or sad, and would anybody cry? Where is each idea kind of boring, so that nobody would want to hear about it at dinner? Does either idea come with snacks, or a place to play? And is there a third idea that everybody likes better, like a swing set for the whole office?

## Response

# Toy #1: The Big Ship Steering Wheel
*(Work inside the existing systems, procedures, and talent)*

**What it does:** You grab the giant wheel on the existing ship and try to turn it. The wheel is attached to 40 committees, a release calendar, and a compliance review.

**Who gets to play:** Technically everyone. In practice, whoever controls roadmaps, budgets, and approvals. You get to play when it's your turn, which is Q3.

**Happy or sad?**
- **Happy:** The CEO, because it's his idea. Long-tenured people, who feel respected rather than replaced.
- **Sad:** You, slowly. The restless people who've been waiting for permission to try something new, because "work within the system" sounds a lot like "nothing changes."
- **Who cries:** You, in meeting #47, when your idea comes back from review sanded down into a slightly better settings page.

**The boring part:** Almost all of it. "We aligned stakeholders and incorporated feedback into the roadmap" has never been said at dinner by a happy person.

**Snacks or a place to play?** No. You play in the same conference rooms where the churn happened.

**Secret strength:** Anything you build is already *inside* the product, the sales motion, and the support process. Nothing has to be "transferred" later.

---

# Toy #2: The Treehouse
*(An independent innovation lab)*

**What it does:** You build a treehouse away from the big house. Different rules, fast decisions, no committees, cool stickers on the laptops.

**Who gets to play:** You and a small, hand-picked crew, often new hires or the company's brightest people, poached from their teams.

**Happy or sad?**
- **Happy:** The treehouse kids, for about 12 to 18 months. It's the most fun job in the company.
- **Sad:** Everyone left in the big house. They're still dealing with angry customers while the treehouse kids demo prototypes and get the CEO's attention. Resentment builds quietly.
- **Who cries:**
  1. The core product team, when they hear the treehouse will "reinvent" what they've spent years building.
  2. The treehouse kids, later, when their prototype needs the core platform, security review, and sales team to reach customers, and the big house says "not this quarter." This is the classic way labs die: the organization rejects what the lab builds, the way a body rejects a transplant.
  3. You, when the CEO, who never wanted a treehouse, cuts it in the next budget cycle.

**The boring part:** Surprisingly, the *outputs*. Labs are great at impressive demos, but a demo that never ships is dinner conversation once. "We built a cool AI claims prototype" is fun. "...and it's still a prototype" is not.

**Snacks or a place to play?** Yes, definitely. Snacks, a separate space, maybe a nicer coffee machine. Unfortunately, the snacks are part of why everyone else resents it.

**Secret weakness:** Your company's problem is *churning customers and losing deals*. A treehouse is far away from customers. The CEO also disagrees with it, and you're new. Starting your job by fighting your boss's core belief spends trust you haven't earned yet.

---

# Toy #3: The Swing Set in the Middle of the Playground
*(A small, protected strike team, built from existing people, aimed at real customers)*

**What it does:** You don't build a treehouse across town, and you don't wait in line at the steering wheel. You put a swing set **in the middle of the playground where everyone can see it.**

Concretely:
- **Pick one painful, real problem.** Examples: a big customer who is about to leave, a deal you keep losing, or the top reason customers cite when they churn.
- **Build a small team of borrowed insiders.** Grab 5 to 8 people *from* the existing organization: an engineer who knows the ugly legacy code, a support person who hears customer complaints daily, a salesperson who keeps losing that deal, and someone from compliance, so they become an ally instead of a blocker.
- **Give them protection and a clock.** They work full-time on this for 90 days. The CEO signs off on a short list of rules they can skip, such as normal approval chains. You shield them from everything else.
- **Put a real customer in the room.** The team talks to that customer every week and ships something the customer actually uses. It doesn't have to be a prototype. It should be something live, even if it's small.
- **Throw a demo day for the whole company.** Customers show up if possible, the CEO speaks, and the snacks are for *everyone*.
- **Send the team home.** They go back to their old teams afterward and bring the new ways of working with them. Then you pick the next problem and a new crew.

**Who gets to play:** Anyone, eventually. Because people rotate through, playing on the swings becomes something people *want* to do, rather than something reserved for the cool kids.

**Happy or sad?**
- **The CEO is happy.** It's literally "existing systems and talent," just pointed differently.
- **You're happy.** You get the speed and independence of a lab without the isolation.
- **Frontline people are happy.** Someone finally listens to what they've been saying about customers for years.
- **Who cries:** Maybe the customer who was about to leave, in a good way. And maybe a middle manager who loses a strong person for 90 days. Give them credit publicly at demo day, and they'll get over it.

**The boring part:** Picking the first problem, negotiating which rules the team gets to skip, and borrowing people from their managers. That's a couple of weeks of unglamorous work. After that, the story is: *"We saved a customer we were about to lose, in 90 days, using people who already worked here."* That one gets told at dinner, and at the next board meeting.

**Snacks and a place to play?** Yes. It gets a real room in the main building, visible to everyone, with a wall showing progress and a demo day with food. The difference is that the fun is out in the open, where everyone can see it and join in.

---

# The straight answer

- **The CEO is right** that you can't abandon the existing organization. The products, customers, and knowledge all live there.
- **You're right** that innovation suffocates under normal procedures.
- **Both pure versions fail predictably.** The steering wheel gets diluted into nothing. The treehouse gets rejected by the organization or defunded.

**Go with the swing set.** Pitch it to the CEO as *his* strategy with a sharper edge: "Existing talent, protected from the existing process, aimed at our churn problem." Win one customer back. After two or three wins, you'll have earned enough trust to build something more independent if it's still needed, and by then the organization will want it instead of resenting it.
