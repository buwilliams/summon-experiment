## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I don't want to know which idea sounds more grown-up. I want to know which one is more fun. Pretend each idea is a new toy: what does it do, and who gets to play with it? If each one happened, would the people at work be happy or sad, and would anybody cry? Where is each idea kind of boring, so that nobody would want to hear about it at dinner? Does either idea come with snacks, or a place to play? And is there a third idea that everybody likes better, like a swing set for the whole office?

## Response

# Toy #1: The Upgrade Kit (the CEO's idea)

**What it does:** You take the big toy the company already owns and snap new parts onto it. It still looks like the old toy, but now it lights up and makes new noises.

**Who gets to play:** Everyone, in theory. In practice, the people who already hold the controls: product managers, engineering leads, the folks who know where the bodies are buried in the codebase.

**Happy or sad?** Mostly relieved at first. Nobody gets left out, and nobody's job looks threatened. Later it gets frustrating, because every new part has to fit the old toy's sockets. People with big ideas slowly learn to have medium ideas.

**Would anyone cry?** Quietly. The one or two genuinely inventive people will cry into their keyboards when their idea goes through its fourth review committee, and then they'll update their LinkedIn.

**The boring part:** Almost all of it, at dinner. "We improved the claims workflow by 11%" is real progress, and it's also how conversation dies. The deeper problem is that you're competing against rivals who built a different toy entirely, and a slightly shinier version of yours may not win those customers back.

**Snacks or a place to play?** No new place. You play in the same conference rooms with the same stale bagels. The upside is that it's cheap and the toy is already on the shelf where customers can reach it.

# Toy #2: The Secret Clubhouse (your idea)

**What it does:** You build a brand-new toy from scratch, somewhere the grown-ups can't meddle. It can be anything, and it doesn't have to fit anybody's sockets.

**Who gets to play:** A small, hand-picked crew, maybe some new hires plus a few rescued rebels from the main company. Everyone else watches through the window.

**Happy or sad?** Very happy inside the clubhouse. Outside, it gets complicated. People in the main building start calling it "the playground" or "the country club," and not nicely. The team that pays the bills while you experiment feels like the unglamorous older sibling.

**Would anyone cry?** Possibly you, eighteen months from now. The classic failure pattern for innovation labs is that they build something cool, then try to hand it to the main company, and the main company's immune system rejects it: sales won't sell it, engineering won't support it, and finance asks why it cost so much. The toy never leaves the clubhouse. Separately, the core team may cry about budget going to the clubhouse while they're told to do more with less.

**The boring part:** Surprisingly little at first. It's the best dinner story of the three ("we're building the future of insurance!"). The boring part comes later: governance, integration, and the "how does this actually reach customers" meetings that the clubhouse was designed to avoid.

**Snacks or a place to play?** Yes, lots. Beanbags, whiteboard walls, probably a fancy coffee machine. Be careful with that, because visible perks become a symbol of resentment fast.

**One more thing:** The CEO who hired you believes in the upgrade kit. Building a clubhouse he didn't ask for, in your first months, is a bit like bringing your own toy to someone's birthday party and saying theirs is broken. Even if you're right, you need him on your side.

# Toy #3: The Treehouse with a Rope Ladder (the swing set)

This is the version most companies that actually pull this off end up with. Researchers call it the "ambidextrous organization," but think of it as a treehouse in the company's own backyard.

**What it does:** A small team gets real independence: its own pace, its own rules for experimenting, and freedom from the normal approval gauntlet. But it's in the backyard, not across town, and it has a rope ladder going down to the main house.

**How it works:**
- **Aim at the real pain.** The treehouse's first job is the thing that's actually hurting: why customers are leaving and why you're losing deals. Go talk to churned customers and lost prospects. That gives the work obvious value to everyone, including the CEO.
- **Rotate people up the ladder.** Folks from the core business spend a few months in the treehouse and then go back down. They carry new habits home, and the core team feels like it owns the treehouse instead of resenting it.
- **Build the landing zone first.** Before you build anything, agree with one core product leader on how a successful experiment gets adopted. Name a sponsor, set a budget line, and define what "good enough to ship" means.
- **Get early wins that show up at dinner.** One small, visible win in 90 days, like a feature a churning customer asked for or a prototype that helps win a deal, buys you the right to try bigger things.
- **Let the CEO tell it as his idea.** You're "turning the ship around with our existing talent," with a fast lane attached. That's true, and it keeps your sponsor enthusiastic.

**Happy or sad?** Mostly happy. Inventive people get a place to go. Core teams get help with their hardest problems instead of competition. The CEO gets his story, and you get your independence.

**Would anyone cry?** Less. Some people will still want the full clubhouse, and some will still resist change. But fewer people are shut out, so fewer people fight you.

**Snacks?** Some, shared with whoever climbs up. That matters more than the snacks themselves.

# The short version

- **Upgrade Kit:** safe and cheap, but probably too slow and too small to stop the churn.
- **Secret Clubhouse:** the most fun, but it often builds great toys nobody ever plays with, and it starts you off at odds with your boss.
- **Treehouse:** independent enough to invent, connected enough to matter, and pitched in a way the CEO can champion.

If you want a first move, spend your first month interviewing customers who left. Whatever they tell you becomes the treehouse's first project, and the evidence behind your case for how much independence it needs.
