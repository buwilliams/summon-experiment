## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

Neither strategy is right on its own, and choosing between them may be premature. A few points are worth weighing before you commit, especially since you're new.

## Start with the diagnosis, not the structure

Your company is **churning customers and losing deals**. That's a symptom, and the cause determines the structure:

- **If customers leave because the core product is slow, buggy, hard to implement, or missing table-stakes features**, you need to fix the core. An independent lab building something new won't stop churn in the existing base. It might even pull talent and attention away from the problem.
- **If customers leave because the market is shifting** (e.g., to cloud-native platforms, embedded insurance, AI-driven underwriting/claims, or new competitors with different business models) and your architecture or business model can't follow, then working within existing systems will likely fail. That's where a separate unit earns its keep.

Most struggling incumbents have *both* problems. Spend your first 60–90 days talking to churned customers, lost prospects, sales, and support to figure out which dominates.

## What the evidence says about each approach

**Pure "work within the system" (CEO's view)** tends to produce incremental improvement. The existing processes, incentives, and approval cycles were built to protect a stable business, and they reliably smother anything that threatens current revenue, margins, or architecture. It's rarely how "outsized results" happen.

**Pure independent lab (your view)** has a poor track record too. Common failure modes:
- It becomes "innovation theater": demos and prototypes that never ship.
- The core organization treats it as a threat or a toy, and its outputs are rejected ("not invented here").
- It loses funding when the sponsoring executive leaves or budgets tighten.
- It's disconnected from customers, sales channels, and domain expertise, which are huge assets in insurance, a heavily regulated, relationship-driven industry.

**What tends to work** is what O'Reilly and Tushman call an *ambidextrous* structure. The new unit is separate in how it operates (its own team, processes, metrics, tools, and freedom from legacy approval cycles) but tightly integrated at the top: a senior sponsor, shared strategy, and deliberate paths to leverage the core's customers, data, distribution, and regulatory know-how.

## A practical path forward

1. **Don't frame it to the CEO as "you're wrong."** You're weeks in, and his support is your most important asset. His instinct to use existing talent and customers is partly correct. Those are real advantages startups don't have.
2. **Propose a small, protected team rather than a big lab.** A few strong people, some pulled from inside (credibility, domain knowledge), some from outside (fresh methods). Give them explicit exemptions from specific processes that would slow them.
3. **Aim it at a real customer problem tied to churn or win rates**, not blue-sky exploration. An early win that sales can sell or that saves an at-risk account buys you political capital.
4. **Define the handoff mechanism up front.** How does something the team builds get adopted, funded, supported, and sold by the core? This is where most labs die.
5. **Use different metrics.** Measure learning velocity, customer validation, and pilot conversions early, not revenue or ROI on core-business timelines.
6. **Secure direct CEO sponsorship** with a regular review cadence, so the unit has air cover when the organization pushes back. It will.

## How to pitch it

Something like: *"I agree we should leverage our existing talent, customers, and systems. That's our advantage. To move at the speed the market demands, I'd like to set up a small team that operates with different rules for a defined set of problems, with clear paths to bring what works back into the core."*

That respects his view, gets you the independence you need, and lowers the risk for both of you.

If you share more about why customers are leaving and what the product looks like, I can help you think through which problems should go to the core versus a separate team.
