## Prompt

I run customer success at a company that sells claims-management software to regional insurance carriers. Over the last 18 months our annual churn has doubled. Our VP of Product believes customers are leaving because of product gaps: competitors have better analytics and AI features, so we need to accelerate the roadmap. Our VP of Services believes customers are leaving because of a painful implementation and support experience: our implementations run long, and customers never get to full value, so we need to fix onboarding and service before building more features. Which belief is right, and what should we do?

I don't want to know which idea sounds more grown-up. I want to know which one is more fun. Pretend each idea is a new toy: what does it do, and who gets to play with it? If each one happened, would the people at work be happy or sad, and would anybody cry? Where is each idea kind of boring, so that nobody would want to hear about it at dinner? Does either idea come with snacks, or a place to play? And is there a third idea that everybody likes better, like a swing set for the whole office?

## Response

# Two Toys and a Swing Set

## Toy #1: The Shiny Robot (VP of Product's idea)

**What it does:** It lights up, talks, and makes predictions. AI claims triage! Fraud-scoring dashboards! It looks amazing in the box.

**Who gets to play with it:** Product managers, engineers, the marketing team (they *love* this toy), and Sales, who finally get to wave something shiny at prospects. Customers can play with it only once their data is fully hooked up and flowing.

**Happy or sad?** Engineers and marketing are thrilled. Sales is thrilled. The Customer Success team starts out excited, then quietly gets nervous, because every new feature is another thing to implement, train, and support on top of a process that's already slow.

**Would anybody cry?** Your implementation team might. If onboarding is the bottleneck, a bigger roadmap means more stuff piling onto an already-overloaded conveyor belt. And customers stuck in month nine of implementation would cry a little when they hear about the cool new AI they still can't reach.

**The boring part nobody wants at dinner:** Analytics and AI run on clean, fully integrated claims data. The robot needs batteries, and the batteries are... data migration, integrations with policy admin systems, and field mapping. That's implementation work. So the shiny toy secretly depends on the boring toy.

**Snacks?** Yes, lots. Launch parties, conference booths, webinars, press releases. This toy is all snacks.

## Toy #2: The Instruction Manual That Actually Works (VP of Services' idea)

**What it does:** It makes the toys you already own finally work. Shorter implementations, faster go-lives, customers actually using the product they paid for.

**Who gets to play with it:** Customers, mostly, which is sort of the point. Also implementation consultants, support agents, and CSMs, who stop spending their days apologizing.

**Happy or sad?** Customers get happy slowly but deeply. Your services and CS teams get much happier. Product and engineering may feel a bit sad, since "pause the fun roadmap to fix onboarding" can feel like being told to clean your room before recess.

**Would anybody cry?** Sales might sniffle. "Our onboarding is 40% faster now" is true and valuable, but it's harder to demo than a talking robot. And if competitors really are pulling ahead on features, a long roadmap freeze could hurt later.

**The boring part nobody wants at dinner:** Nearly all of it. Templated configurations, data-migration tooling, implementation playbooks, go-live checklists. Nobody posts about this on LinkedIn.

**Snacks?** Not many. But it comes with a **place to play**: the customer's actual office, where adjusters are either using your software every day or working around it with spreadsheets.

## So Which Toy Is "Right"?

Honestly, you can't tell from the two stories alone, and both VPs are describing the world from where they sit. But there's a clue in the details: **a customer who never reached full value can't be leaving because a competitor has better AI.** They never got far enough to miss it. If a lot of your churned accounts were stuck in or just past implementation, Services is closer to right. If your churned accounts were fully live, heavy users who left for a competitor's analytics, Product is closer to right.

My guess is you'll find mostly the first group with a meaningful slice of the second. But you can find out for real.

## The Swing Set Everybody Likes: "Time to Value"

The third idea: stop arguing about features vs. service and make the whole company chase one shared goal, **how fast a new customer gets real, measurable value**. Everybody can push that swing:

- **Services** shortens implementations and builds repeatable playbooks.
- **Product** builds features that *speed up* implementation (data import tools, prebuilt integrations, configuration templates), plus analytics that show value early, like a "here's what you saved this quarter" dashboard. That's still fun to build, and it's demo-able.
- **Sales** gets a great new story: "Live in 90 days, value in 120."
- **CS** gets customers who renew.

Nobody has to lose. The robot gets built, but it gets built so that it helps people get the rest of their toys working faster.

## What to Actually Do (the Grown-Up Part, Briefly)

1. **Pull every churned account from the last 18 months.** For each one, record: implementation length, whether they ever fully went live, product usage before leaving, where they went, and what they said on the way out.
2. **Compare against retained customers.** If retained customers went live faster, that's strong evidence for Services.
3. **Call 8–10 lost customers yourself.** Ask what made them leave, not which features they wanted. People often say "features" when they mean "we never got it working."
4. **Ask what changed 18 months ago.** Churn doubling suggests a trigger: a new customer segment, services staff turnover, a pricing change, or a competitor launch.
5. **Bring both VPs the data together** and propose time-to-value as the shared metric. It's much easier to agree on a swing set when you've both seen the playground.
