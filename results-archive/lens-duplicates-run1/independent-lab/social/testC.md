# independent-lab/social/testC

## Prompt

Use impression management to answer the following.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

# Short Answer

**On substance, you're probably closer to right. On impression management, pitching it as you've framed it would likely fail.**

The research on established firms facing disruption (Christensen, O'Reilly & Tushman's work on "ambidextrous organizations") generally supports protecting new ventures from the core business's processes, metrics, and antibodies. But you're a new hire proposing, in effect, that the CEO's theory of the turnaround is wrong. How you're perceived will determine whether you ever get to test your idea.

The winning strategy is a **sequenced hybrid**: earn credibility inside the system first, then build a semi-autonomous lab framed as an extension of the CEO's vision, not a rejection of it.

---

## Reading the Situation

Impression management (Goffman; Jones & Pittman) is the process of shaping how others perceive you and your ideas. Consider how each audience would read "we need an independent innovation lab":

| Audience | What they may hear | Likely reaction |
|---|---|---|
| **CEO** | "Your strategy is wrong, and you hired me to tell you so." | Face threat. His "turn the ship around with what we have" narrative is part of his public identity, likely already told to the board. |
| **Middle managers** | "The new person thinks we're the problem." | Defensive resistance, passive non-cooperation, starving the lab of data and customers. |
| **Existing talent** | "Innovation happens elsewhere, by other people." | Disengagement. This confirms the CEO's fear that outsiders don't value his people. |
| **Board / customers** | Could read as bold, or as internal disagreement. | Depends entirely on how the CEO frames it. |

You currently have little **idiosyncrasy credit** (Hollander): the accumulated trust that lets someone deviate from group norms. New leaders who propose radical structural change before earning that credit are often seen as arrogant or naive, even when they're right.

---

## The Recommended Strategy: Three Phases

### Phase 1 (roughly days 1–90): Work *within* the system
**IM tactics: exemplification and sincere ingratiation**

- **Visibly honor the CEO's thesis.** Spend time with existing teams, product, sales, and churned customers. Be seen learning.
- **Find a quick win inside the current structure**, such as a retention fix, a stalled deal you help close, or a feature cut that frees capacity. This proves you can deliver in *their* world.
- **Document the friction.** Quietly note where existing procedures, approval chains, or metrics kill promising ideas. That becomes your evidence later, gathered from inside rather than asserted from outside.
- **Identify internal talent who are hungry to build.** They'll become the lab's core, which matters a great deal for Phase 2.

*Impression you're building:* "Respects what we have, gets results, understands our business."

### Phase 2: Reframe the lab in the CEO's language
**IM tactics: framing, credit-sharing, and letting the CEO own the idea**

Don't pitch "independence from the organization." Pitch **"a way to unleash our existing talent faster."**

| Instead of saying... | Say... |
|---|---|
| "An independent lab separate from the org" | "A dedicated team drawn from our best people, given room to move fast" |
| "The current systems are too slow" | "Let's protect a few bets from the processes built for our core product" |
| "We need outside talent" | "Mostly our people, plus a few specialists to complement them" |
| "This is how we'll innovate" | "This is how we'll accelerate your turnaround" |

A useful metaphor: **"The ship turns faster with a speedboat out front finding the channel."** It preserves his "turn the ship" narrative while justifying separation.

Present it as a **time-boxed pilot** with clear success metrics tied to his priorities (churn and new wins). Pilots are face-saving. If it works, he looks visionary. If it doesn't, nobody "lost."

### Phase 3: Build a semi-autonomous structure
**IM goal: avoid the "island of cool kids" perception that causes organizational rejection**

Design choices that are both good practice *and* good impression management:

- **Report directly to the CEO.** This gives protection from middle management and gives him ownership and visibility.
- **Staff it mostly with internal people**, which validates his belief in existing talent.
- **Build bridges to the core:** rotations, shared customer pilots, and demos open to everyone. Make the core business a co-owner of the wins.
- **Separate metrics and processes**, but shared purpose and branding. Separation should look like focus, not exile.
- **Publicly credit core teams** when lab ideas ship through them.

---

## Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Over-self-promotion:** positioning yourself as the savior invites people to watch you fail.
- **Intimidation:** "If we don't do this, we'll die" alienates people even when true.
- **Hollow ingratiation:** agreeing with the CEO and then quietly building your own thing will be discovered, and trust won't recover.
- **Waiting too long:** Phase 1 isn't indefinite. A company churning customers doesn't have unlimited time, and you were hired for outsized results. Aim to make the lab proposal within your first few months.

---

## A Sample Opening Line for the CEO Conversation

> "You were right that the talent here is strong. I've seen it firsthand over the past few months. What I've also seen is that our best people are working inside processes built to protect our core product, which makes it hard for them to take risks on new bets. I'd like to propose a small pilot: pull a handful of them into a dedicated team, report directly to you, and give them 6 months to move the needle on churn. Your turnaround, just moving faster."

---

## An Honest Caveat

Impression management works when it's backed by substance, not as a substitute for it. If, after earning credibility and framing it well, the CEO still insists that *everything* run through existing systems, you face a real strategic disagreement, not a perception problem. At that point you'll need to decide whether you can produce the results you were hired for under those constraints, and say so plainly.
