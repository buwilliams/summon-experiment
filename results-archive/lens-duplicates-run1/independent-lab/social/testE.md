## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I want to know which idea makes more people want to be your friend. Think about how each idea would look to kids at work and kids outside. Who gets a gold star, and who gets in trouble or says sorry? If each idea happened, would the boss, the helpers, the customers, and everybody's moms think it was cool? Where might wearing a cape or being loud matter more than doing chores? Who would sit together at lunch because of each idea? And is there a third idea where everybody gets to play and nobody feels left out?

## Response

# The Playground Version

You asked which idea makes more people want to be your friend. I'll answer that, and also point out where "popular" and "right" split apart, because for a new hire those can pull in different directions.

## Idea 1: The Secret Clubhouse (Independent Innovation Lab)

**Kids at work:** Most of them feel left out. A new kid shows up and builds a clubhouse with a "no grown-up rules" sign. The people who have kept the product running for years hear, "You're the reason we're losing." Some ambitious insiders want in, and the ones who don't get picked feel it.

**Kids outside:** They love it. Recruiters, the press, prospective customers, and conference audiences see the cape. "Insurance software company launches innovation lab" is an easy headline.

**Gold star:** You get one early, for demos, energy, and a story the board likes.

**Who says sorry:** Probably you, later. The classic failure mode (Xerox PARC is the famous example) is that the clubhouse invents great things and the main house refuses to adopt them. Engineers won't integrate the prototype, sales won't sell it, and support won't support it. Also, you'll have openly gone against the CEO's stated belief in your first months. If it stumbles, there is no one to share the blame.

**Lunch table:** You, the lab team, and maybe marketing sit together. Middle managers, core engineering, and operations sit at the other table, and they control what actually ships.

## Idea 2: Doing Chores in the Main House (Work Within Existing Systems)

**Kids at work:** Relieved and respected. Nobody gets called out.

**Kids outside:** They barely notice. No cape, no headline.

**Gold star:** The CEO gets one for being right, if it works.

**Who says sorry:** You again, if nothing changes. You were hired for outsized results, and the existing systems may be exactly what's producing the churn. The "antibodies" that smother new ideas are real.

**Lunch table:** You, the CEO, and the veterans. The restless insiders who hoped you'd shake things up quietly lose faith, and strong outside talent won't come.

## Cape vs. Chores

Your churning customers probably don't want a cape. Insurance carriers are risk-averse buyers. They leave over reliability, slow implementations, painful integrations, and ignored feature requests. **Retention is mostly a chores problem.**

Winning *new* business is partly a cape problem. Buyers need a reason to believe you're modern and moving. So you need both, and in that order. Capes without chores look like theater, and customers who are already leaving can spot theater.

## What the Moms Think

The moms are the board, employees' families, and the wider reputation. They want stability and progress without chaos. A lab that feels like a prelude to "the old team is being replaced" makes them nervous. So does a year of no visible change.

## The Third Idea: A Clubhouse With the Door Open

This is an **embedded lab**, or "ambidextrous" team. It keeps the lab's speed and fixes its adoption problem.

1. **Staff it mostly with insiders on rotation.** Borrow respected engineers, a product person, a support lead, and a salesperson for 3–6 month tours, plus a few outside hires. This literally honors the CEO's "existing talent" thesis, and everyone gets a turn.
2. **Point it at churn first.** Talk to recently lost and at-risk customers, then pick 2–3 problems that show up in the churn data. Early wins should be chores done fast.
3. **Give every project a co-owner from the core business.** The receiving team's leader shares credit and accountability, so they want the result to ship.
4. **Protect the process, not the membership.** Grant exemptions from the slowest procedures (approvals, release cadence, procurement), but keep a defined path back into the main product.
5. **Invite customers in.** A small advisory group of key accounts co-designs with you. Customers who are asked to help build something are less likely to leave while it's being built.
6. **Earn more independence over time.** After a couple of visible wins, you have the standing to argue for bigger, more separate bets, such as a new product line.

**How to pitch it to the CEO:** "You're right that the answer is in our people. I want to give some of them a place to work at a different speed, aimed at the customers we're losing." You aren't contradicting him. You're operationalizing him.

**Lunch table:** Mixed. Veterans, new hires, a customer or two, and the CEO drops by. That's the table that actually changes a company.

## The Honest Caveat

Being liked isn't the goal. Getting results is. But in a large organization, popularity is really a proxy for adoption. An idea nobody wants to sit with doesn't get shipped. The third idea is the one most likely to be both popular and right, and it lets you start in the CEO's direction while building toward yours.
