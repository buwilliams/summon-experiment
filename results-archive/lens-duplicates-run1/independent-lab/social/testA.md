## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

Neither is right as a blanket strategy, and framing it as a choice between the two may be the biggest risk to your success. The right answer depends on **what kind of problem is actually causing the churn and lost deals**, and on your position as a new leader whose first move would be contradicting the CEO who hired you.

## Both approaches have a real track record

**The case for an independent lab is real.** Research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly & Tushman) and disruptive innovation (Christensen) shows that large companies systematically kill new ideas. Budget processes, risk-averse approval chains, sales teams optimizing for existing customers, and legacy architecture all smother anything that doesn't look like the current business. Separation protects new ventures from those antibodies.

**But independent labs fail constantly.** Many corporate innovation labs become "innovation theater": cool demos, hackathons, and prototypes that never reach customers. Common failure modes:
- The lab builds things the core business can't absorb, sell, or support.
- The core organization resents the lab and quietly rejects its output.
- The lab loses its sponsor at the first budget crunch because it has no revenue.
- It's disconnected from customer and domain knowledge, which is critical in a regulated, complex industry like insurance.

## Diagnose before you prescribe

Customer churn and lost deals usually point to **core problems**: product gaps, poor implementation experience, slow release cycles, weak integrations, outdated UX, pricing, or service quality. An innovation lab doesn't fix those. If customers are leaving because onboarding takes 18 months or the platform can't integrate with modern tools, the fix lives inside the existing organization, and the CEO may be more right than you think.

Spend your first 60–90 days finding out:
- **Why are customers leaving?** Talk to churned customers directly, not just internal explanations.
- **Why are you losing deals, and to whom?** Are competitors doing the same thing better (a sustaining problem), or offering a fundamentally different model, such as cloud-native, AI-driven, or modular (a disruptive problem)?
- **Where does innovation die internally?** Is it talent, process, architecture, incentives, or leadership decisions?

## The likely answer is a portfolio

| Type of innovation | Example | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **Core fixes** | Close product gaps driving churn, speed up implementations | Inside, with your leadership driving process change |
| **Adjacent bets** | New modules, AI features, better integrations | Cross-functional "tiger teams" with protected time and executive air cover |
| **Transformational bets** | New business model, new market segment, AI-native platform | Separate unit, but tightly linked to leadership and with a clear path back into the core |

The key design principle from the ambidexterity research is **separate at the operating level, integrated at the senior level**. Give new ventures their own team, processes, and metrics, but keep a senior executive (ideally the CEO) personally connected so the core can eventually absorb the results.

## The political reality

You're new, and the CEO has stated a belief. Opening with "your approach is wrong, I need a separate lab" will likely cost you credibility before you've earned any. A better sequence:

1. **Earn trust by working within the system first.** Deliver one or two visible wins on the churn problem within 6 months. This also teaches you where the real blockers are.
2. **Document the specific ways the system blocks progress**, such as "this feature took 9 months due to X approval process." Evidence is more persuasive than theory.
3. **Propose a protected team as an experiment, not a rebuke.** Frame it as "extending your strategy," not replacing it: "We're fixing the core. To also capture the market shifting toward X, I'd like a small team with different rules for 12 months, measured on these outcomes."
4. **Tie everything to revenue and retention metrics** the CEO already cares about.

## Bottom line

Your instinct that the core organization will struggle to produce breakthrough innovation is probably correct. But the company's immediate crisis is churn and competitiveness, which is mostly a core-business problem. Win there first, use that credibility to carve out a protected space for bigger bets, and design that space so its output can flow back into the core.

If you share what you're hearing about why customers are leaving, I can help you work out which parts of the problem belong where.
