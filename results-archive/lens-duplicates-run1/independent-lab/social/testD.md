## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I want to know which approach wins approval and improves standing. Consider how each proposal would play inside and outside the company. Who gains status or credit, and who risks losing face or taking the blame? If each proposal went ahead, what would it signal to the board, employees, customers, and the public? Where might looking decisive or reassuring an audience matter more than operational results? What alliances and reputations would each proposal strengthen or threaten? And is there a third approach that wins greater support while letting the important players save face?

## Response

# Which Strategy Wins?

**Neither, as you've framed them.** Pitching an independent lab as the alternative to the CEO's view forces him to either reverse himself publicly or reject you. The version that wins is a **protected innovation unit that sits inside the company, is framed as the sharp end of the CEO's turnaround, and has the independence it needs.** This isn't just a political compromise. The best research on incumbent innovation (O'Reilly & Tushman's "ambidextrous organization") finds that *structural separation plus senior-level integration* outperforms both pure options.

## Why the Binary Is a Trap for You

The CEO has already staked out a position: "turn the ship around with existing systems, procedures, and talent." That's not just strategy. It's a public commitment to his board and a message of reassurance to his executives. If you advocate for independence as a rejection of that:

- **He loses face** if he agrees ("my new hire told me my plan won't work").
- **You lose standing** if he refuses. You become the outsider who didn't understand the culture, and you've spent your early political capital on a loss.
- **Everyone else learns** that the innovation leader and the CEO aren't aligned, which invites executives to wait you out.

## Option A: Work Within Existing Systems

| Audience | How it plays |
|---|---|
| **CEO** | Validates his thesis. He gets credit for belief in his people. |
| **Business unit heads / senior execs** | Safe for them. No threat to their budgets or headcount, and they keep control. Quietly, they can slow-walk anything inconvenient. |
| **Middle managers** | Relief. No rival organization, no judgment of their teams. |
| **Employees** | Reassuring at first ("they believe in us"). Cynicism later if nothing changes, since they've seen initiatives come and go. |
| **Board** | Reads as steady, low-risk, cheap. But if churn continues, they'll ask why the turnaround looks like the status quo. |
| **Customers** | Largely invisible. Customers don't care about org charts, only whether the product improves. |
| **Public / market / recruits** | No story to tell. Doesn't change the perception of a legacy vendor. |

**Blame dynamics:** If it succeeds, credit flows to the CEO and existing leaders. If it fails, blame flows to *you*. You were hired to produce outsized results and the existing system absorbed you. This is the worst risk-reward profile for your career: limited upside, concentrated downside.

**Operational reality:** Large organizations have an immune system. Quarterly roadmaps, legacy architecture, compliance processes, and incentive structures reward protecting existing revenue. Breakthrough work tends to get deprioritized, diluted, or killed in review cycles.

## Option B: Independent Innovation Lab

| Audience | How it plays |
|---|---|
| **CEO** | Implicit rebuke of his stated approach, unless he can reframe it as his idea. |
| **Senior execs** | Threat. It competes for budget, talent, and the CEO's attention, and it implies they couldn't innovate. Expect passive resistance, especially at handoff time. |
| **Middle managers / core employees** | "The cool kids get the fun work, we keep the lights on." Resentment and talent drain from the core. |
| **Board** | Reads as decisive and bold, and makes a good slide. Also reads as new cost with uncertain return, and possibly as a sign the CEO has lost faith in his org. |
| **Customers** | Can be a strong signal ("they're investing in the future") *if* publicized with concrete outcomes. Can look like theater if nothing ships. |
| **Public / recruits** | Best external story. Helps recruit talent that wouldn't otherwise join a legacy insurance software vendor. |

**Blame dynamics:** You get disproportionate credit for wins, which is exactly why others may want it to fail. If it fails, it fails visibly, with your name on it. Labs also have a well-known failure mode: they produce great prototypes that the core business never adopts. When that happens, the core blames the lab for being unrealistic, and the lab blames the core for being hidebound. Both lose, but the lab is easier to shut down.

## Where Optics Outweigh Results, and Where They Don't

**Optics matter more:**
- **Board meetings in the next 6–12 months.** A turnaround needs a visible, decisive move. "We're working harder within existing processes" doesn't read as a response to churn.
- **Recruiting.** A branded, protected unit attracts people your current org can't.
- **Sales conversations with at-risk customers.** A credible "here's what we're building and when" can buy renewal time.
- **Internal morale.** Employees need to believe change is real *and* that they're part of it.

**Optics can't save you:**
- **Churn.** Customers leave over product gaps, service, integration pain, and price. A lab announcement without shipped improvements becomes evidence of empty promises.
- **Your own reputation over 18–24 months.** You'll be judged on outcomes, not structure. Choose the structure that maximizes the odds of shipping something customers pay for.

The political aim is to *look decisive early* while *setting up real results later*, not to trade one for the other.

## Alliances and Reputations

**Option A** strengthens the incumbent executive coalition and the CEO's consistency narrative. It weakens your mandate and your appeal to outside talent.

**Option B** strengthens your personal brand, the board's perception of boldness, and your external network. It threatens business unit heads, likely the CTO or head of product (whose territory you'd encroach on), and HR/finance norms.

**Key swing players to win early:**
- **Head of Product / CTO.** If they see you as a rival, they can starve any handoff.
- **Head of Sales / Customer Success.** They feel churn most acutely and can be your strongest ally if you frame innovation around retaining their accounts.
- **CFO.** You'll need them to accept a different funding model (stage-gated, not annual budget).
- **A respected long-tenured insider.** Their involvement signals this isn't an outsider takeover.

## The Third Approach: A Protected Unit Framed as the CEO's Plan

### The Pitch

Don't say "we need independence from the organization." Say:

> "Your turnaround is right to bet on our people and our assets: our customer base, our domain knowledge, our data. My job is to be the sharp end of that. I want to pull some of our best people into a small, protected team with the speed and freedom of a startup, focused on the problems driving churn, and feed what works back into the core."

This lets the CEO keep his thesis ("existing talent") while getting the structural protection you need.

### The Design

1. **Staffed mostly by internal people, plus a few external hires.** This honors "existing talent," gives employees a path in, and neutralizes the "cool kids" resentment. Rotations make it feel open.
2. **Independent where it matters, integrated where it counts.** Separate from:
   - normal budgeting cycles
   - release processes
   - roadmap approval
   - some procurement and tooling rules

   Tied into:
   - the customer base
   - the sales team
   - the core platform
3. **Reports to the CEO (or you, reporting to the CEO).** Senior sponsorship is the single strongest predictor of whether these units survive.
4. **Aimed at churn, not blue-sky ideas.** Pick 2–3 problems tied directly to lost or at-risk revenue. Co-develop with actual customers. This makes Sales and Customer Success allies and gives the board metrics it cares about.
5. **Stage-gated funding with 90-day milestones.** This keeps the CFO comfortable and forces early visible wins.
6. **Built-in graduation paths.** Each project has a named core business owner from the start, with shared credit and shared metrics. This is how you avoid the "lab builds, core rejects" trap, and it gives business unit heads a reason to want you to succeed.
7. **Deliberate credit distribution.**
   - The CEO announces it and gets the "bold turnaround" narrative.
   - Business unit heads get credit when their teams ship graduated products.
   - Engineers who rotate in get visible recognition.
   - You get credit as the orchestrator, which ages better than being the lone hero.

### Signals It Sends

- **Board:** Decisive, contained risk, measurable, aligned leadership.
- **Employees:** "We're betting on you, and you can be part of the new thing."
- **Customers:** "We heard you, and here's a team co-building fixes with you."
- **Public / recruits:** A real innovation story without admitting the core is broken.

### Who Saves Face

- **The CEO** is consistent with his stated thesis and looks bold.
- **Business unit heads** are partners and beneficiaries, not failures being routed around.
- **You** get the protection you believed was necessary without having to win an argument against your new boss.

## Your Next 60–90 Days

1. **Listen before proposing.** Do one-on-ones with the executive team, top customers (especially churned or at-risk ones), and frontline engineers. You'll learn where the real blockers are and collect allies. Leaders who show up with the answer tend to fare poorly with long-tenured teams.
2. **Pre-wire the proposal.** Test it privately with the CFO, the head of product, and the head of sales before the CEO sees a formal version. Incorporate their fingerprints so they co-own it.
3. **Get one quick win.** Find a churn-driving problem you can visibly improve within a quarter using a small team. That earns the credibility to ask for more structure.
4. **Let the CEO name it.** If he feels ownership of the concept, he'll defend it when budget season or executive resistance arrives.

**Bottom line:** Your instinct that innovation needs protection from the core is well-founded. But framing that as a rejection of the CEO's approach is the fastest way to lose the argument. Build the protection *into* his narrative, distribute credit widely, and tie everything to the churn problem everyone already agrees is real.
