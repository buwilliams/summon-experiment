## Prompt

Use profit maximization to answer the following.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

# Which Innovation Strategy Maximizes Profit?

**Short answer:** Neither pure strategy is right. The profit-maximizing move is a **small, semi-independent team aimed first at the core business's churn problem**, with a clearly defined path to a more independent lab if the evidence supports it. Here's the reasoning.

---

## 1. Start With the Profit Equation

Your company's problem can be expressed as:

**Profit = (Retained Revenue + New Revenue) − (Cost to Serve + Cost of Innovation)**

Right now two terms are moving the wrong way: retained revenue is falling (churn) and new revenue is stalling (weak win rate). Any strategy should be judged by **how much, how fast, and how reliably** it improves those terms relative to its cost.

## 2. Compare the Two Strategies

| Factor | CEO: Work Within Existing Systems | You: Independent Innovation Lab |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed to cash impact** | Fast. Improvements reach existing customers immediately. | Slow. New products typically take 18 to 36 months to generate meaningful revenue. |
| **Impact on churn** | Direct. Fixes the product customers are leaving. | Indirect or none, unless the lab targets retention. |
| **Impact on new wins** | Limited if the core product is fundamentally uncompetitive. | Potentially high if the market needs something the core can't build. |
| **Cost** | Low incremental cost, since it uses existing talent. | High: new hires, separate infrastructure, and duplicated functions. |
| **Probability of success** | Moderate for incremental gains; low for breakthroughs, because processes tend to kill novel ideas. | Low per bet. Many corporate labs fail to transfer innovations back to the core. |
| **Political risk to you** | Low | High. You'd be contradicting the CEO who just hired you. |

## 3. The Key Insight: Churn Is the Most Expensive Problem

In B2B software, retaining a customer is usually far cheaper than acquiring one, and profits compound through renewals. A rough illustration:

- Suppose the company has **$500M ARR** and **15% annual churn**. That's **$75M lost per year**.
- Cutting churn by 5 points preserves **about $25M per year**, recurring, at relatively low cost.
- A new lab product might plausibly reach $10–20M ARR in three years, *if* it succeeds.

On expected value and time-to-cash, **fixing retention usually beats launching new ventures**. An independent lab that ignores the core bleeding would be optimizing the smaller term in the equation.

## 4. But the CEO's Approach Has a Real Flaw

"Existing systems, procedures, and talent" produced the current situation. If churn stems from structural issues, incremental work inside the same system won't fix it. Examples include legacy architecture, slow release cycles, or a product that doesn't fit how insurers now buy (cloud-native, API-first, modular). Clayton Christensen's research shows incumbents consistently fail when an innovation requires a different cost structure, business model, or speed than the core allows.

**So the right answer depends on why customers are leaving.**

| If customers churn because of... | Profit-maximizing approach |
|---|---|
| Bugs, poor service, missing features, pricing | **Work within the core** (CEO is right) |
| Slow delivery, poor UX, integration pain | **Semi-autonomous team** with process exemptions |
| Fundamentally outdated platform or business model | **Independent lab** (you're right) |

## 5. Recommended Strategy: A "Tethered Lab"

1. **Diagnose first (first 30–60 days).** Interview churned and at-risk customers and lost prospects. Quantify the revenue attached to each churn reason. This data resolves the debate with the CEO objectively.

2. **Form a small, protected team.** Staff it with 6 to 12 of the best people, drawn partly from inside the company. They get exemptions from standard processes (approval chains, release cycles, procurement) but stay connected to real customers.

3. **Aim its first mission at retention.** Pick a high-value churn driver and deliver a measurable win within 6 to 9 months. This proves the model, creates cash impact, and earns credibility.

4. **Allocate the portfolio deliberately.** A common split is roughly 70% core improvement, 20% adjacent innovation, and 10% transformational bets. Grow the transformational share only as results justify it.

5. **Set profit-based metrics and stage-gates.** Measure net revenue retention, win rate, and time-to-release. Fund further independence in stages based on evidence, not upfront.

6. **Build the path back to the core now.** Define how successful innovations will be adopted by sales, support, and engineering. Most corporate labs fail at this handoff, not at invention.

## 6. How to Handle the CEO

Don't frame this as "you're wrong, we need a separate lab." Instead:

> "I agree we should use our existing talent and customers. Let me run a small, focused team with some process freedom, aimed at our biggest churn driver. If it works, we scale it. If the diagnosis shows a deeper platform problem, we'll have the data to decide on a larger investment."

This lowers your political risk, aligns with his view, and still gives you the structural independence innovation needs.

---

**Bottom line:** The CEO is right that the fastest profit comes from the existing business. You're right that the existing system probably can't produce breakthrough results on its own. The profit-maximizing path is a **small, autonomous team focused first on retention**, expanded into a truly independent lab only if the evidence shows the core platform itself is the problem.
