# independent-lab/economic/testA

Model: claude-opus-5-5 · stop_reason: end_turn · 2026-09-24T11:34:01.816682+00:00

## Prompt

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

## Response

Neither strategy is right on its own, and framing it as a choice between them may be the biggest risk. The right structure depends on what kind of problem you're solving. Right now, you may not know that yet.

## The case for your instinct (independent lab)

You're drawing on well-supported ideas. Large organizations tend to reject anything that doesn't fit existing processes, metrics, and incentives. Christensen's work on disruption and O'Reilly and Tushman's research on "ambidextrous organizations" both show that genuinely new business models usually need protection from the core's planning cycles, approval chains, and quarterly pressures. If your company needs a fundamentally different product or business model, a lab embedded in the old machinery will likely get slowed down, starved of resources, or bent back into the old shape.

## The case for the CEO's view (work within the system)

Independent innovation labs also have a poor track record. Common failure modes:

- **Innovation theater.** The lab produces demos and prototypes that never ship.
- **No path to scale.** The lab builds something, but the core org (sales, implementation, support) won't adopt or sell it.
- **Resentment.** The core sees the lab as a privileged team that gets the fun work while they keep the lights on.
- **Early death.** Labs are easy to cut in the first bad quarter, especially without deep executive and business-unit buy-in.

## The critical question: what's actually causing the churn?

Churning customers and losing deals is often a **core execution problem**, not a disruptive innovation problem. Before choosing a structure, find out why customers are leaving:

- Is the product outdated (legacy architecture, poor UX, no cloud or API capabilities)?
- Is it slow implementation, bad support, or pricing?
- Are competitors offering a fundamentally different model (modern SaaS platforms, embedded AI, faster configuration)?
- Are you losing deals on features, price, speed, or trust?

If the answer is "implementations take 18 months and support is terrible," a lab won't help. That's a core fix, and the CEO is right. If the answer is "insurers are moving to modern platforms and ours can't get there incrementally," you need something protected from the core, and you're right. Most likely it's a mix.

## A more workable approach: portfolio plus bridges

Govindarajan and Trimble's research ("The Other Side of Innovation") suggests the most successful model is a **dedicated team that deliberately partners with the core**, rather than full independence or full integration.

1. **Diagnose first (first 30–60 days).** Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, sales, and support. Build a fact base on why you're losing. This also gives you credibility and allies.
2. **Deliver early wins inside the core.** Pick one or two visible problems (a churn driver, a stalled deal pattern) and fix them with existing teams. This proves you can work within the system and earns you political capital.
3. **Build a small, protected team for the bets that need it.** Give it different metrics (learning, customer validation, speed) rather than revenue in year one, plus its own decision rights and a direct line to the CEO.
4. **Design the bridges from day one.** Decide how lab work will reach customers: who sells it, who implements it, how it connects to the existing platform, and which core leaders have a stake in its success. Labs die when this is an afterthought.

## On navigating the CEO

You're new, and your first major recommendation contradicts the person who hired you. Rather than arguing "lab vs. no lab," reframe it: *"You're right that we have to fix the core, and I'll show results there quickly. Some of what's causing churn may require things our current processes can't produce fast enough. Let me bring you data on which is which."* That respects his view, buys you time to gather evidence, and positions a protected team as a response to findings rather than an ideology.

If it helps, I can go deeper on any piece: structuring the churn diagnosis, designing lab governance, or making the pitch to the CEO.
