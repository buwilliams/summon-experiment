# The Two Toys (and a Swing Set)

## Toy #1: The Shiny Robot (VP of Product's idea)

**What it does:** It lights up, talks, and does tricks. It has AI and pretty charts, and it looks amazing in the store window.

**Who gets to play with it:** Sales plays with it the most, because it makes demos exciting. Product and engineering get to build it, which is fun. Customers who are already set up and happy get new tricks.

**Happy or sad?**
- 😀 Product, engineering, sales, marketing
- 😟 Services, because every new trick is another thing to install and explain
- 😢 **Who cries:** The customer who's been stuck in implementation for nine months and now gets an email about a new AI feature they can't reach. It's like getting a new robot when you still haven't opened the box your last toy came in.

**Boring part:** Not much. That's the danger. It's the idea everyone wants to hear about at dinner, which is why it's easy to pick even if it's the wrong one.

**Snacks?** Yes. Launch parties, a conference booth, maybe cake.

## Toy #2: The Really Good Instruction Booklet (VP of Services' idea)

**What it does:** It helps people put their toys together faster and actually play with them. Implementations get shorter, support gets better, and customers reach the part where the software pays off.

**Who gets to play with it:** Customers, especially the ones who are struggling right now. Services and support get to stop being yelled at.

**Happy or sad?**
- 😀 Customers, services, support, and eventually finance (kept customers are cheap customers)
- 😟 Product, because the roadmap slows down. Sales, because "we onboard faster now" isn't a flashy demo.
- 😢 **Who cries:** Maybe a salesperson who loses a deal to a competitor with a cooler AI demo.

**Boring part:** Very. "We cut data-mapping time from six weeks to three" makes people at dinner check their phones. But boring things are often what's broken.

**Snacks?** Not really. Maybe a pizza when the first 90-day go-live happens.

## So which toy is right?

Right now, **nobody knows. Both VPs are guessing**, and each is guessing in favor of their own department. The good news is you can find out, because your churned customers left clues. Look at the accounts that left in the last 18 months and ask:

1. **Did they ever finish implementation?** How long did it take compared to customers who stayed?
2. **Did they actually use the product?** Look at logins, claims processed, and features adopted.
3. **Where did they go?** To a competitor with better AI, or back to spreadsheets or a legacy system?
4. **What did they say when they left?** Check exit notes, support tickets from their last six months, and renewal conversations.

**How to read the clues:**
- If churners **never got fully live or barely used the product**, the Instruction Booklet wins. A cooler robot won't help people who can't open the box.
- If churners were **fully live, used it heavily, and left for a competitor's features**, the Shiny Robot wins.
- If it's **mixed**, which is common, look at which group is bigger and which one is growing.

My hunch, which the data needs to confirm: with regional carriers, long implementations and never reaching full value are very often the real problem. Competitor features then become the reason customers give when they leave, because "your AI is better" is easier to say than "we never got this working." Watch for that in exit interviews.

## Toy #3: The Swing Set for the Whole Office

Here's the idea that gets everyone playing together: **make "time to first value" the shared game.**

- **One scoreboard for everyone:** how many days from contract signature until a customer is live and getting real value, whatever you define that as, like claims flowing through or a measurable cycle-time drop. Product, services, and CS all get measured on it.
- **Product builds toys that help onboarding:** implementation templates for common carrier setups, data-migration tools, configuration wizards. That's real product work, and engineers can own it.
- **Analytics become a "look how much value you're getting" dashboard.** Build the analytics customers crave, but point them first at showing carriers their own results, like faster cycle times and adjuster productivity. That gives Product its shiny feature, makes Services' job easier, and hands CS proof of value at renewal time.
- **Start with a joint churn review:** both VPs in the same room with the churned-account data from above. It's the cheapest, fastest step, and it stops the argument from being about opinions.

**Who cries?** Hopefully nobody. Product still ships cool things, Services gets its problem taken seriously, sales gets a new story ("live in 90 days, with dashboards proving ROI"), and customers stay.

**Snacks?** Put a bell in the office and ring it every time a customer goes live faster than the old average. Then bring donuts.

**Assistant:**

The fun version got us to a real answer. Here's the plan.

## Step 1: Find out why customers actually left (first 30 days)

Get both VPs, you, and someone from finance or sales ops together to look at every account that churned in the last 18 months. For each one, pull:

- **How long implementation took**, and whether it ever fully finished
- **How much they used the product**: logins, claims processed, features adopted
- **Where they went**: a competitor, a legacy system, or spreadsheets
- **What they said**: exit notes, support tickets from their last six months, renewal conversations

Then compare them to customers who stayed. The goal is to replace two opinions with one set of facts.

## Step 2: Read the results honestly

- **Churners mostly never got fully live or barely used the product:** Prioritize fixing onboarding and support.
- **Churners were healthy, heavy users who left for competitor features:** Prioritize the roadmap.
- **Mixed:** Size each group and fix the bigger one first, while starting on the other.

Agree on these rules before you look at the data, so nobody moves the goalposts afterward.

## Step 3: Make one shared goal

Pick a single metric everyone owns: **time from contract signature to first real value**. Define "value" concretely, like claims flowing through the system or a measurable cycle-time improvement. Product, services, and CS all get measured on it.

## Step 4: Point product work at the problem

Whatever the data says, some roadmap time should go to features that make implementation faster:

- Templates for common regional-carrier setups
- Data-migration and configuration tools
- A value dashboard that shows each customer their own results

That last one lets Product build the analytics it wants while giving CS proof of value at renewal time.

## Step 5: Protect the customers you have right now

While all this is happening, find current customers who look like past churners (stuck in implementation, low usage, lots of tickets) and give them extra attention now. Saving even a few accounts this quarter buys credibility for the bigger changes.

## Step 6: Celebrate progress

Every time a customer goes live faster than the old average, ring the bell. 🔔🍩

**The short version:** don't pick a side yet. Spend a month finding out who's right, get everyone chasing the same goal, and make the robot and the booklet work together.
