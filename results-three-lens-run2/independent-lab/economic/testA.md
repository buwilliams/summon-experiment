## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Neither strategy is right as stated. The more useful question is what's actually causing the churn and lost deals, because that determines where innovation needs to happen.

## Why the CEO's approach alone is risky

If the existing systems, procedures, and talent could produce outsized results, they probably would be already. Large organizations tend to optimize for predictability and existing customers. New ideas get judged by the core business's metrics, compete for its resources, and get slowed by its approval processes. Innovation efforts that live entirely inside the machine usually get absorbed or starved.

## Why your approach alone is also risky

Independent innovation labs have a poor track record, especially in enterprise software:

- **They rarely fix churn.** Customers usually leave because of problems in the core product: reliability, implementation pain, missing features, service, pricing. A lab building something new doesn't help the customer about to leave next quarter.
- **They trigger organizational antibodies.** A separate, well-funded group that implicitly says "the rest of you can't innovate" breeds resentment. When the lab needs engineering, sales, compliance, or customer access, it finds none.
- **They struggle to scale.** Many labs produce promising prototypes that die in the handoff back to the core business.
- **It puts you in direct opposition to the CEO** in your first months, before you've earned credibility. That is a hard position to lead from.

## What tends to work: connected autonomy

Research on organizational ambidexterity (O'Reilly and Tushman's work is a good starting point) suggests the most successful model separates *how* the innovation team operates while tightly linking it to the core business:

- **A small team with real autonomy** over process, tools, hiring, and speed, exempt from the normal approval layers
- **Senior-level integration.** You or the CEO actively protect the team and connect it to the core's resources, customers, and distribution
- **Work aimed at real business problems**, not blue-sky exploration
- **A defined path to scale**, deciding upfront how successful work gets adopted by the core product or sales teams
- **Internal talent in the mix.** Include respected insiders so the team has credibility and allies, not just outside hires

## A practical sequence

1. **Diagnose before prescribing (first 30–60 days).** Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, sales reps, and support staff. Find out whether you're losing on product, price, service, speed, or a market shift like cloud-native or AI-driven competitors.
2. **Split the problem.** Churn is likely a core-business problem, fixable largely within existing structures, which supports the CEO's view. Winning new business may require offerings or models the current organization can't easily produce, which is where protected autonomy matters.
3. **Deliver an early win the CEO's way.** Find one or two churn drivers you can visibly improve by working across existing teams. This builds trust and teaches you how the organization really works.
4. **Then propose a protected team** for the specific opportunity your diagnosis uncovered, framed as extending the CEO's strategy rather than contradicting it: "We turn the ship with our existing people *and* create a small, fast team to go after X."

## The bottom line

The CEO is probably right that the ship turns through the core business. You're probably right that outsized results need some protection from how the core business operates. The job is to combine the two, and to earn the standing to do it before asking for it.

If you share what you know so far about why customers are leaving or deals are being lost, I can help you think through where each approach fits.

## Turn 2

**User:** How do I get honest answers from churned customers and lost prospects when they've already walked away from us?

**Assistant:**

Most churned customers and lost prospects are willing to talk. They've just never been asked in a way that feels safe and worthwhile. The key is removing the reasons people give polite, vague answers.

## Why you usually don't get the truth

- **They don't want an awkward conversation or a sales pitch.** They assume the call is a win-back attempt.
- **They're being polite.** "Price" or "budget" is the socially easy answer. It often masks "we didn't see enough value to justify the price" or "we didn't trust you to deliver."
- **The wrong person is asking.** They won't criticize the account rep to the account rep, or your product to the people who built it.
- **Internal records are unreliable.** Loss reasons logged by sales reps tend to blame price, features, or timing rather than anything the rep controlled.

## Who should ask

- **You, as the new innovation leader.** "I'm new, I've been asked to figure out what we need to change, and I'd value your candor" is disarming. It signals the conversation matters and that you have no stake in defending past decisions.
- **A neutral third party** for more candor or scale. Win/loss firms (Clozd, Primary Intelligence, and others) do this professionally, and people are often franker with an outsider. This is worth considering for lost prospects especially.
- **Not the account team or salesperson** who owned the relationship.

## How to ask

**The outreach:**
- Keep it short and personal, from you rather than a marketing system.
- State explicitly that this is not a sales call and you won't try to win them back.
- Ask for 20–30 minutes and say how you'll use what you learn.
- Offer something: a gift card, a charity donation, or a summary of what you learned across interviews. Senior people often value the last one most.

**The format:**
- Phone or video, not surveys. Surveys capture the polite answer; conversations let you follow up.
- Talk to multiple roles where you can. Insurance software decisions involve several stakeholders (IT, underwriting, claims, operations, finance, procurement), and the "why" often differs by seat. The executive may cite strategy while the users reveal it was implementation pain.
- Record (with permission) or bring a note-taker so you can stay present.

## What to ask

Focus on the story of the decision, not their opinions about your product. Past behavior is more reliable than general assessments.

- "Take me back to when you first started thinking about making a change. What was happening?"
- "Who was involved in the decision? What did each of them care about?"
- "What alternatives did you look at, and how did you compare them?"
- "What was the moment you knew you'd leave us / go with someone else?"
- "What did the winner do or show you that we didn't?"
- "If you could have changed one thing about us, would it have changed the outcome?"
- "What almost made you stay / choose us?"

**Dig past the first answer.** When you hear "price," ask "Price compared to what?" or "If we'd matched their price, would you have chosen us?" When you hear "features," ask which ones and what they would have enabled. The first answer is rarely the real one; the third "why" often is.

**Listen more than you talk.** Don't explain, defend, or correct, even if they're factually wrong about your product. Their perception is the data. Silence after an answer often prompts the most honest follow-up.

## Making it worthwhile

- **Aim for 15–25 conversations** across churned customers and lost deals before drawing conclusions. Patterns usually emerge around 10–12.
- **Separate churn from lost deals.** They often have different causes. Churn tends to reflect delivery and service; lost deals tend to reflect positioning, product gaps, or the sales process.
- **Triangulate** with support tickets, implementation timelines, product usage data, and conversations with your own frontline staff. Customers tell you *why*; data tells you *how widespread*.
- **Close the loop.** Send participants a brief thank-you and, later, a note on what you're changing. It builds goodwill, and some of these accounts come back.

## A bonus for your broader goal

These interviews do double duty. Beyond diagnosing the problem, they give you something no internal debate can: direct customer evidence. When you later propose changes to the CEO, whether fixes in the core business or a protected innovation team, verbatim quotes from customers who left will carry far more weight than your opinion or anyone else's.

## Turn 3

**User:** How do I handle the sales and account teams who might feel threatened or defensive about me contacting their former customers?

**Assistant:**

Their concern is reasonable. From their side, a new executive calling their lost accounts could expose mistakes, undermine relationships they're still nurturing, or become ammunition in performance reviews. If you address those fears directly and give them a real stake in the work, many of them will become your best allies.

## Understand what they're actually worried about

- **Blame.** "This will be used to show I lost the deal."
- **Relationship damage.** "I'm still in touch with that buyer and hoping to win them back."
- **Loss of control.** "Someone outside sales is talking to my market."
- **Being bypassed.** "Why didn't anyone ask me? I know why we lost."

Each has a specific remedy.

## Start with sales leadership, not the reps

- Meet the head of sales and head of account management first. Explain the goal, ask for their input, and invite them to co-sponsor the effort.
- Be explicit that this is about understanding systemic causes, not evaluating individuals.
- Ideally, the outreach plan is announced to their teams by their own leaders, not by you.

If sales leadership sees this as their initiative too, most of the resistance below won't materialize.

## Interview the teams before the customers

This is the most important step. Talk to the account managers and salespeople first:

- "Why do you think we're losing customers and deals?"
- "What do customers complain about that never gets fixed?"
- "What do you wish the company understood?"

This does three things. It shows respect for their expertise. It gives you hypotheses to test. And frontline teams usually have strong opinions about product, pricing, and delivery problems they've been unable to escalate. Many will welcome someone finally asking, and they'll see the customer interviews as a way to prove what they've been saying.

## Set clear ground rules and put them in writing

- **Findings are reported as patterns, not attributed to individuals.** No rep names in the readout.
- **Nothing feeds into performance reviews or compensation.** Get sales leadership to commit to this publicly.
- **Account owners get a say.** Share the list of accounts you plan to contact and let them flag ones to exclude: active win-back efforts, disputes, legal issues, or fragile relationships. Respect those flags, while asking for a reason so exclusions don't become a way to hide the most revealing losses.
- **No selling.** You won't make promises, discuss pricing, or pitch anything. Any genuine opportunity that surfaces gets routed back to the account owner.
- **Advance notice.** Tell the owner before you contact one of their former accounts.

## Give them something they value

- **Let them shape the questions.** Ask what they'd most want to know from lost customers.
- **Invite some to listen in** on calls where they weren't the account owner, which avoids chilling candor.
- **Share findings with them first,** before the executive readout. It lets them process the results and prevents them from being surprised in front of leadership.
- **Frame the output as a sales tool.** Competitive intelligence, objections to prepare for, and evidence to push for product fixes all help them hit their numbers.
- **Send win-back opportunities their way.** If a former customer signals openness to returning, the account owner gets the lead and the credit.

## Be honest about the hard case

Sometimes interviews will reveal that a specific person handled an account badly. Decide in advance how you'll handle it:

- Individual issues go privately to the relevant manager, not into the broader report.
- In most cases, a single rep's mistake points to a systemic gap (training, handoffs, incentives, or support), and that's what gets reported.

Being upfront that this could happen, and how you'll handle it, builds more trust than promising it won't.

## Get the CEO to set the frame

A brief message from the CEO saying this effort is about learning and fixing the company, not assigning blame, will carry more weight than anything you say. It also signals that cooperation is expected.

## The bigger opportunity

How you run this will be the organization's first real impression of you. Handled well, it shows you can work with the existing organization rather than around it, which is exactly the credibility you'll need later when you propose bigger changes, including any protected innovation team. The sales and account teams can either be the people who say "that new innovation leader doesn't understand our business" or the ones who say "finally, someone's listening." This process largely decides which.

## Turn 4

**User:** How do I get the CEO on board with this interview effort, given he might see it as me questioning his "work within existing systems" approach?

**Assistant:**

The good news is that this effort doesn't actually challenge his approach. Talking to customers is diagnostic work done with existing people and systems. It tests *how* to turn the ship, not *whether* his strategy is right. If you frame it that way, and genuinely mean it, most of the tension disappears.

## Check your own intent first

If part of you sees these interviews as a way to build the case for your innovation lab, the CEO may sense it, and he'd be right to be wary. Go in genuinely open to the possibility that the findings support his view. They may well show that churn comes from fixable problems in the core business. Being truly open is what makes the pitch credible, and it protects the integrity of the research.

## Understand what he's worried about

Before pitching, learn what's behind his "existing systems" stance. It might be:

- **Past failures,** such as an innovation initiative that burned money and demoralized people
- **Cost and focus,** especially if the company is under financial pressure
- **Loyalty to his team** and a belief that they've been underused rather than inadequate
- **Board expectations** or a turnaround plan he's already committed to

Ask him directly: "What would success look like for me in the first six months?" and "What's been tried before?" His answers tell you how to frame everything else.

## Frame it as serving his strategy

Position the interviews as the way to make his approach work:

- **"To turn the ship with our existing teams, we need to aim them at the right problems."** The interviews tell you where to point the resources you already have.
- **"This is how I get up to speed on the business."** As a new leader, wanting to hear from customers is natural and hard to object to.
- **"It gives the whole organization shared facts."** Customer evidence settles internal debates and gets teams aligned, which a turnaround needs.

Avoid language that implies judgment, like "figuring out what's broken" or "finding out why our approach isn't working."

## Make it easy to say yes

- **Small and fast.** Propose 15–25 conversations over 6–8 weeks, not a major program.
- **Cheap.** Mostly your own time. If you want a third-party firm, present it as an option, not a requirement.
- **Low risk.** Walk him through the safeguards: no selling, account owner input, sales leadership co-sponsorship, and careful handling of sensitive accounts.
- **Concrete output.** Promise a clear readout of top churn and loss drivers with recommendations, many of which can likely be acted on by existing teams.

## Give him ownership

- **Ask for his hypotheses.** "Why do you think we're losing customers?" Then include questions that test his views. If the findings confirm them, he gets validation; if not, he's more likely to accept evidence he helped design.
- **Invite him to join two or three calls.** Hearing a former customer directly is powerful and makes the findings his, not yours. Many CEOs rarely get this exposure.
- **Offer him the first look.** Commit to sharing findings with him privately before anyone else, so he's never surprised in front of his team or board.
- **Let him set the frame to the organization,** as discussed with the sales teams. A message from him makes it his initiative.

## Anticipate his objections

- **"We already know why customers leave."** "You may be right. This lets us confirm it with evidence and quantify which issues matter most, so we fix the right things first."
- **"This could upset customers or the sales team."** Walk through the safeguards and sales leadership's involvement.
- **"I don't want bad news spreading."** Agree on how findings will be shared and with whom before you start.
- **"I hired you to deliver results, not do research."** "This is the fastest way to results. It keeps us from spending months fixing the wrong things." Offer to pair it with an early quick win.

## A sample pitch

"I want to make sure our teams are focused on the problems that matter most to customers. Over the next six to eight weeks, I'd like to talk with about twenty customers we've lost and prospects who chose competitors, working closely with sales leadership. I'd value your view on why you think we're losing, so we can test it directly, and I'd love for you to join a couple of the calls. You'll see the findings first, and I'll come back with specific recommendations for what our teams can act on."

## If he says no or hesitates

Don't force it. Offer a smaller version: five or six conversations as a pilot, or starting with internal data and frontline staff interviews. A modest start that goes well usually earns permission to expand.

## The longer game

This conversation is about more than the interviews. It's your chance to show the CEO that you're working with him, not around him. If the findings later point toward needing a protected team for certain opportunities, that recommendation will come from someone he trusts, backed by evidence he helped gather. That's a far stronger position than arguing for a lab on principle in your first months.
