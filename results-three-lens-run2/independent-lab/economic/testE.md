## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I want to know which idea gets you more stuff, like a lemonade stand. Follow the piggy bank through each idea. How many cookies would it get you, and how much allowance would you spend first? If each idea happened, how long until the treats come, and could a big dog eat them all? Where could you keep spending money and still not get more candy? Who would hold the piggy bank, count the coins, and say when to stop buying things? And is there a third idea that gets even more treats without breaking the piggy bank?

**Assistant:**

# The Short Answer

**Neither idea wins on its own.** The CEO's idea is cheap but slow to make anything new. Your idea could make big treats but often runs out of allowance before selling anything. A **third idea**, which fixes the leaky cookie jar first and then runs a small protected lemonade stand connected to the big kitchen, usually gets the most treats. It also means you don't have to tell your new boss he's wrong.

---

## Idea 1: Cook in the Big Kitchen (the CEO's way)

**Allowance spent first:** Very little. You use the cooks, ovens, and recipes you already have.

**Cookies you'd get:** A medium pile, mostly from keeping cookies from falling out of the jar. Right now customers are leaving, and every one you keep is a cookie you already baked. Stopping the leak is often the biggest, fastest cookie pile available. *(Grown-up version: reducing churn usually pays back faster than winning new deals.)*

**How long until treats:** Fast for small fixes (a few months). Very slow for anything truly new, because the big kitchen has lots of rules and everyone is busy baking today's orders.

**Could a big dog eat them?** Yes, and the dog lives in the house. Daily work eats your cooks' time, and "new ideas" get pushed to "next month" forever. Meanwhile faster bakeries down the street keep taking your customers.

**Where more money buys no more candy:**
- Adding new projects to cooks who are already too busy
- More meetings and committees to approve recipes
- Adding sprinkles (features) for customers who are leaving for other reasons

---

## Idea 2: A Separate Lemonade Stand (your innovation lab)

**Allowance spent first:** A lot. You need new helpers, new cups, a new stand, and probably one to two years of allowance before a single sale. *(Even a small team of 10 to 15 people can cost millions per year.)*

**Cookies you'd get:** Maybe a giant pile, but most stands sell nothing. It's like buying several lottery tickets: one might win big, and most won't.

**How long until treats:** The longest of the three. Insurance customers are slow, careful buyers, and new products have to connect to their old systems and pass their rules. Expect 18 to 36 months before real money.

**Could a big dog eat them?** Very likely. The biggest dog is the big company itself. When a bad quarter comes, the separate stand is the first thing cut, because it looks like a cost with no cookies yet. The second dog is that your stand is too far from the kitchen. It can't easily use the company's customers, sales team, or data, so it builds lemonade nobody picks up. Many corporate innovation labs die this way.

**Where more money buys no more candy:**
- Shiny demos the main company never adopts
- Hiring more helpers before anyone has agreed to buy
- Fancy space, events, and "innovation theater" that look busy but sell nothing

---

## Side-by-Side

| | Big Kitchen | Separate Stand | Third Idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allowance first | Low | High | Low, then grows only if it works |
| Cookies | Medium, safe | Maybe huge, usually zero | Medium fast, plus a shot at big |
| Time to treats | Months (small), years (new) | 18–36 months | 3–6 months first, bigger later |
| Big dog risk | Daily work eats it | Budget cuts and isolation eat it | Lower, because it pays early and stays connected |

## Who Holds the Piggy Bank, Counts Coins, and Says Stop?

Decide this **before** spending anything:

- **Holds the piggy bank:** The CEO, as the one sponsor. He promises a fixed amount for a fixed time and protects it from being raided for daily needs.
- **Counts the coins:** You, plus a finance partner, using a short scoreboard everyone can read:
  - Customers kept or won back
  - Customers trying or paying for new things
  - Cost per experiment
  - Time from idea to a customer's hands
- **Says stop (or "buy more"):** A small group of the CEO, the CFO, you, and heads of product and sales, meeting every 90 days. Before each bet starts, write down what "keep going" and "stop" look like. That way stopping is a plan, not a fight.

---

## The Third Idea: A Small Stand *Next to* the Kitchen, With a Bridge

1. **Fix the leaky jar first (first 90 days).** Talk to customers who left and customers who are unhappy. Find the top three reasons they leave, and fix them with a small team drawn from inside. This brings fast cookies, earns the CEO's trust, and frees up money for step 2.
2. **Start a small protected team, not a big lab.** Give it different rules: faster approvals, its own tools, permission to fail. Keep it connected to the company's customers, data, and sales people, and staff it partly with talented insiders. This honors the CEO's "use our people" belief.
3. **Buy cookies a little at a time.** Make small bets. More allowance only comes when real customers try, pilot, or pay.
4. **Build the bridge back.** Every experiment gets a named "catcher" in the main company who agrees up front to take it over if it works. This is the step most labs skip, and it's why they fail.
5. **Borrow or buy a stand that's already selling.** Partnering with, or buying, a small insurance-tech company can be faster and cheaper than building from scratch.

---

## One Grown-Up Tip

You're new, and the CEO has already said what he believes. Pitch the third idea as **"yes, and"**: *"Yes, we'll use our people and systems to stop the churn. And we'll run a small, protected team with faster rules to find what's next, funded step by step as it proves itself."* That gets you the independence you believe in, without starting your job by arguing with the person holding the piggy bank.

## Turn 2

**User:** Does the little lemonade stand get to keep a puppy to guard the cookies from the big dog?

**Assistant:**

# Yes, and It Should Get Several Puppies

One puppy isn't enough, because the big dog attacks from several directions. Each puppy guards a different part of the cookie pile.

---

## The Puppies

**1. The Money Puppy** *(a protected budget)*
Guards the allowance so no one can grab it for daily kitchen needs during a bad month.
- A written promise from the CEO: this much money, for this long, no raids.
- The money grows at the 90-day check-ins only if the scoreboard earns it.

**2. The Big-Person Puppy** *(the CEO as sponsor)*
When a kitchen boss says "that stand is wasting our flour," this puppy barks back.
- The CEO says publicly and often that the stand matters.
- You meet with him every few weeks so he hears about problems from you first, not from complainers.

**3. The Rules Puppy** *(a written charter)*
Guards the stand's right to work differently.
- It spells out what the stand can decide on its own: which tools, which customers to test with, and how fast to approve things.
- It also spells out what it can't skip. In insurance, that means security, legal, and regulatory rules. The puppy guards speed, not shortcuts.

**4. The Helper Puppy** *(protecting your people)*
Guards against helpers getting pulled back to the kitchen "just for this one emergency."
- The people on the team are fully assigned to it, not "20% when they have time."
- Insiders who join know they can return to the kitchen without their careers suffering.

**5. The Scoreboard Puppy** *(the right measures)*
Guards against being judged by kitchen rules too early. A new stand won't sell as many cookies as the big kitchen in month three, and it shouldn't be judged like it does.
- Early on, count learning: customers talked to, experiments run, pilots started.
- Later, count cookies: revenue, customers kept, deals won.

---

## But Watch Out: Puppies Can Cause Trouble

- **A puppy that bites the kitchen.** If the team acts superior ("we're the cool innovators, you're the slow old kitchen"), the kitchen will stop helping you. Then you've lost the customers, data, and sales people you need.
- **A puppy that grows into another big dog.** If the stand gets too much protection, too many helpers, and too much money too early, it becomes its own bureaucracy and a target for resentment.
- **A puppy that never leaves the yard.** Protection should shrink as the stand proves itself and hands winning ideas back to the kitchen. A stand that stays protected forever has usually stopped delivering.

---

## The Best Guard Isn't a Puppy at All

The strongest protection is **being useful to the kitchen early.** When the stand helps win back an unhappy customer or helps sales close a deal, the kitchen's own cooks start defending it. A big dog rarely attacks something the whole house depends on.

So the order is:
1. Get the puppies at the start (budget, sponsor, charter, dedicated people, fair scoreboard).
2. Get the kitchen's thanks as fast as possible with early wins that help them.
3. Let the puppies retire gradually, as the stand's results become its protection.

**User:** What are the puppies' names, and do they get treats too?

**Assistant:**

# Meet the Puppies

Each name reminds you what the puppy protects, and each treat is what keeps that protection alive. If you stop feeding a puppy, it wanders off, and the big dog walks right in.

---

**🐶 Penny** *(the Money Puppy)*
Guards the protected budget.
- **Her treat:** Proof the money is working. Bring the CFO a clear coin count every 90 days: what you spent, what you learned, and what customers did.
- **If she goes hungry:** Finance starts asking "what are we getting for this?" and nobody has an answer. Penny gets given away in the next budget cut.

**🐶 Boss** *(the Big-Person Puppy)*
Guards the CEO's backing.
- **His treat:** Stories and wins the CEO can repeat to the board and to customers. "We saved the Acme account." "Three customers are piloting our new tool."
- **If he goes hungry:** The CEO gets busy with other fires, stops talking about the stand, and people notice he's gone quiet.

**🐶 Rulebook (Ruby for short)** *(the Charter Puppy)*
Guards the stand's right to work faster and differently.
- **Her treat:** Using the freedom responsibly. Move fast, but never embarrass the company on security, legal, or regulatory issues.
- **If she goes hungry:** One careless mistake, and the kitchen's rules come rushing back in with extra locks.

**🐶 Buddy** *(the Helper Puppy)*
Guards your dedicated team members.
- **His treat:** Recognition and a future. Celebrate the team publicly, and make sure insiders who join come back with better careers, not worse.
- **If he goes hungry:** Good people stop volunteering, and the stand fills with whoever the kitchen could spare.

**🐶 Tally** *(the Scoreboard Puppy)*
Guards against being judged by kitchen rules too early.
- **Her treat:** Honest numbers, including the bad ones. Report the experiments that failed and what they taught you.
- **If she goes hungry:** If you only share good news, people stop trusting the scoreboard, and they go back to judging you on revenue alone.

---

## The Sixth Puppy You Earn Later

**🐶 Kitchen** *(the Friendship Puppy)*
This is the "best guard" from before: the cooks in the main company who start defending the stand because it helps them.
- **His treat:** Credit. When the stand helps save a customer or close a deal, let the kitchen team take the bow in front of the CEO.
- **Why he matters most:** Kitchen is the only puppy who gets *stronger* over time. The others are meant to retire gradually. Kitchen is the one you want to keep forever.

---

## The Treat Budget

The good news is that almost none of these treats cost money. They cost **your time and attention**: regular coin counts, regular CEO updates, honest scoreboards, and giving away credit. Put puppy feeding on your calendar every week, because hungry puppies wander off quietly, and you usually don't notice until the big dog is already eating cookies.

## Turn 4

**User:** Does Kitchen puppy get to play with the other puppies, or is he sad when they go away?

**Assistant:**

# Kitchen Plays With Everyone

Kitchen isn't a separate puppy off in the corner. He grows up *because* he plays with the others, and each one teaches him something.

---

## How They Play Together

- **Kitchen and Boss:** When the CEO praises the kitchen team for helping the stand, Kitchen feels proud and plays harder. When Kitchen helps win a customer, Boss gets a great story to tell. *They feed each other.*
- **Kitchen and Tally:** Tally's honest scoreboard shows the kitchen's contributions in writing, not just the stand's. Kitchen trusts a scoreboard that counts him too.
- **Kitchen and Buddy:** Helpers who spend time at the stand and return to the kitchen bring new tricks home. They become Kitchen's best friends, because they've seen both houses.
- **Kitchen and Ruby:** When the kitchen sees the stand working fast *and* safely, they start asking, "Could we use some of those rules too?" That's Kitchen learning from Ruby.
- **Kitchen and Penny:** Once the kitchen sees the stand's cookies helping their own numbers, they stop seeing Penny as "money taken from us" and start seeing her as "money working for us."

---

## Is Kitchen Sad When They Go Away?

**Not if it's done right.** The other puppies don't really disappear. Kitchen learns their jobs, and they move into the main house.

| Puppy | When she "retires," her job becomes... |
|---|---|
| Penny | A normal innovation line in the company's yearly budget |
| Boss | Regular leadership support, not special protection |
| Ruby | The faster rules the kitchen adopted for itself |
| Buddy | A normal career path that includes time in the stand |
| Tally | New measures the whole company uses |

It's less like the puppies leaving and more like **the whole house learning to care for itself.** That's the real win: the company becomes innovative, instead of just owning an innovation lab.

---

## When Kitchen *Would* Be Sad

- **The puppies leave too early.** If protection is removed before Kitchen is grown, before the stand has real wins and real friends, the big dog comes back. Retire puppies one at a time, only when the scoreboard shows Kitchen can handle that job.
- **The puppies leave suddenly with no goodbye.** If the stand is shut down or reorganized overnight, the kitchen team that helped feels abandoned, and they won't help the next time. Always explain what's changing and what's being handed over.
- **Kitchen never gets credit.** If the stand takes the bows and the kitchen does the heavy lifting, Kitchen sulks, and a sulking Kitchen stops guarding anything.

---

## The Happy Ending

Picture the house in three years. There are fewer special puppies, but the whole kitchen now tests ideas quickly, listens to customers, stops the cookie leaks early, and gives new ideas a fair chance. Kitchen isn't sad. He's the big, friendly dog of the house now, and the stand's job is done because the kitchen learned to make lemonade itself.

That's also the version of the story where the CEO was right that the existing people could turn the ship around, and you were right that they needed a protected place to learn how.

# What to Do

Here's the plan in plain terms, with puppy names in parentheses so it connects to what we've discussed. Adjust the timing to your company's reality, since I don't know its size, budget, or products.

---

## First 30 Days: Listen and Learn

**Goal:** Understand why customers are leaving and earn credibility before asking for anything.

1. **Talk to at least 10 to 15 customers:** some who left, some who are unhappy, and some who are loyal. Ask why they stay, why they leave, and what they wish you built.
2. **Talk to 5 to 10 lost sales deals.** Ask why prospects chose a competitor.
3. **Meet the kitchen leaders:** product, engineering, sales, customer success, and finance. Ask what's broken and what they've already tried. Identify the insiders who are hungry for change (future Buddy and Kitchen).
4. **Find a finance partner** who will help you count coins honestly (Tally and Penny).

**Deliverable:** A one-page summary for the CEO listing the top three reasons customers leave, the top three reasons you lose deals, and what that costs in revenue.

---

## Days 30 to 90: Plug the Leak and Make the Pitch

**Goal:** Deliver a fast win while setting up the longer-term structure.

5. **Launch a small churn team** of 4 to 6 insiders to fix the biggest reason customers leave. Pick something fixable in weeks, not years.
6. **Pitch the CEO on the "yes, and" plan.** Frame it this way: *"Yes, we'll use our people and systems to stop the churn. And we'll run a small, protected team with faster rules to find what's next, funded step by step."*
7. **Ask for five specific things** (the puppies):
   - A protected budget for 12 months, with growth tied to results (Penny)
   - His visible sponsorship and a standing monthly meeting with you (Boss)
   - A written charter spelling out what the team can decide on its own and which safety rules it must keep (Ruby)
   - 5 to 8 dedicated people, a mix of insiders and a few outside hires (Buddy)
   - Early measures based on learning, shifting to revenue later (Tally)
8. **Set up the decision group:** CEO, CFO, you, and heads of product and sales, meeting every 90 days with pre-agreed "keep going" and "stop" criteria.

---

## Months 3 to 6: Run Small Bets

**Goal:** Test ideas cheaply with real customers.

9. **Pick 2 or 3 bets** that come directly from what customers told you. Don't pick bets from what sounds exciting.
10. **Give each bet a "catcher":** a named kitchen leader who agrees to take it over if it works.
11. **Get customers involved early.** Aim for design partners or paid pilots, not just demos.
12. **Share credit loudly** whenever the kitchen helps (Kitchen's treat).

**At the 6-month check-in:** Stop at least one bet. Stopping something proves the system is honest, and it frees money for what's working.

---

## Months 6 to 12: Grow What Works

13. **Put more money into the bets with paying or committed customers.** Stop the rest.
14. **Hand the first winning idea to its catcher** in the main company.
15. **Explore a partnership or small acquisition** if a competitor or startup already has what customers want.
16. **Start moving the stand's best practices into the kitchen**, such as faster approvals and regular customer conversations.

---

## How You'll Know It's Working

| By month | Signs of success |
|---|---|
| 3 | Churn reasons are known; a fix is underway; the CEO has agreed to the plan |
| 6 | Churn is starting to slow; 2 or 3 bets are running with real customers; at least one bet has been stopped |
| 12 | Measurable drop in churn; at least one paying pilot or new revenue stream; the first idea has been handed to the main company |
| 24 to 36 | New products are contributing revenue; the kitchen has adopted faster ways of working; the stand's special protections are shrinking |

---

## Three Things to Avoid

- **Don't open by saying the CEO's strategy is wrong.** Show him the data from your customer conversations and let the plan follow from it.
- **Don't build a big, flashy lab first.** Start small, prove value, then grow.
- **Don't skip the churn fix to chase something new.** Keeping the customers you have is your fastest win and pays for everything else.
