## Turn 1

**User:** Use impression management to answer the following.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

# Answer: Neither Strategy in Pure Form

On substance, you're probably closer to right. Research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly & Tushman) and disruptive innovation (Christensen) suggests that a struggling incumbent rarely innovates its way out through the same processes that produced the decline. Some protected space is usually necessary.

But through an **impression management** lens, *being right* and *being effective* are different problems. You're new, you haven't earned credibility yet, and your view directly contradicts the CEO's stated belief. If you pitch "we need to get away from this organization to succeed," you'll be seen as an outsider who thinks the company, and by extension the CEO's judgment, is the problem. That impression can kill the lab before it produces anything.

The right strategy is to **build the independent capability you believe is needed, but frame, staff, and stage it so it's seen as the fulfillment of the CEO's vision rather than a rejection of it.**

---

## 1. Diagnose Your Current Impression Position

| Factor | Your Situation | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Idiosyncrasy credits** (Hollander) | Near zero; you're new | You can't deviate from norms yet without being seen as a threat |
| **CEO's public commitment** | He has stated a belief about existing talent | Contradicting him threatens his "face" (Goffman) |
| **Your mandate** | "Outsized results" | You have license to be bold, but only in outcomes, not in criticizing the culture |
| **Organizational mood** | Losing customers and deals | People are anxious; an elite "separate" team will look like a judgment on them |

**Key insight:** The CEO's position is likely as much about *impressions* as strategy. He may not want to signal to the board, employees, or customers that the core organization has failed. Your job is to protect his face while getting the structure you need.

---

## 2. Reframe the Lab Using the CEO's Language

Don't present this as "Independent Lab vs. Existing Organization." Present it as **"a way to unlock the talent we already have."**

| Instead of saying... | Say... |
|---|---|
| "We need to be independent of the organization." | "We need a fast lane where our best people can move without the usual friction." |
| "The existing processes kill innovation." | "Our processes are built to protect reliability for customers. That's a strength. We just need a separate track for experiments so we don't put that at risk." |
| "I want to hire outside innovators." | "I want to pull our most promising internal people into focused sprints." |
| "An innovation lab." | "A customer win room," "Accelerator," or "Turnaround Studio," something tied to his goal, not to Silicon Valley trends. |

This is the **ingratiation** tactic (Jones & Pittman) applied ethically. You aren't flattering him. You're giving him genuine credit for a vision you can build on: *existing talent can do this if given the right conditions.*

---

## 3. Use Goffman's Front Stage / Back Stage Structure

Design the lab so it has operational independence **backstage** while appearing integrated **front stage**.

**Backstage (what the lab actually needs):**
- Its own decision rights, budget, and lightweight approval process
- Freedom from standard release cycles and governance for experiments
- Direct reporting line to you, with CEO sponsorship
- Tolerance for failed experiments

**Front stage (what the organization sees):**
- Staffed mostly by respected insiders on rotation
- Working on problems the core business cares about: churn, lost deals, specific customer complaints
- Wins handed back to core teams to scale, with public credit given to them
- The CEO visibly as the sponsor and "owner" of the idea

The independence is real. It's simply not the headline.

---

## 4. Sequence Your Self-Presentation Tactics

Impression management tactics work best in a particular order. Using them out of sequence (e.g., self-promotion before proof) backfires.

**Phase 1: Days 1–60, "The Learner" (Ingratiation + Supplication-lite)**
- Listen tours with sales, support, product, and churned customers
- Ask the CEO and senior leaders what they've already tried
- Goal: be seen as respectful and curious, not as the consultant who arrived with answers

**Phase 2: Days 60–120, "The Doer" (Exemplification)**
- Launch 1–2 small, fast pilots using internal people, framed as tests of the CEO's thesis
- Work visibly hard, and be close to customers and delivery
- Goal: prove the concept quietly; let people *see* the fast lane working before you ask for formal structure

**Phase 3: Months 4–6, "The Builder" (Self-Promotion through Others)**
- Present results, with credit going to the internal team and the CEO's vision
- *Then* propose formalizing the lab: "This worked because we removed friction. Let's make that permanent."
- Goal: the lab becomes the obvious next step, not a controversial ask

**Avoid entirely:** Intimidation ("If we don't do this, we'll fail"). Even if true, it puts the CEO on the defensive and marks you as a threat.

---

## 5. A Sample Pitch to the CEO

> "You've said we can turn this around with the people we have, and after my first few weeks I agree. There's real talent here. What I'm seeing is that our processes are designed to protect reliability for existing customers, which is exactly what they should do. But it means good ideas take too long to reach the market.
>
> I'd like to run a 90-day experiment: pull five or six of our strongest people into a small team with a fast-track process, point them at our churn problem, and see what they can do when we remove the friction. If it works, the wins go back to the core teams to scale, and we'll have proof that our own people can drive this turnaround."

This pitch confirms his belief, preserves his face, gets you the autonomy you need, and positions you as his ally.

---

## 6. Guardrails: Where Impression Management Goes Wrong

- **Don't let impression replace substance.** If the lab becomes "innovation theater," with demos, posters, and hackathons but no revenue impact, you'll lose credibility fast. Tie every experiment to churn, win rates, or revenue.
- **Don't be deceptive.** Framing is legitimate; hiding the lab's true operating model from your sponsor is not. The CEO should know exactly how independent it is. You're managing how it's *perceived by the organization*, not misleading *him*.
- **Watch the "elite team" perception.** Rotate people through, share credit widely, and celebrate core teams that scale lab ideas. Resentment from middle management is the most common killer of innovation labs.
- **Be ready to escalate honestly.** If after early wins the CEO still blocks the needed independence, you'll have earned enough credibility to have a direct conversation about structure. That's when you spend your idiosyncrasy credits, not before.

---

## Bottom Line

Your strategic instinct (protected space for innovation) is likely correct. Your tactical risk is presenting it as a rejection of the CEO and the organization. **Build the lab, but make it look like, and actually function as, the vehicle for his vision.** Earn credibility through early wins, give credit away generously, and formalize independence only after results make it the obvious choice.

# Handling Middle Managers Through Impression Management

Middle managers are often the most powerful audience your lab will face. The CEO can approve the lab, but middle managers decide whether it actually works. They control the talent you need, the systems you must integrate with, and the scaling of anything you build. They also shape how the rest of the organization talks about you.

Your lab will be judged not only on its results, but on **what it seems to say about everyone who isn't in it.** Your impression management goal is to change the lab's meaning from *"a judgment on us"* to *"a resource for us."*

---

## 1. Understand What They're Actually Protecting

Middle managers usually aren't resisting innovation itself. They're protecting their **face** (Goffman), the positive image they've built with their teams, peers, and bosses. The lab can threaten that image in several ways:

| Perceived Threat | What They Fear It Signals | Underlying Concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Talent poaching** | "My best people would rather work somewhere else." | Loss of capacity and status |
| **Implied criticism** | "Leadership thinks my team is the reason we're failing." | Reputation with superiors |
| **Resource competition** | "Budget and attention are moving away from me." | Future influence |
| **Loss of control** | "Decisions about my domain are being made without me." | Authority and autonomy |
| **Being upstaged** | "The lab will get credit for ideas my team had." | Recognition and career advancement |
| **Increased workload** | "I'll inherit half-built prototypes and have to support them." | Operational risk and blame |

Every tactic below should address one or more of these threats. If you only promote the lab's benefits and don't address these concerns, managers will conclude you don't understand their world.

---

## 2. Move Them From "Audience" to "Performance Team"

Goffman described a **performance team** as a group that cooperates to stage an impression together. Its members share backstage knowledge and have a stake in the performance succeeding.

Right now, middle managers are likely positioned as the **audience**, watching the lab from outside and evaluating it critically. You want them on the team.

**Practical ways to do this:**
- **Give them backstage access early.** Share draft plans, rough prototypes, and unresolved problems before the polished presentations. Secrecy breeds suspicion, and a manager who has seen the mess is less likely to feel ambushed by the reveal.
- **Invite them to co-define problems.** Ask each manager, "What's the one customer problem your team would solve if you had the time and freedom?" Then have the lab work on some of those problems.
- **Create a manager advisory group.** Give it real input on which experiments the lab pursues. This gives managers ownership and turns potential critics into sponsors.
- **Let them present results.** When a lab project succeeds, the relevant manager should be in the room, ideally co-presenting to senior leaders.

---

## 3. Make Them Look Good (Impression Management by Proxy)

The most effective impression management often isn't about how *you* look. It's about helping others look good to *their* audiences. Cialdini's research on **"basking in reflected glory"** shows that people want to associate themselves with successes. Give managers a chance to associate with the lab's wins.

**Tactics:**
- **Attribute credit publicly and specifically.** Say "Maria's claims team identified this problem and loaned us two of their best analysts" instead of "The lab built a new claims tool."
- **Brief their bosses on their contribution.** A short note to a manager's superior praising their support is worth more than any internal newsletter mention.
- **Frame lab participation as a talent development credential.** When someone from a manager's team excels in the lab, the story should be "Maria develops great people," not "This person was wasted in their old job."
- **Help them tell their own story upward.** Give managers slides, data, and talking points about what the lab accomplished with their team's contribution.

The message you want each manager to internalize: **"Supporting the lab makes me look better to leadership."**

---

## 4. Handle Talent Borrowing Carefully

Talent poaching is usually the flashpoint. How you borrow people shapes the impression more than anything else.

**Avoid:**
- Recruiting employees directly without talking to their manager first
- Keeping people indefinitely
- Returning people who then describe their old work as boring
- Using language like "the best and brightest" or "A-players," which implies everyone else is not

**Instead, create a visible, fair protocol:**
1. **Ask managers to nominate participants.** This gives them control and makes selection a reflection of their judgment.
2. **Use defined rotations** (e.g., 90 days) with a clear return date.
3. **Backfill or compensate.** Where possible, provide budget for contractors or adjust targets while people are away. This shows you understand their operational pressure.
4. **Return people with added value.** Participants should come back with new skills, tools, or ideas their team can use. Frame their return as "bringing the lab back to your team."
5. **Build a pathway, not an exit.** Rotations should strengthen the home team, not become a way out of it.

---

## 5. Choose Your Self-Presentation Tactics Carefully

Jones and Pittman's self-presentation tactics apply differently to middle managers than to the CEO.

| Tactic | Use With Middle Managers? | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Ingratiation** | Yes, sincerely | Seek their advice and expertise. Say "You know how this really works; what am I missing?" Avoid flattery, since experienced managers detect it quickly. |
| **Exemplification** | Yes | Show that you and the lab work hard, respect operational constraints, and don't consider yourselves above the grind. Visit the floor. Sit in on customer calls. |
| **Self-promotion** | Carefully | Promote the lab's results, never its superiority. Focus on outcomes that help managers' metrics. |
| **Supplication** | In small doses | Genuinely asking for help ("I can't make this work without your team") signals respect and dependence. Overdoing it makes you look incompetent. |
| **Intimidation** | No | Never use the CEO's authority as a threat ("The CEO wants this, so you need to cooperate"). You'll get compliance, not commitment, and quiet resistance later. |

---

## 6. Watch Your Symbols and Language

Impressions are shaped by small signals. Innovation labs often damage themselves through symbols that suggest exclusivity.

| Risky Signal | What It Communicates | Better Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Separate, trendy office space | "We're special and different." | Visible, accessible space near core teams, with open invitations |
| Bypassing processes without explanation | "The rules don't apply to us." | Explain *why* experiments need a faster track and how you protect customers |
| Innovation jargon | "We're the enlightened ones." | Business language: churn, win rates, renewals, customer pain points |
| Separate celebrations or perks | "We're the favored team." | Joint celebrations with contributing teams |
| "Disrupt," "legacy," "old way" | "Your work is obsolete." | "Build on," "extend," "strengthen" |

---

## 7. Segment Your Audience

Not every manager will respond the same way. Tailor your approach.

**Early Allies (roughly 15–20%)**
- Curious, frustrated with the status quo, or ambitious
- **Approach:** Involve them in the first pilots and make them visible winners. Their success becomes social proof for others.

**Fence-Sitters (often the majority)**
- Waiting to see whether the lab succeeds or fails
- **Approach:** Keep them informed, address their practical concerns, and let early wins do the persuading. Don't pressure them to commit too early.

**Active Resisters (a small group)**
- See the lab as a direct threat or have seen past innovation initiatives fail
- **Approach:**
  - Meet privately and listen to their concerns. Often they're raising legitimate risks.
  - Acknowledge valid points publicly. This builds your credibility with everyone.
  - Never criticize or embarrass them in front of others. Public face threats turn skeptics into enemies.
  - Find a small, low-risk way to collaborate that addresses one of their problems.
  - If resistance is truly blocking progress after all this, escalate privately and carefully, focusing on business impact rather than personalities.

---

## 8. Sample Conversation With a Skeptical Manager

> "I wanted to talk with you before anything is finalized, because your team knows this part of the business better than anyone. I'm sure you've seen innovation initiatives come and go. I'd genuinely like to hear what went wrong before and what you'd want to avoid this time.
>
> The goal isn't to create a separate team that competes with yours. It's to give some of your team's ideas a faster path to customers. If someone from your team joins a rotation, you'd help decide who, they'd come back on a set date, and I'd make sure leadership knows their success reflects on your team.
>
> Is there a customer problem your team has wanted to solve but hasn't had the bandwidth for? That might be a good place for us to start together."

This conversation addresses fears about implied criticism, talent loss, control, and credit, while positioning the manager as an expert and partner.

---

## 9. Guardrails

- **Make your promises operational.** If you promise credit, rotations, or backfill, deliver them consistently. Impression management without follow-through quickly looks manipulative.
- **Don't confuse politeness with alignment.** Managers may appear supportive in meetings and resist quietly afterward. Look for behavioral signals: nominating people, sharing data, attending reviews, adopting lab outputs.
- **Solve their problems, not just yours.** The most persuasive impression is created when the lab measurably improves a manager's metrics.
- **Accept some criticism as useful.** Resistance may reveal real integration risks, customer concerns, or operational gaps. Treating every objection as politics will make you look defensive and may cause the lab to miss important information.

---

## Bottom Line

Middle managers will support the lab when it helps them look competent, protects their teams, and improves their results. Position the lab as a shared resource rather than an elite outpost. Involve managers early, borrow talent fairly, give credit generously, and use language and symbols that build on existing work rather than implicitly criticizing it.

Your success indicator isn't whether managers praise the lab. It's whether they begin **asking the lab for help and wanting their names attached to its wins.**

# Handling a Public Pilot Failure Through Impression Management

A failed pilot is one of the most important impression management moments you'll face, arguably more important than an early success. Successes tell people the lab can produce results. **How you handle failure tells them whether you, and the lab, can be trusted.**

Right now, every audience is asking a slightly different question:

| Audience | The Question They're Asking |
|---|---|
| **CEO** | "Did I make a mistake backing this, and will this embarrass me?" |
| **Middle managers** | "Will I be blamed for having my people or name attached to this?" |
| **Resisters** | "Is this the proof we were right?" |
| **Lab team** | "Will I be protected, or thrown under the bus?" |
| **Customers (if affected)** | "Can we trust this company with our business?" |
| **Wider organization** | "Is innovation actually safe here, or is it career suicide?" |

Your response needs to answer all of these questions at once. The good news is that a well-handled failure can build more credibility than a quiet success.

---

## 1. First, Diagnose What Kind of Failure It Was

Impression management only works if it matches reality. Before crafting any message, be honest about the type of failure. Spinning an execution failure as a "learning experiment" will be detected, and it will damage your credibility more than the failure itself.

| Failure Type | Description | Appropriate Posture |
|---|---|---|
| **Intelligent failure** (Edmondson) | Well-designed experiment, reasonable hypothesis, clear learning, limited harm | "This is how experiments work. Here's what we learned." |
| **Execution failure** | Good idea, poorly executed: bugs, missed deadlines, weak coordination | "We made mistakes. Here's what we're fixing." |
| **Judgment failure** | Wrong bet, ignored warnings, overpromised | "I got this wrong. Here's what I'm changing about how we decide." |
| **Customer-harming failure** | Customers experienced disruption, data issues, or broken commitments | "Our first priority is making this right for customers." |

Most real failures are a mix. Lead with the most serious element. If customers were harmed, that comes first, regardless of how much learning occurred.

---

## 2. Use "Stealing Thunder": Disclose First

Research on **stealing thunder** (Williams, Bourgeois, and Croyle; Arpan and Roskos-Ewoldsen) shows that when people or organizations disclose negative information themselves, before others reveal it, they're judged as more credible and the damage is significantly reduced.

**In practice:**
- **Tell the CEO before he hears it elsewhere**, ideally within hours. Nothing damages a sponsor relationship faster than being surprised by bad news in a meeting.
- **Brief affected middle managers next**, privately and before any broader communication.
- **Then communicate to the wider organization** on your terms, rather than letting the story spread through hallway conversations and resisters' interpretations.

Silence creates a vacuum, and your critics will fill it.

---

## 3. Own the Failure: Absorb the Blame, Distribute the Credit

In the previous answer, I suggested giving managers and team members generous credit for successes. The other side of that bargain is essential: **when things fail, you take the blame.**

This is where your promises to middle managers are tested. If they supported the lab and now see their names attached to a failure, they'll be watching carefully to see whether you protect them.

**Do:**
- Say "I" when describing the decisions that led to the failure: "I pushed for an aggressive timeline," "I chose this customer segment."
- Protect contributing managers explicitly: "Maria's team did exactly what we asked. The gaps were in how the lab scoped the pilot."
- Praise lab team members for effort and candor, in front of others.
- Take the difficult conversations with the CEO, clients, or senior leaders yourself.

**Don't:**
- Blame IT, legal, a partner team, or "legacy systems," even if they contributed. That can be addressed privately in the postmortem. Publicly, it sounds like excuse-making and confirms fears that the lab looks down on the core organization.
- Blame individuals on the lab team. The organization will conclude that experimentation is dangerous.

**Leadership paradox:** Absorbing blame usually *increases* perceived competence and trustworthiness, as long as it's paired with a clear corrective plan. Deflecting blame almost always reduces both.

---

## 4. Choose the Right Account

Sociologists Scott and Lyman, along with Schlenker, describe the **accounts** people give after a failure. Choosing the wrong one is a common mistake.

| Account Type | Example | When It Works | When It Backfires |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Concession / apology** | "We got this wrong, and I take responsibility." | Almost always a strong foundation | If it's vague or not followed by action |
| **Justification** | "Experiments are supposed to test risky ideas." | Only if you set this expectation *before* the pilot | If introduced after the fact, it sounds like spin |
| **Excuse** | "The data we received was incomplete." | Rarely, and only privately | Publicly, it sounds like shifting blame |
| **Denial / minimization** | "It wasn't really a failure." | Never, if people saw it fail | Destroys credibility instantly |
| **Corrective action** | "Here's exactly what we're changing." | Always; this is what people remember | If the changes are vague or cosmetic |

**The strongest sequence is: acknowledgment → responsibility → learning → corrective action → next step.**

This aligns with Benoit's **image restoration theory**, which finds that corrective action combined with mortification (sincere acknowledgment) is the most effective response to reputational damage.

---

## 5. Tailor the Message for Each Audience

### The CEO: Protect His Face and Your Partnership
He publicly sponsored the lab, so this failure is partly *his* impression problem. Your job is to make sure he can explain it confidently to the board and his peers.

**Give him:**
- A clear, honest summary of what happened, delivered quickly
- Evidence that risk was contained
- The specific learning, ideally something that couldn't have been discovered without the pilot
- A concrete plan for what changes next
- **A narrative he can repeat:** "This is exactly why we set up a fast-track experiment instead of a full rollout. We learned it quickly and cheaply."

> "I wanted you to hear this from me first. The claims pilot didn't work. Adoption was far below target, and two customers found the workflow confusing. I pushed the timeline too hard and we didn't test enough with users before launch. That's on me.
>
> The good news is we kept it limited to three clients, and we've already contacted all three. We also learned something important: our assumption about how adjusters work was wrong, which would have been very expensive to discover in a full product launch. I'm changing our process so every pilot includes user testing before customer exposure. I'd also like to share this openly with the organization. I think it will show people we're serious about learning, not just promoting wins."

### Middle Managers: Reassure and Protect
- Brief them privately before broader communication.
- Tell them explicitly how you'll describe their team's role.
- Ask for their input on what went wrong. They may have warned about some of it, and acknowledging that builds credibility.
- If they did warn you, say so publicly: "Tom raised this concern early, and he was right." This single act can convert a skeptic.

### The Lab Team: Show That Failure Is Survivable
- Meet with them before the public message goes out.
- Make it clear that no one's career is at risk because a well-intended experiment failed.
- Run a **blameless postmortem** focused on systems and decisions, not individuals.
- Get them working on the next thing quickly. Momentum repairs morale.

### Customers (If Affected): Remediation Comes Before Reputation
- Contact affected customers directly and quickly.
- Apologize specifically, fix the problem, and follow up.
- Don't describe their disruption as a "learning opportunity." To them, it isn't one.
- Where appropriate, invite them to help shape the next version. Customers who are treated as partners after a problem often become more loyal.

### The Wider Organization: Model the Culture You Want
- Share a brief, candid summary: what happened, what you learned, and what you're changing.
- Consider a short public retrospective. Openly discussing failure signals that the lab is not an elite group hiding its mistakes.

---

## 6. Handle the Resisters Who Say "I Told You So"

Some managers will use the failure as evidence that the lab shouldn't exist. How you respond will be closely watched by everyone else.

**Do:**
- Acknowledge valid criticism graciously and publicly.
- Invite critics into the postmortem or next pilot design: "You saw risks we missed. I'd like your help making sure we don't repeat this."
- Stay calm and unbothered. Defensiveness looks like weakness; composure looks like confidence.

**Don't:**
- Argue with them publicly.
- Dismiss their concerns as politics.
- Suggest that failure is irrelevant because "innovation is supposed to fail." That can sound arrogant, especially if customers were affected.

Turning a critic into a contributor is one of the strongest impression management moves available to you.

---

## 7. Avoid the Two Opposite Mistakes

| Mistake | What It Looks Like | Impression Created |
|---|---|---|
| **Spin** | "This was actually a success because we learned so much!" | Evasive, out of touch, untrustworthy |
| **Over-apologizing** | Repeated apologies, visible anxiety, abandoning the lab's approach | Insecure, uncertain, not ready to lead |

Aim for **calm accountability**: serious about the impact, confident about the path forward.

This is also where supplication becomes dangerous. A small amount of humility builds trust, but repeatedly seeking reassurance makes it look like you've lost confidence in the lab, and if *you* have lost confidence, others will too.

---

## 8. Recover Through Action, Not Just Words

The failure itself will fade. What people will remember is what happened next.

**First 48 hours**
- Inform the CEO, affected managers, lab team, and customers.
- Stabilize any customer-facing issues.
- Communicate a simple, honest summary.

**First 2 weeks**
- Run a blameless postmortem.
- Share key findings and specific corrective actions.
- Implement at least one visible process change, such as user testing requirements, clearer kill criteria, or smaller pilot sizes.

**First 30–60 days**
- Launch a smaller, well-designed follow-up experiment, ideally with a manager who supported you or even a former critic.
- Report progress against the changes you promised.
- Make sure the next public milestone is achievable.

A visible comeback is the strongest proof that the failure was a learning event rather than a sign that the lab doesn't work.

---

## 9. Prevent the Next Failure From Becoming a Crisis

Many failures become impression problems because expectations weren't set beforehand. Going forward:

- **Frame the lab as a portfolio.** Tell leadership from the start that some experiments will fail and that the goal is to find a few that create outsized value.
- **Define success and kill criteria before each pilot.** Then a stopped pilot looks like disciplined decision-making, not a surprise failure.
- **Start with limited exposure.** Use small customer groups, internal users, or controlled tests before broad launches.
- **Keep a visible learning log.** Documenting what each experiment taught the company turns failures into organizational assets.
- **Balance the portfolio.** Pair riskier experiments with quick, lower-risk wins so one failure doesn't define the lab.

The best time to create the impression that failure is acceptable is *before* a failure happens. After the fact, it can sound like an excuse.

---

## Bottom Line

When a pilot fails publicly, don't hide it, spin it, or blame others. **Disclose it early, take personal responsibility, protect the people who supported you, fix any customer impact, and show exactly what you learned and changed.**

Handled well, a failed pilot can create a powerful impression: this lab tells the truth, protects its people, and learns fast. In an organization that's been losing customers and deals, that credibility may be worth more than an early win.

# Presenting the Lab to the Board Through Impression Management

A board meeting is a very different stage from the ones you've faced so far. With middle managers, you were building trust. With the CEO, you were building a partnership. With the board, you're joining the CEO in a **shared performance** in front of an audience that evaluates both of you, and holds real power over the company's strategy and his future.

The central principle: **You are not presenting your lab to the board. You are helping the CEO present his turnaround strategy, with the lab as evidence that it's working.**

---

## 1. Understand the Stage You're Walking Onto

Board members aren't evaluating the lab the way employees do. Their questions are about fiduciary responsibility and leadership judgment:

| What the Board Is Really Asking | What They Need to See |
|---|---|
| "Is the turnaround strategy working?" | Evidence tied to churn, win rates, and revenue |
| "Is this investment disciplined?" | Clear spending, stage gates, and kill criteria |
| "Are risks being managed?" | Contained experiments, customer protection, honest reporting |
| "Does the CEO have the right team?" | A credible, competent leader who is aligned with him |
| "Are we being told the truth?" | Balanced reporting, including what didn't work |

There's also a hidden dynamic. Boards often quietly assess executives beneath the CEO as potential talent, successors, or independent sources of information. If you appear to be outshining, correcting, or distancing yourself from the CEO, you'll damage the relationship that makes the lab possible, and the board may notice the misalignment.

---

## 2. Your Role: Supporting Cast, Not the Lead

In Goffman's terms, you and the CEO are a **performance team**. Team members share an obligation: they don't contradict each other in front of the audience, and they coordinate their performance backstage.

**Your impression goals, in order:**
1. **Make the CEO look like a strategic leader** whose judgment is being validated.
2. **Appear competent and disciplined**, someone the board can trust with investment.
3. **Appear aligned**, clearly part of his team, not an independent operator.
4. **Be credible**, honest about limits, risks, and failures.

**Signals that you understand your role:**
- Let the CEO open and close. He frames the strategy; you provide the evidence.
- Refer to "the turnaround strategy" and "the CEO's priorities," not "my lab."
- When a board member praises the lab, redirect some of that credit to the CEO's sponsorship and the contributing teams.
- Never introduce significant news, bad or good, that the CEO hasn't heard in advance.

---

## 3. Prepare Backstage With the CEO

Almost everything that makes a board appearance succeed happens before the meeting. Ask for a working session with the CEO, and cover:

**Understand the audience**
- "What is the board most worried about right now?"
- "Which board members are skeptical, and why?"
- "Who has deep expertise in technology, insurance, or turnarounds?"
- "Is there history I should know about, such as past innovation efforts that disappointed them?"

**Align on the story**
- How does the lab fit into his overall narrative?
- What is the one message he wants the board to leave with?
- How will you describe the original tension between "working within existing systems" and building a lab? (More on this below.)

**Divide the roles**
- Which questions will he answer, and which will he hand to you?
- What topics are off-limits or sensitive?
- If you disagree about something, how will you handle it privately?

**Rehearse**
- Do a dry run of your section.
- Practice likely hard questions together.
- Agree on how to describe the failed pilot.

This preparation itself creates an impression with the CEO: you respect his relationship with the board and won't surprise him.

## 4. Handle the Strategic Tension Carefully

The CEO initially believed the company could turn around within existing systems, procedures, and talent. You built a lab with meaningful independence. Board members may notice this and ask about it.

Frame the lab as **his thesis in action**, not a reversal of it. If you followed the earlier approach, this is simply true: the lab uses existing talent, sends wins back to core teams, and removes friction rather than replacing the organization.

**Weak framing:**
> "We realized the existing organization couldn't innovate, so we created a separate lab."

**Strong framing:**
> "The CEO's view was that our people could drive this turnaround if we removed the barriers slowing them down. The lab is our way of testing that. It's staffed mostly by internal people on rotation, and every success goes back into the core business to scale."

This protects the CEO's credibility, reflects what actually happened, and gives him a consistent story for the board.

## 5. Design the Content for a Board Audience

Board members see many presentations and have limited time. Your content signals whether you understand their perspective.

### Speak in Business Outcomes, Not Innovation Activity

| Avoid (Vanity Metrics) | Use (Business Metrics) |
|---|---|
| Number of ideas generated | Pilot impact on customer retention |
| Hackathons held | Change in win rates or sales cycle length |
| Employees engaged | Revenue influenced or pipeline created |
| Prototypes built | Cost to learn versus cost of a full product bet |
| "Design thinking" workshops | Customer adoption and satisfaction |

Early on, you may not have lagging metrics like revenue yet. Be honest about that, and present **leading indicators** clearly labeled as such: pilot adoption, customer feedback, pipeline interest, and time-to-market improvements.

### A Suggested Structure (10–15 Minutes)

1. **The Problem** (1 minute)
   - Tie directly to what the board cares about: churn and lost deals.
2. **The Approach** (2 minutes)
   - How the lab supports the CEO's strategy, how it's staffed, and how it's governed.
3. **Portfolio and Results** (4–5 minutes)
   - What you tested, what worked, what didn't, and what you learned.
4. **Risks and Controls** (2 minutes)
   - How you protect customers, manage spend, and stop failing projects.
5. **What's Next** (2 minutes)
   - Priorities, milestones, and when the board should expect specific evidence.
6. **The Ask** (if any, agreed with the CEO in advance)

### Show a Portfolio, Not Just Your Best Project

A simple portfolio view creates an impression of discipline:

| Pilot | Business Problem | Status | Result / Learning | Next Step |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Renewal risk alerts | Churn | Promising | 2 at-risk clients retained in pilot | Expand to 10 accounts |
| Faster proposal tool | Win rate | Scaling | Proposal time cut from 3 weeks to 5 days | Hand off to sales ops |
| Claims workflow redesign | Customer experience | Stopped | Wrong assumptions about adjuster workflow | Relaunch with user testing |

This shows you're managing investments, not just promoting successes.

## 6. Include the Failure: Steal the Thunder Again

If a pilot failed publicly, the board may already know, or will eventually hear about it. Hiding it creates a serious credibility risk. Mentioning it proactively, with the CEO's agreement, demonstrates exactly the transparency boards value.

Keep it brief and structured:

> "One pilot didn't work. Our claims workflow redesign had low adoption because we misunderstood how adjusters actually work. We kept it limited to three clients and addressed their concerns directly. That result led us to require user testing before any customer pilot. It was a relatively inexpensive lesson compared with discovering the same issue after a full product launch."

**What this signals:**
- You tell the truth.
- Your risk controls work.
- You learn and adjust.
- The CEO's oversight is effective.

Present the failure as part of the portfolio, not as a confession. Its tone should be calm and factual, neither defensive nor apologetic.

## 7. Manage Your Self-Presentation in the Room

The board is forming an impression of you from how you speak and behave, not just from your content.

| Tactic | How It Applies |
|---|---|
| **Self-promotion (competence)** | Let data and results demonstrate competence. Avoid telling them how innovative you are. |
| **Exemplification** | Show discipline: know your numbers, respect time limits, acknowledge constraints. |
| **Ingratiation** | Be respectful but not deferential. Excessive flattery of board members looks political, especially in front of the CEO. |
| **Supplication** | Avoid. Don't ask the board for reassurance or signal uncertainty about the lab's future. |
| **Intimidation** | Avoid entirely. Never suggest disaster if the lab isn't funded. |

**Demeanor to aim for: calm, concise, credible.**
- **Lead with the answer.** Boards prefer conclusions first and supporting detail second.
- **Use plain language.** Say "We tested whether customers would use it" instead of "We validated product-market fit."
- **Keep slides simple.** Put detail in an appendix or pre-read.
- **Finish on time, or early.** Few things impress boards more.
- **Look to the CEO when appropriate.** Brief glances, handoffs, and references to his priorities reinforce that you're a team.

## 8. Handle Questions Strategically

Board Q&A is where impressions are most vulnerable.

### When You Know the Answer
Answer directly and briefly, then stop. Long answers often introduce unnecessary risk.

### When You Don't Know
> "I don't have that number with me. I'll follow up with the CEO's office by Friday."

Boards trust executives who admit limits. Guessing damages credibility quickly if you're wrong.

### When a Question Belongs to the CEO
Questions about company strategy, budgets beyond the lab, organizational changes, or board-level decisions should usually go to him:
> "That's part of the broader turnaround plan, so I'll let [CEO] speak to that."

### When a Board Member Is Skeptical
- Acknowledge the concern: "That's a fair question."
- Answer with evidence, not enthusiasm.
- If the concern is valid, say so: "You're right that it's too early to see revenue impact. The next milestone that will tell us is..."

### When a Board Member Invites You to Criticize the Organization
Some questions are traps, even when unintentional:
> "Why couldn't the existing product team have done this?"

Avoid any answer that implies failure by colleagues:
> "The product team has been focused on protecting reliability for current customers, which is critical. The lab gives us a way to test new ideas without putting that at risk, and the product team is now scaling two of the lab's successes."

### When You Disagree With Something the CEO Says
Unless it's a serious factual or ethical issue, **don't contradict him in the meeting.** Raise it privately afterward. Public disagreement damages both of your credibility and signals a divided leadership team.

If something materially inaccurate is stated, clarify gently:
> "To add some detail to that, the retention pilot covered five accounts so far, so we'll know more after the next phase."

## 9. Be Careful With Direct Board Relationships

Board members may approach you after the meeting, ask for your direct opinion, or suggest a separate conversation.

Be courteous and responsive, but **route follow-up through or with the knowledge of the CEO.** Building an independent line to the board without his awareness can look like positioning around him, even if that isn't your intent.

> "I'd be happy to share more. I'll coordinate with [CEO] on the best way to get you that information."

This protects your relationship with the CEO while showing the board you understand governance.

---

## 10. Follow Up After the Meeting

The performance continues after the board leaves.

**With the CEO (same day if possible):**
- Ask for candid feedback: "What landed well, and what should I change next time?"
- Review any concerns raised by board members.
- Agree on follow-up items and who owns them.
- Thank him for the opportunity. It's professional, not flattery.

**With your team and contributing managers:**
- Share relevant positive feedback, attributing credit to them.
- Let managers know their teams were recognized in front of the board, if true. This reinforces the promise you made that supporting the lab would make them look good.

**With the board (through the CEO's office):**
- Deliver promised follow-up information quickly and accurately.
