The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I want to know which approach wins approval and improves standing. Consider how each proposal would play inside and outside the company. Who gains status or credit, and who risks losing face or taking the blame? If each proposal went ahead, what would it signal to the board, employees, customers, and the public? Where might looking decisive or reassuring an audience matter more than operational results? What alliances and reputations would each proposal strengthen or threaten? And is there a third approach that wins greater support while letting the important players save face?

**Assistant:**

# Innovation Strategy: The Political Analysis

**Neither pure option is your best move.** The fully independent lab is probably closer to what the business needs operationally, but pitching it head-on as a new hire would force the CEO to concede his own thesis. The fully internal approach wins easy approval, but it tends to absorb innovation leaders and leave them holding the blame when results come slowly. The strongest position is a hybrid that is independent in practice but framed and owned as the CEO's strategy.

## Stakeholders and What They Want

| Player | What they care about | What they fear |
|---|---|---|
| **CEO** | A credible turnaround narrative he authored | Looking wrong to the board; losing control of the story |
| **Board** | Visible, decisive action and a timeline to results | Expensive "innovation theater" |
| **Exec peers** (CTO, CPO, Sales, CS, CFO) | Budget, headcount, credit for wins | Being implicitly labeled as the problem |
| **Middle managers** | Stability and their processes | Being bypassed or made irrelevant |
| **Employees** | Job security, belief that leadership has a plan | "Elite team" favoritism; being called the old guard |
| **Customers** (especially churning ones) | Fixes to the product they already pay for | The vendor chasing shiny objects while their issues rot |
| **Prospects / market / analysts** | Evidence of momentum and modernization | Instability at a vendor running core insurance systems |
| **You** | Early wins, a mandate, a protected runway | Getting absorbed, or being isolated and blamed |

## Option A: Work Within Existing Systems (the CEO's Thesis)

**Who gains:** The CEO's judgment is validated. Existing execs keep control and avoid being cast as the problem. Middle management is reassured.

**Who risks blame:** You. If results are incremental, which is the likely outcome given the same processes that produced the churn, the story becomes "we hired an innovation leader and nothing changed." You have the least tenure and the fewest allies, so you are the easiest to blame.

**Signals:**
- **Board:** Continuity and prudence. It is reassuring at first, but it may read as "no real change" if churn continues.
- **Employees:** "We're trusted." This is good for morale, but top performers who wanted change may conclude nothing will change.
- **Customers:** Stability. That is mildly positive, but they judge by product outcomes, not org charts.
- **Market:** Nothing newsworthy.

**Alliances:** It strengthens your relationship with the CEO and incumbent execs in the short term. It weakens your credibility with change-hungry employees and possibly with board members who pushed for your hire.

**Verdict:** Easy approval, poor long-term standing for you.

## Option B: Independent Innovation Lab

**Who gains:** You, if it works, since you get clear attribution. So do outside hires, and any board members who were skeptical of the incumbent team.

**Who loses face:**
- **The CEO.** Endorsing it concedes that "existing systems, procedures, and talent" can't do the job, which is the opposite of what he likely told the board.
- **CTO and CPO.** It implies their organizations are too slow.
- **Everyone left behind.** It implies they are the legacy.

**Who risks blame:** Still mostly you. Independent labs are highly visible, and when they struggle the organization is often quick to say "we told you so." Many corporate labs are shut down within a few years, and boards know that pattern.

**Signals:**
- **Board:** Boldness, but also cost and risk. Some directors will be skeptical of the "innovation lab" model.
- **Employees:** "The future is happening somewhere else." This can deepen disengagement at a company already under stress.
- **Customers:** This is split. Churning customers may read it as the vendor abandoning the core product. Insurance buyers are conservative and heavily regulated, so they value reliability over novelty.
- **Market:** A press release and short-term buzz, but analysts discount lab announcements until products ship.

**Alliances:** It creates immediate antibodies. The core organization controls data, customers, integration, and release pipelines, and a lab that depends on them while threatening them tends to get quietly starved.

**Verdict:** Possibly better for results, but high political cost and a real chance of rejection. Even if approved, you would start with enemies.

## Where Optics Matter vs. Where Results Matter

- **Optics dominate in the first 3–6 months.** The board wants to see decisive action, the CEO needs a narrative, and employees and customers need reassurance that someone has a plan.
- **Results dominate after about 6–12 months.** In a churning business, retention and win-rate numbers eventually overrule any story. A plan that looks decisive but doesn't move those numbers will expose you.

So you need something that **looks decisive and reassuring now** and is **structurally capable of real results later**. Neither pure option does both.

## Option C: A CEO-Sponsored Strike Team

Think of it as "a speedboat that tows the ship": autonomous in how it operates, embedded in who it serves and who gets credit.

**Structure:**
1. **Charter it under the CEO's name**, for example a "Customer Turnaround Team" or similar. Avoid the word "lab," which carries theater connotations.
2. **Staff it mostly with internal high performers on loan** from engineering, product, and customer success, plus two or three targeted outside hires. This preserves the CEO's "existing talent" thesis.
3. **Negotiate specific process exemptions in writing**: approval layers, release cadence, procurement limits, tooling. Ask for exemptions from particular processes rather than general independence. This gets you most of a separate lab's speed without the symbolism.
4. **Aim it at the CEO's stated pain first**: the top at-risk accounts, plus one new-business wedge. Co-develop with two or three customers as design partners. Churning customers who see their vendor building with them are a retention win and a reference story.
5. **Set short milestones and pre-agreed kill criteria**, for example a 90-day checkpoint. This makes it cheap to approve and limits blame exposure for everyone, including you.
6. **Build a graduation path.** Successful work transfers into core product teams, and those line leaders share credit.
7. **Earn more autonomy over time.** If it delivers, you can expand toward a more independent unit with a track record behind you.

**How it plays with each audience:**

| Audience | Signal |
|---|---|
| CEO | "My thesis works, and I made a bold move." He owns the story. |
| Board | Decisive action, small budget, clear milestones: bold but disciplined. |
| Exec peers | Their people are on the winning team, and they share credit on shipped work. |
| Employees | "Our people, freed up," not "outsiders replacing us." A loan to the team becomes a career opportunity. |
| Customers | "They're fixing things with us." |
| Market | Announce only after the first customer win, which gives you proof instead of promises. |

**Face-saving:** The CEO is right, the execs aren't the problem, and the processes aren't condemned, only selectively streamlined for a pilot.

**Your standing:** You own the method and the results, you are backed by the CEO, and you have allies instead of enemies.

## How to Win Approval

1. **Learn what the CEO told the board** before proposing anything. Frame the proposal as executing his commitment.
2. **Pre-wire key peers one-on-one.**
   - Head of Customer Success is likely your natural ally, because churn hurts them most.
   - The CFO will like the small budget and kill criteria.
   - Win over the CTO and CPO by offering co-ownership rather than competition.
3. **Pitch it to the CEO as a refinement of his idea:** "You're right that we have the talent. The problem is that our processes don't let them move fast enough. Let's free a small team from those constraints, point them at our top churn risks, and prove it in 90 days."
4. **Let the CEO announce it.** You gain more by being the person who delivered than by being the person who proposed.
5. **Secure an early, visible win**, ideally a saved account or a signed design partner, before expanding scope.

## One Caution

This is a sound political strategy, but it only holds if it produces real results. The hybrid works because it gives you the operational freedom to do so. If you negotiate too many compromises during pre-wiring, such as no real exemptions or people on loan only part-time, you will have Option A with a new name. Know your non-negotiables before you walk into those conversations: dedicated staff, specific process exemptions, and direct access to customers.

**Assistant:**

# Protecting Your Downside and Getting Visible Credit

Your concern is legitimate, but the asymmetry is smaller than it looks, and most of it can be managed.

## Reality Check: The Asymmetry Isn't Total

**Sponsorship cuts both ways.** If the CEO announces and owns the team, a disappointing checkpoint is partly his disappointment too. That gives him an incentive to frame setbacks as learning rather than failure, which protects you. An independent lab you championed alone would leave you far more exposed.

**Boards know who does the work.** Directors are used to CEOs fronting initiatives that others run. Their question is less "whose idea was this?" and more "who is the talent here?" They form that view from who presents the substance, who answers hard questions, and who the CEO relies on.

**Visible credit-seeking costs more than it gains.** In most organizations, people who are seen as generous with credit get promoted, and people who are seen as grabbing it get isolated. Your goal isn't to be credited with the win. It's to be understood as the person who made it happen. That reputation travels by different channels.

## Protecting Yourself at the 90-Day Checkpoint

Most of the blame risk is set before the checkpoint, by how success is defined.

**1. Define success in stages, in writing, before you start.**
- **Days 0–90:** Leading indicators you control, such as design partners signed, prototypes in customer hands, cycle time versus the normal process, and at-risk accounts re-engaged.
- **6–12 months:** Lagging business outcomes like retention, renewals, and win rate.

If the CEO agrees that 90 days means leading indicators, a slow revenue impact at day 90 isn't a failure. It's on plan.

**2. Get the criteria co-signed.** A one-page charter approved by the CEO, and ideally shared with the CFO, turns the checkpoint from a verdict on you into a joint review against agreed terms.

**3. Make the resource commitments part of the deal.** Write down which people, which process exemptions, and which customer access you were promised. If those don't materialize, the record shows why, and you can say so without sounding defensive.

**4. Under-promise publicly, over-deliver privately.** Keep official targets conservative. Share encouraging early signals with the CEO informally so he's never surprised and feels invested.

**5. Report early and often.** Give the CEO short updates every two weeks, including bad news. A CEO who has watched the problems unfold with you shares ownership of them. A CEO blindsided at day 90 looks for someone to blame.

**6. Frame the checkpoint as a decision, not a grade.** Present it as "here's what we learned, here's what we recommend: double down, pivot, or stop." Leaders who bring a clear-eyed recommendation, even to stop, usually gain credibility.

## Getting Board Visibility Without Upstaging

The principle: **the CEO owns the strategy, you own the substance.** Let him be the author while you become known as the expert.

**Ask him to bring you into the room.** Frame it as serving him: "Would it help if I walked the board through the operating detail so you're not fielding every tactical question?" Most CEOs welcome this. He introduces the initiative, and you present the specifics and take questions. Directors will draw their own conclusions about your capability.

**Let customers speak.** A quote or short video from a design-partner customer, with your team visible in the work, is far more persuasive to a board than self-promotion, and nobody can call it upstaging.

**Credit others publicly, by name.** Praising the engineers and product leads on loan builds peer alliances, and it signals leadership. The person handing out credit is understood to be the one running things.

**Put your name on the artifacts, not the headlines.** The charter, the operating model, the checkpoint reports, and the playbook for scaling the approach should carry your name, while the announcement and strategy carry his. Written material circulates and lasts longer than press releases.

**Keep board contact transparent.** If a director reaches out to you directly, take the conversation, tell the CEO it happened, and never position yourself against him. Back-channeling to the board is the fastest way to turn a sponsor into an adversary, and directors generally distrust executives who go around their CEO.

**Build reputation outside the company carefully.** Speaking or writing about the methodology, with his approval and with the company as the hero, builds your market value without internal friction.

## Warning Signs That the Deal Is Bad

These tactics assume a CEO who shares credit reasonably and doesn't reflexively shift blame. Watch for evidence otherwise:
- He resists putting success criteria in writing.
- He discourages you from ever presenting to the board.
- Resource commitments quietly erode while targets stay fixed.
- You hear secondhand that he's describing the team's work as solely his.
- Past innovation or transformation leaders at the company left under a cloud. Ask around about your predecessors.

If you see these patterns, raise role clarity directly and early, for example: "I want to make sure we're aligned on how my role is represented to the board." You may also need to accept that this organization isn't a place where you can build standing. It's better to learn that at month three than at month eighteen.

## Summary

- **Before launch:** A written charter with staged success criteria and resource commitments, co-signed by the CEO.
- **During:** Frequent, candid updates so ownership is shared.
- **At the board:** He presents the strategy, you present the substance, and customers provide the proof.
- **Always:** Be generous with credit and transparent with the CEO. That makes your role obvious without making you a threat.

## Turn 3

**User:** Won't pushing a brand-new boss to co-sign a charter documenting his resource promises look like I'm building a paper trail to protect myself, and how do I get that protection without signaling distrust in my first months?

**Assistant:**

# Getting Clarity Without Signaling Distrust

Yes, if you present it as protection, it will read as distrust. A new hire who asks the CEO to "sign off on his commitments" sounds like someone preparing for a dispute. The fix isn't to skip the documentation. It's to change its purpose, form, and tone so it serves him as much as you.

## Reframe the Purpose: Alignment, Not Accountability

Most of the real protection comes from **preventing misunderstanding**, not from having evidence afterward. In organizational politics, a paper trail rarely wins a dispute. By the time you're pointing to documents, you've usually lost the relationship. What actually protects you is that you and the CEO have the same picture of success from the start, so there's no gap for blame to fall into.

So treat the document as a tool for getting aligned quickly, which is something any new executive is expected to do.

## Make It Serve Him

Position the document around his needs:

- **Getting it right:** "I want to make sure I'm executing on what you actually want, not my interpretation of it."
- **Board readiness:** "It would be useful to have a one-page summary you can use with the board."
- **Speed:** "If I have clarity on scope and resources, I won't need to come back to you for approvals every week."
- **Onboarding:** "As the new person, I'd like to check that I've understood the priorities correctly."

Each of these is true, and each makes the document something he benefits from rather than something he's bound by.

## Change the Form: Drop the Signatures

A co-signed charter feels like a contract. You can get nearly all the benefit through lighter formats.

**1. The recap email.** After a planning conversation, send a short note:

> "Thanks for the time today. Here's what I took away, so I can get moving. Correct anything I've got wrong:
> - Focus: top 10 at-risk accounts plus one new-business pilot
> - Team: [names/roles], full-time for the first 90 days
> - Process: we'll use the lightweight release path we discussed
> - At 90 days, we'll review design partners signed, prototypes in customer hands, and account re-engagement, then decide whether to expand."

This is standard practice. It reads as diligent, not defensive, and if he doesn't correct it, it becomes the shared understanding.

**2. The working plan.** Draft a one-page plan and ask for his input: "Here's a first draft of the plan. What would you change?" If he edits it, he owns it more than any signature would make him.

**3. The board brief.** If the plan becomes the basis for his board update, the commitments are recorded where they matter most, and he authored them.

## Lead With Your Commitments, Not His

Tone depends heavily on order. If the document opens with what he's promising you, it feels like a demand. If it opens with what you'll deliver, it feels like accountability.

Structure it as:
1. The goal (in his words)
2. What the team will deliver, and when
3. How we'll measure progress
4. What the team needs to deliver it (resources, process flexibility, customer access)
5. When we'll review and decide next steps

Resources then appear as inputs to your plan, which is standard, rather than as promises you're holding him to.

## Watch the Language

Avoid wording that sounds legal or defensive:

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Per our agreement…" | "Here's what I took away…" |
| "You committed to…" | "The plan assumes…" |
| "Resource guarantees" | "What we'll need" |
| "Sign-off" | "Does this match your thinking?" |
| Copying HR or several execs | Send it to him alone first |

Share it more widely only after he's comfortable with it, and preferably let him do the sharing.

## Let the Rhythm Do the Protecting

The strongest protection is the habit you set up, not the single document. A short update every week or two, covering progress, risks, and anything you need from him, does several things at once:
- It creates an ongoing record without looking like you're building one.
- It surfaces problems early, such as a loaned engineer being pulled back, while they're still easy to fix.
- It makes him a participant in the work, so setbacks become shared problems.

When a resource commitment slips, raise it in the next update as a problem to solve together: "We've lost two of the four engineers to the release crunch. That will push the prototype about three weeks. Do you want to adjust the timeline, or can we get them back?" That's documentation in the form of good management.

## Reading His Response

How he reacts is useful information:
- **He engages and edits:** Good sign. He's investing.
- **He says "no need, just go":** Common with busy CEOs. Send the recap email anyway, framed as notes for yourself: "Just so I have it written down."
- **He actively resists anything in writing or keeps things vague:** Pay attention. Vagueness keeps options open, including the option to reframe results later. It doesn't mean bad intent, but it's a reason to lean harder on the regular updates.

## Summary

Protection that looks like protection signals distrust. Protection that looks like good execution signals competence. Write it yourself, frame it around his goals, lead with your commitments, skip the signatures, and let regular updates build the record naturally.

# Bringing the CTO and CPO In Without Diluting the CEO

Yes, this is a real risk, and arguably the most common way strike teams fail. Initiatives rarely get killed openly. They get starved: a loaned engineer is needed "just this week" for a release, then never fully comes back. The people controlling your resources have to feel like partners, not donors.

The good news is that giving them a stake doesn't take anything from the CEO, because each of you owns something different.

## Separate the Ownership Layers

| Player | Owns | Gets |
|---|---|---|
| **CEO** | The strategy and sponsorship ("why we're doing this") | The turnaround narrative, the board story |
| **CTO / CPO** | Their people and the long-term product ("who does it, where it lands") | Talent development, product wins that graduate into their roadmaps, credit for enabling it |
| **You** | Execution ("how it gets done") | Results and a reputation for delivering |

When the layers are clear, involving the CTO and CPO doesn't compete with the CEO's ownership. It supports it, because a CEO whose initiative has his senior team behind it looks stronger to the board than one pushing it alone.

## Get the CEO's Blessing to Pre-Wire

Going around someone means acting without their knowledge. So before finalizing the plan, ask the CEO:

> "Before we lock this in, I'd like to sit down with [CTO] and [CPO]. Their people are the core of the team, and I want them invested rather than just complying. Does that work for you?"

Almost any CEO will say yes, and the request makes you look politically sensible. Once he's agreed, your conversations with them are part of his plan.

**Then meet them before any announcement.** Nobody should learn from an all-hands that their best engineers are being reassigned. Being consulted before it's final is the difference between feeling bypassed and feeling respected.

## Give Them Real Stakes, Not Courtesy Updates

Keeping them informed isn't enough. They need reasons to want the team to succeed.

**1. Ask what they need.** "What problem would you most want this team to crack?" If the strike team can tackle something on their list, such as a product gap the CPO has been pushing or a technical approach the CTO wants to prove, the team becomes a vehicle for their goals.

**2. Let them shape who goes.** Ask them to recommend people rather than you picking their best performers. They'll feel respected, and they'll be less likely to pull back people they chose.

**3. Frame the loan as career development.** "Your people will come back with direct customer experience and a record of shipping fast." Managers who can offer stretch assignments keep strong people longer.

**4. Promise a landing path.** Work that succeeds graduates into their product roadmap, and they get credit for the result. The CPO is then a future owner of your output, not a competitor.

**5. Put them in front of the board, too.** When results are shown, their teams' contributions should be visible and named.

## Agree the Loan Terms Up Front

Most quiet pullbacks happen because nobody agreed what happens during a crunch. Settle it directly with each of them, briefly and informally:

- **Duration:** Full-time for 90 days, reviewed at the checkpoint.
- **Backfill:** Is their team covering the gap, or does it need contractors or rebalancing?
- **Emergency recall:** "If you truly need someone back for a production issue, just tell me directly, and we'll figure it out together." This gives them a legitimate channel, so they don't pull people back quietly.
- **Return:** How and when people rejoin their teams, and how their performance reviews will reflect the strike team work.

That last point matters more than it seems. If loaned engineers worry their home manager will review them poorly for being away, they'll drift back on their own.

## Set Up Communication in Tiers

Give each person the information relevant to what they own:

- **CEO:** Strategic updates on progress against goals, decisions needed, and risks to the plan.
- **CTO and CPO:** Operational updates on how their people are doing, what's being built, anything that touches their systems or roadmap, and upcoming resource needs.
- **Monthly joint review:** A short session with all three, chaired by the CEO. This is where the CEO's ownership shows. He runs the meeting, sets direction, and settles tradeoffs, while the CTO and CPO participate as senior stakeholders.

Tell the CEO you're doing this: "I'm keeping [CTO] and [CPO] updated on the operational side so resource issues get solved before they reach you." That frames it as protecting his time, which is true.

## Handle Conflicts With Them First

When a resource problem comes up, and it will, go to the CTO or CPO directly before involving the CEO:

> "We've lost Priya to the release crunch for two weeks now. I understand why, but it's putting the pilot at risk. Can we find a way to get her back, or get someone else, by next week?"

Escalate to the CEO only if you can't solve it together, and when you do, present it as a tradeoff for him to decide, not a complaint: "We have a conflict between the release and the pilot. [CTO] and I see it differently. How would you like us to prioritize?"

Going over a peer's head quickly is the fastest way to turn them into an opponent. Solving problems together first builds trust.

## Credit Them Generously

- In updates, name loaned people and their home teams: "Thanks to [CTO]'s team, specifically Priya and Sam, we cut integration time from six weeks to eight days."
- When the CEO presents results, suggest he highlight both leaders.
- When work moves into the core product, make sure the receiving team gets credit for scaling it.

Being generous with credit costs you little. Everyone with any sense knows who ran the team, and the CTO and CPO will want to work with you again.

## Watch for Quiet Pullback

- Loaned people start missing strike team meetings or mentioning "priorities from my manager."
- Requests to the CTO or CPO get slower responses.
- Their updates about the team turn noncommittal.
- A loaned engineer's manager starts scheduling one-on-ones about their "return plan" early.

When you see these, talk to the leader directly and early. Usually there's an unmet concern behind it, such as feeling out of the loop, backlog pressure, or worry about their team's standing, and it's easier to address before it hardens.

## Summary

The CEO owns why, the CTO and CPO own who and where it lands, and you own how. Get the CEO's go-ahead to involve them, consult them before anything is final, give them real stakes, agree loan terms up front, settle conflicts with them before escalating, and credit them publicly. That protects your resources and strengthens the CEO's position.

# What to Do: The Plan in One Place

**Recommendation:** Build the CEO-sponsored strike team, aim it at churn first, and earn more independence by delivering. Don't pitch the independent lab now. Treat it as where you may end up, not where you start.

One thing matters more than all the political advice: **your standing will ultimately depend on whether customers stop leaving.** The stakeholder work exists to buy you the room to fix that. So the plan starts with understanding the problem, not with the org chart.

## Phase 1: Listen (Weeks 1–4)

Build credibility by understanding the problem better than anyone else, before you propose anything.

1. **Talk to customers directly.** Aim for 15–20 conversations: recently churned customers, at-risk accounts, and a few deals you lost. Ask why they left, what almost made them stay, and who won and why. Hardly anyone in a large company does this systematically, and it will give you authority no one can dispute.
2. **Meet every key executive one-on-one.** CTO, CPO, heads of Sales, Customer Success, and Finance. Ask what they think is causing churn, what they've tried, and what gets in the way. You'll learn where the real bottlenecks are and who wants change.
3. **Learn the CEO's commitments.** What has he told the board about the turnaround, and on what timeline? Your proposal has to fit that story.
4. **Map the obstacles.** Which specific processes slow things down, such as release cycles, approval layers, procurement, or data access? Your exemption requests should target these exactly.
5. **Ask about your predecessors.** If earlier transformation or innovation leaders failed, find out why.

**Output:** A clear diagnosis of why customers leave and why the organization can't respond quickly. That diagnosis is the foundation of your pitch.

## Phase 2: Design and Pre-Wire (Weeks 4–6)

1. **Test the concept with the CEO informally**, framed around his thesis: "You're right that we have the talent. What's holding them back is process. Let me show you what customers told me and what I think we can do."
2. **Get his go-ahead to involve the CTO and CPO** before anything is final.
3. **Co-design with the CTO and CPO.** Ask what problem they'd want solved, let them recommend people, and agree loan terms, backfill, and recall rules.
4. **Enlist the head of Customer Success** as your closest ally. They feel churn most, and they can open doors to at-risk accounts.
5. **Line up two or three design-partner customers** from your Phase 1 conversations, ideally accounts at risk of leaving.
6. **Draft a one-page plan** covering the goal, deliverables, 90-day leading indicators, resource needs, and review date. Send it to the CEO for his edits.

## Phase 3: Launch (Week 6–7)

- **The CEO announces it**, as his initiative, with the CTO and CPO visibly supportive.
- **Call it something plain and customer-focused**, not a "lab."
- **Don't issue a press release.** Save external announcements for your first customer win.

## Phase 4: Execute (Days 0–90)

- **Ship something real to design partners fast.** Aim for weeks, not months. Speed compared with the normal process is itself your proof point.
- **Save at least one at-risk account** that can be credibly tied to the team's work. That's your most valuable early win.
- **Update the CEO every two weeks**, candidly, including bad news. Update the CTO and CPO on the operational side. Hold a monthly joint review chaired by the CEO.
- **Settle resource conflicts directly with peers first.** Escalate to the CEO only as a tradeoff for him to decide.
- **Credit loaned people and their home teams by name**, consistently.
- **Document the operating model as you go**: what was different, what made it faster. That playbook is how the approach scales, and it's your lasting contribution.

## Phase 5: Checkpoint and Next Step (Day 90)

Present it as a decision meeting, not a grade. Show results against the agreed leading indicators, what you learned, and a recommendation:

- **If it's working:** Expand. Add a second team or problem, bring the first wins into the core product, and negotiate more autonomy. This is when a more independent unit becomes realistic, because you've shown results rather than argued for it.
- **If it's partly working:** Adjust the focus, fix resource problems, and set a 60-day follow-up.
- **If it isn't working:** Say so plainly and recommend stopping or pivoting. A clear-eyed recommendation to stop protects your credibility far better than defending a failing effort.

Aim to be the one to walk the board through the substance at this point, at the CEO's invitation.

## Your Non-Negotiables

Hold firm on these during pre-wiring, because without them the strike team is the old approach with a new name:

1. **Dedicated people**, full-time rather than fractional
2. **Specific written process exemptions**
3. **Direct access to customers**
4. **A clear review date with agreed criteria**

If you can't get these, you've learned something important about whether this organization will let you succeed. Better to find that out in month two than month twelve.

## Summary

Spend your first month learning why customers leave. Then propose a small, fast team as the CEO's initiative, staffed by internal talent the CTO and CPO help choose, pointed at the accounts you're about to lose. Deliver something real within 90 days, share credit widely, and use the results to earn the independence you originally wanted.

Working purely within existing processes would likely produce incremental results and leave the newcomer blamed. Above all, standing ultimately depends on whether customers stop leaving.

The plan has five phases:

1. **Weeks 1–4:** Hold 15–20 conversations with churned, at-risk and lost-deal customers. Meet each key executive one-on-one. Learn what the CEO promised the board, identify which specific processes slow work down, and find out why predecessors failed.
2. **Weeks 4–6:** Frame the proposal to the CEO as executing his thesis. Get his approval to involve the CTO and CPO, and co-design the team with them, including who is loaned, backfill and recall terms. Enlist the head of Customer Success, line up two or three at-risk design-partner customers, and send the CEO a one-page plan to edit.
3. **Launch:** The CEO announces the team under a plain, customer-focused name, not a "lab," and without a press release.
4. **First 90 days:** Ship to design partners within weeks, save at least one at-risk account, and update stakeholders candidly. Settle resource conflicts with peers before escalating, credit contributors by name, and document the operating model.
5. **Day 90:** Hold a decision meeting that recommends expanding, adjusting or stopping.

The key caveat is to hold firm on four non-negotiables: full-time dedicated people, specific written process exemptions, direct customer access, and a clear review date with agreed criteria. Without them, the strike team is the old approach renamed, and failing to secure them reveals whether the organization will allow success.
