## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

You're both partly right, and framing it as a binary choice is the real trap. The better question is: **which problems need which approach?**

## Start with the problem, not the structure

Your company has two distinct problems:

1. **Churning customers.** This is almost always a core product problem: reliability, implementation pain, missing features, poor service, outdated UX, slow release cycles. An independent lab won't fix this. Customers leaving today need the existing product and organization to improve.
2. **Losing new business.** This could be a core problem (you're behind competitors on table stakes) or a positioning problem (the market is shifting toward cloud-native, API-first, embedded insurance, or AI-driven underwriting and claims, and you're not there). The latter may need something new.

Before picking a structure, find out why customers leave and why you lose deals. Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, and the sales team. The answer will tell you how much of your mandate is fixing the core versus building something new.

## Where the CEO is right

- **Isolated labs have a poor track record.** Many corporate innovation labs become "innovation theater": nice demos, prototypes that never ship, and resentment from the core business. The common failure is the handoff, when the lab builds something the core organization can't or won't adopt.
- **The core holds your real advantages.** In insurance software, those are customer relationships, carrier trust, domain expertise, regulatory knowledge, data, integrations, and a sales channel. A startup-style lab without these is just a poorly funded startup.
- **Near-term survival depends on the core.** If churn continues, there may be no company left to benefit from a lab's 3-year bet.

## Where you're right

- **Large organizations reject new things.** Existing processes, incentives, budgeting cycles, and risk aversion are optimized for the current business. Genuinely new products often get starved, slowed, or bent back toward the old model.
- **"Outsized results" rarely come from existing procedures.** If the current system could produce breakthrough results, it probably already would have.
- **Some talent and speed you need may not exist internally**, and new people need protection from the organization's defaults.

## What tends to work: structured ambidexterity

The research on this (O'Reilly and Tushman's work on "ambidextrous organizations" is the best starting point) suggests a hybrid:

- **Separate where it matters:** a small dedicated team with its own processes, metrics, hiring, and budget, protected from core-business quarterly pressures.
- **Integrate where it matters:** tight links to the core's customers, sales, data, and domain experts, with senior executive sponsorship (ideally the CEO) to resolve conflicts.
- **Plan the path back:** decide up front how successful experiments get scaled, whether through the core's distribution, as a new product line, or by spinning out.
- **Use different metrics:** judge the new team on learning velocity, customer validation, and pilot conversions, not revenue in year one.

A practical version for you might look like this:

1. **Track 1 (improve the core):** use innovation methods such as rapid customer discovery, experimentation, and small cross-functional teams to attack the top churn drivers inside the existing organization. This builds credibility and shows the CEO you respect his thesis.
2. **Track 2 (explore the new):** a small, semi-autonomous team working on 2–3 bets tied to why you're losing new business, co-developed with a few friendly customers.

## A political note

You're new, and the CEO has a stated belief. Opening with "we need to go around the organization" risks being seen as dismissing him and alienating the leaders whose cooperation you'll need. A stronger path:

- Spend your first 60–90 days diagnosing churn and lost deals, and building relationships.
- Deliver a visible win in the core early.
- Then propose the protected team as an extension of his strategy ("working within our talent and systems, with a small unit that has room to move faster"), backed by the data you've gathered.

## Questions worth answering early

- What are the top three reasons customers leave? Are they fixable in the current product?
- Who is winning the deals you lose, and why?
- Is the CEO's resistance to a separate lab about cost, culture, past failed attempts, or control?
- What budget, headcount, and authority do you actually have?
- What does "outsized results" mean to him, and on what timeline?

## Turn 2

**User:** How do I find out why customers are actually leaving when I'm brand new and don't have relationships with anyone yet?

**Assistant:**

Being new is actually an advantage here. You have permission to ask naive questions, you carry no baggage from past decisions, and people will often tell a newcomer things they've stopped saying to colleagues. Use that window. It closes in a few months.

## 1. Get a mandate first

Ask the CEO to send a short note introducing you and saying you're spending your first weeks understanding customers and why they leave. That one email opens doors internally and with customers. It also signals you're taking his "work within the organization" view seriously.

## 2. Mine what already exists (week 1–2)

Before talking to anyone, gather the data so your conversations are sharper:

- **Churn list:** who left in the last 2–3 years, contract size, tenure, product, and segment (carrier size, line of business, MGA vs. carrier, etc.)
- **CRM notes** on lost renewals and lost deals
- **Support tickets** for churned accounts in their final 6–12 months. Look at volume, severity, and unresolved issues.
- **NPS/CSAT surveys, QBR decks, and account health scores**, if they exist
- **Implementation history:** how long go-lives took and how many ran over
- **Finance data:** downgrades, discounting at renewal, late payments

Look for patterns. Did churned accounts share a segment, product version, or implementation partner? Did they complain about the same things?

## 3. Talk to the people closest to customers (week 1–4)

Start with those who feel churn most directly. They usually know the answer and are frustrated nobody asks.

- **Customer success and account managers:** "Walk me through the last three accounts you lost. When did you first know?"
- **Support leads:** "What issues come up again and again that never get fixed?"
- **Implementation and professional services:** "Where do projects go wrong?"
- **Sales:** "What do prospects say about our reputation? What do competitors say about us?"
- **Product and engineering:** "What do you know is broken but can't get prioritized?"

Useful questions for everyone:
- "If you were CEO, what would you fix first?"
- "What's the thing everyone knows but nobody says in meetings?"
- "Who else should I talk to?" This builds your network fast.

Frame it as learning, not auditing. Churn is sensitive, and people may worry about blame. Make clear you're looking for system problems, not culprits.

## 4. Talk to customers directly (week 3–8)

This is the most valuable step and the one most often skipped.

**Former customers.** Aim for 8–15 conversations. Many will talk, especially if you're new and explicitly not trying to win them back. Something like: "I've just joined to lead innovation, and I want to understand honestly where we fell short. This isn't a sales call." Offer 30 minutes.

**At-risk current customers.** Ask customer success which accounts are wobbling and join calls or request a direct conversation.

**Healthy customers.** They tell you what's worth keeping and what nearly made them leave.

**Lost prospects.** These explain the new-business problem.

In insurance software, buying decisions involve several people. Try to talk to more than one role per account: an executive sponsor, IT, and operational users such as underwriters, claims handlers, or policy admin staff. They often leave for different reasons.

## 5. How to run the interviews

The stated reason for leaving ("price," "strategic direction") is rarely the real one. Dig into the story:

- "Take me back to when you first started considering alternatives. What was happening?"
- "What had you tried before deciding to leave?"
- "What did the new vendor offer that made the switch worth the pain?" Switching core insurance systems is painful, so something strong pushed them.
- "Was there a specific moment you lost confidence in us?"
- "What would we have needed to do to keep you?"

Listen more than you talk, and don't defend the company. If they say "price," ask "compared to what value?" Price complaints usually mean the value wasn't there.

## 6. Synthesize and share back

After 20–30 conversations, patterns will be obvious. Write a short, evidence-based summary with the top 3–5 churn drivers, supported by quotes and data, plus which ones are fixable and by whom.

Share it first with the people who helped you, then with the CEO and leadership. This does three things:
- It shows the people you interviewed that you listened, which builds trust.
- It gives you a fact-based foundation for your innovation agenda.
- It positions you as someone who understands the business, not an outsider with a theory.

## A realistic timeline

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | CEO intro, gather data, first internal conversations |
| 2–4 | Broad internal interviews, identify customers to contact |
| 3–8 | Customer and ex-customer interviews |
| 8–10 | Synthesize, validate with key people, present findings |

One caution: you'll probably hear some uncomfortable truths about specific teams or leaders. Present findings as patterns rather than pointing at individuals. You'll need those people's help to fix things.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if the churned customers won't take my call, or the account managers get defensive because they think I'm looking for someone to blame?

**Assistant:**

Both are common, and both are manageable. Here's how to handle each.

## If churned customers won't talk

First, understand why they might refuse. They may feel burned and see no upside, they may be busy with their new system, they may suspect a win-back pitch, or there may be an unresolved billing or contract dispute. Check that last one with finance or legal before reaching out, so you don't walk into a live disagreement.

**Change the messenger.** A cold email from a stranger is easy to ignore. Better options:
- A note from the CEO: "I've asked [you] to understand where we let you down. I'd be grateful if you'd give them 20 minutes."
- An introduction from the former account manager or executive sponsor they actually liked
- Someone in your network who knows them. Insurance is a small industry.

**Make the ask smaller and safer.**
- Offer 15–20 minutes, not an hour.
- Say plainly that this isn't a sales call and you won't try to win them back.
- Offer alternatives: three questions by email, or a short anonymous survey.
- Offer something in return, such as a summary of what you learned or simply a genuine acknowledgment that you want to fix what went wrong. Many people respond to being heard.

**Try different people at the account.** The executive who signed the termination may not engage, but a former power user or IT lead might. Look on LinkedIn for people who've since moved companies. They're often the most candid because they no longer have a stake.

**Use a neutral third party.** Win/loss firms such as Clozd or Primary Intelligence interview churned customers and lost prospects for you. People are often more honest with an outsider than with the vendor. If budget allows, this is worth it.

**Go around them if needed.** You can learn a lot without churned customers:
- **Implementation partners and system integrators** who helped customers switch away from you. They know exactly why, and they talk to everyone.
- **Industry analysts** who cover insurance software (Celent, Datos Insights, Gartner). They hear candid vendor comparisons constantly.
- **Review sites** like Gartner Peer Insights and G2
- **Former employees** from customer success, support, or sales
- **At-risk current customers.** They're still talking to you, and their frustrations likely mirror those of the ones who left.
- **Lost prospects**, who often reveal your reputation in the market

You don't need dozens of conversations. Even 5–8 honest ones, combined with internal data, usually reveal clear patterns.

## If account managers get defensive

Their fear is often rational. They may have been blamed for churn before, their compensation may be tied to retention, or a previous "initiative" may have ended in layoffs. Take that seriously rather than dismissing it.

**Watch how you're introduced.** If the CEO's email says you're "investigating churn," people will hear "audit." Aim for something closer to: "understanding what our customer-facing teams need to keep customers successful."

**Name the concern directly, then prove it through actions.** Say something like, "I'm not here to evaluate anyone. My hunch is that most churn comes from things account managers can't control, like product gaps, bugs, and slow implementations. I want to understand those so we can fix them." Then behave consistently. Trust comes from what you do next, not from the reassurance.

**Ask questions that put them on the side of the customer:**
- "What did you need from the company to save that account that you couldn't get?"
- "Where did you escalate, and what happened?"
- "What promises did customers get that the product couldn't deliver?"

These questions position them as the experts who saw the problem coming, not as the cause.

**Set clear rules and keep them:**
- Findings are reported as patterns, never attributed to individuals.
- You won't share individual comments with their managers.
- They see the findings before leadership does.

**Make them partners, not subjects.**
- Invite them to join customer conversations with you.
- Ask them to help you interpret patterns.
- Credit their insights publicly when you present.

**Deliver something quickly.** If an account manager tells you about a recurring issue, like a report that never works or a process that makes renewals painful, try to get it fixed or escalated within weeks. Nothing builds trust faster than showing that talking to you leads to results. Word spreads.

**Start with the willing.** Every team has one or two people who are frustrated and eager to talk. Begin with them. Their positive experience will reassure the others.

## A caveat

Occasionally, defensiveness signals a real performance problem. You may hear that a particular person mishandled accounts. Don't become the channel for that feedback. If it matters, note it privately, raise it carefully with the appropriate leader if necessary, and keep your research focused on systems. If you're seen as the person who gets people in trouble, your ability to learn anything will vanish.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if the CEO wants results fast and sees spending two months on interviews as stalling?

**Assistant:**

He has a point. Two months of pure listening is a long time in a turnaround, and a new innovation leader who disappears into interviews can look like someone avoiding commitment. The fix isn't to skip discovery. It's to **make discovery fast, visible, and paired with action from week one.**

## Find out what "fast" means to him

Before adjusting your plan, ask him directly:
- "What would you need to see in 30, 60, and 90 days to feel we're on track?"
- "Is there a specific customer, deal, or renewal you're worried about right now?"
- "What's driving the urgency: the board, a big renewal, cash, competitors?"

"Fast" might mean visible activity, a board-ready story, saving a specific account, or revenue impact. Each calls for a different response. You may find he'd be satisfied with weekly progress and one early win, not a finished strategy.

## Compress the timeline

Most of the insight comes early. You can get to useful findings in 2–3 weeks by running things in parallel:

| Week | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | Pull churn data and support tickets, run 8–10 internal conversations, form initial hypotheses |
| 2 | First 5–6 customer conversations, test hypotheses, identify 1–2 quick fixes |
| 3 | Share preliminary findings with the CEO and launch first actions |
| 4–8 | Keep interviewing while acting, refining as you go |

The difference is that discovery continues in the background instead of being a gate before anything happens.

## Make progress visible

- **Send a weekly update**: a short email with what you learned, what surprised you, and what you're doing about it. It shows momentum and keeps him involved.
- **Lead with hypotheses early.** In week one, say, "Based on the data, I suspect churn is driven by X, Y, and Z. Here's how I'm testing that." This shows you're thinking, not just collecting.
- **Bring him into it.** Invite him to join one or two customer calls. Hearing a frustrated ex-customer directly is often more persuasive than any report, and he'll stop seeing it as stalling.

## Deliver early wins alongside discovery

Look for quick, concrete actions you can take within weeks:
- **Save an at-risk account.** Pick one or two renewals in danger, join the calls, and remove obstacles. Nothing earns credibility faster than helping retain revenue.
- **Fix a recurring irritant** that account managers and support keep raising, like a broken report or a painful workflow.
- **Help win a live deal** by joining sales calls, understanding objections, and bringing product or engineering resources to address them.

These wins buy you time and trust for the deeper work.

## Frame discovery as de-risking, not delay

When talking to him, frame it in business terms:

> "I want to move fast, and I'm starting action this week. But if I launch big initiatives before understanding why customers leave, we risk spending six months and serious money on the wrong thing. In three weeks I'll bring you our top churn drivers with evidence, plus the first fixes already underway. In the meantime, I'm working on [specific at-risk account or deal]."

This shows urgency, respects his concern, and makes the case that a short investment in understanding prevents a costly mistake.

## What to avoid

- **Defending the two-month plan as necessary.** It looks inflexible.
- **Jumping to big initiatives just to look busy.** A flashy project aimed at the wrong problem will hurt you more later.
- **Going quiet.** Silence reads as stalling even when you're working hard.

The CEO needs confidence that you're moving. You need enough understanding to avoid expensive mistakes. A compressed, visible, action-paired plan gives you both.

## The short version

**Don't build the independent lab yet. Earn the right to build it.** Spend the next 90 days attacking churn inside the existing organization, as the CEO wants, while gathering the evidence to decide whether, where, and how a protected team is needed. If the case is there, propose it in month three with data and credibility behind you.

This isn't abandoning your instinct. It's sequencing it. A lab proposed on day 30 by a new hire looks like a bet against the CEO. A lab proposed on day 90 by the person who helped save key accounts and can explain exactly why you're losing deals looks like the obvious next step.

## The 90-day plan

**Weeks 1–3: Understand the problem, visibly**
- Meet the CEO to agree on what "results" means at 30, 60, and 90 days.
- Get his introduction email out, framed around helping customers succeed, not investigating churn.
- Pull churn data, support tickets, and lost-deal records, and form initial hypotheses in week one.
- Talk to account managers, support, implementation, and sales, starting with the most willing.
- Begin customer and ex-customer conversations, using warm introductions and partners or analysts where needed.
- Send the CEO a short weekly update.
- By week 3, present the top churn drivers with evidence, plus the first actions underway.

**Weeks 3–8: Deliver wins in the core**
- Pick one or two at-risk renewals and help save them.
- Fix one or two recurring problems that customer-facing teams keep raising.
- Join live sales pursuits to understand why you're losing and to help win one.
- Keep interviewing in the background, especially lost prospects.
- Credit the account managers and teams who helped, publicly.

**Weeks 8–12: Decide on the structure**

By now you'll know whether your problems are mostly:
- **Core execution problems** (product quality, implementation, service): fix them inside the organization, using small cross-functional teams and faster experimentation. A lab would be a distraction.
- **Market-shift problems** (competitors offering something fundamentally different that your organization can't build within its current processes): propose a small, protected team with its own methods and metrics, tightly linked to the core's customers, sales, and domain experts, and sponsored directly by the CEO.
- **Both**, which is most likely: a two-track plan with core improvement plus a small exploration team working on 2–3 validated bets.

- **Retention first.** Keeping current customers is the fastest route to results and the foundation for everything else.
- **Move fast and show your work.** Weekly updates, early hypotheses, and visible action.
- **Make allies, not suspects.** Customer-facing teams are your best source of insight and your future partners.
- **Let evidence decide the structure.** Your conviction about a lab might be right, but prove it.
