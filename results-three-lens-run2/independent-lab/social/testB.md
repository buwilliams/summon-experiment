## Turn 1

**User:** You are a world-class sociologist who studies status and reputation.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Neither, in its pure form. But the more important point is that **your status as a new hire may matter more than the org chart.** Here is how I'd think about it through the lens of status and reputation.

## 1. The question isn't really "lab vs. no lab"

The research on organizational innovation (O'Reilly and Tushman on ambidexterity, Govindarajan and Trimble on "the other side of innovation") points to a consistent pattern:

- **Fully embedded innovation** gets crushed by the core business's metrics, risk aversion, and resource priorities. New ideas are judged by old standards and lose.
- **Fully independent labs** produce interesting things that never get adopted. Xerox PARC is the classic cautionary tale: it invented the modern personal computer, and Xerox didn't capture the value.

What tends to work is **structural separation plus deliberate integration**: a dedicated team with its own metrics, budget, and permission to fail, tightly linked to the core through shared leadership, shared staff, and a clear path for handing off what works.

So you're both partly right. The CEO is right that the existing organization has to absorb and scale whatever you build. You're right that new ideas need protection from the core's immune system.

## 2. The status dynamics you're walking into

This is where I'd focus most of your attention.

**You have formal authority but little informal status.** You're new, you were hired from outside, and your mandate implicitly says the current people haven't been getting it done. Many insiders will read that as a status threat, even if nobody says so.

**An independent lab amplifies that threat.** When you wall off a group, give it a cool name, a different dress code, and a mandate to "innovate," you create a visible status hierarchy. The message received by the core is "the interesting work happens over there, and you're the legacy." Predictable responses follow:
- "Innovation theater" labeling, which becomes a reputational stigma that's hard to shake
- Withholding of the data, customer access, and engineering help you need
- Quiet satisfaction when the lab stumbles

**Openly contradicting the CEO costs you legitimacy.** He has publicly bet on existing talent. If you frame your proposal as "your approach won't work," you've made it a contest between you, and new hires rarely win those. If the lab later struggles, it becomes *your* failed idea rather than a shared experiment.

**The core holds the status that comes from revenue.** In a company that's losing customers, the people keeping current accounts alive have moral authority. Anything that looks like it's diverting resources from them will be resented.

## 3. Look at the problem itself

Churn and weak new-business wins in insurance software are often **core-product and service problems**: painful implementations, slow configuration, integration headaches, poor support, and a reputation for being hard to work with. Carriers are risk-averse buyers. They care enormously about reliability and a vendor's track record.

A separate lab building the next big thing may do nothing for churn. Customers aren't leaving because you lack a moonshot. They're usually leaving because something about the current experience is failing them. If that's the case, the CEO's instinct to fix things through the existing organization is more right than it might appear.

Before choosing a structure, spend your first 60–90 days talking to churned customers, at-risk customers, lost prospects, and frontline staff. Let the diagnosis determine the structure.

## 4. What I'd recommend

**Frame it in the CEO's language.** Something like: "I want to build a small dedicated team *made of our best people* to move fast on our biggest customer problems." That honors his belief in existing talent while getting you the protection you need. The structure can be the same; the framing changes everything.

**Borrow status from respected insiders.** Recruit a few people the organization already admires, the engineer everyone goes to, the account manager customers trust. Their credibility transfers to the effort. Sociologists call this status leakage, and it works in both directions. Staffing the lab with outside hires signals the opposite.

**Occupy a brokerage position.** Ron Burt's work on structural holes shows that people who bridge disconnected groups gain disproportionate influence and generate more good ideas. Position yourself as the connector between the core, customers, and new approaches rather than as the head of a separate tribe.

**Pick an early win that helps the core.** Choose something visible, achievable in roughly 90 days, and tied to churn or a stalled deal. When the innovation team makes the core business's life easier, it earns legitimacy that no mandate can provide.

**Share credit aggressively.** Every win should be attributed jointly to the lab and the core teams involved. Your long-term reputation benefits more from being seen as the person who makes others successful than from being the visionary with the separate lab.

**Separate the metrics, not the people.** Protect the team with different success criteria, such as learning velocity, customer validation, and pilots, and a ring-fenced budget. But keep it physically and socially connected. Rotate people in and out, and give it a clear handoff path into the core.

**Increase independence over time, as earned.** Once you have credibility and a track record, you can advocate for more autonomy for bigger bets. Asking for full independence on day one spends capital you haven't accumulated yet.

## The bottom line

Structurally, the evidence favors a protected-but-connected team over either extreme. Politically, your success depends on not letting this become "new guy vs. CEO" or "lab vs. everyone else." The strategy most likely to produce outsized results is one where the existing organization feels ownership of the innovation, because it's the existing organization that will have to scale it, sell it, and support it.

## Turn 2

**User:** How do I get the best people onto my team when their managers will probably fight to keep them?

**Assistant:**

Managers won't fight you because they're petty. They'll fight because you're asking them to absorb a real loss. Understand exactly what they're losing, then design the ask so that loss shrinks or turns into a gain.

## Why managers resist

Losing a top performer costs a manager in several ways at once:

- **Performance:** Their team's numbers suffer, and they're still accountable for them.
- **Status:** A manager's reputation is partly built on the talent they've assembled. Losing stars can look like a demotion.
- **Signaling:** If their best person leaves for your team, it can imply the manager's own work is less important or less interesting.
- **Uncertainty:** They don't know if the person will come back, or in what condition.
- **Precedent:** If they give in once, they fear becoming the team everyone raids.

Every tactic below addresses one or more of these.

## 1. Don't start with transfers

A permanent move is the most threatening possible ask. Start smaller:

- **Part-time contributions:** 20% of someone's time on a specific problem.
- **Time-boxed sprints:** "Six weeks on this churn problem, then back."
- **Rotations:** A defined tour of duty with a guaranteed return.
- **Problem-based pull:** Invite people to work on a problem their own team cares about, so their manager benefits directly.

Small commitments are easier to grant, and they let you test fit before asking for more. Once managers see people return more skilled and more motivated, resistance drops.

## 2. Make giving talent a status gain

This is the most powerful lever you have. Reframe contributing people as a sign of leadership strength rather than weakness.

- **Credit the manager publicly.** When your team wins, name the managers who contributed people, especially in front of the CEO and executive team.
- **Build a "talent developer" reputation.** In many organizations, the managers most respected over time are the ones known for growing people who go on to bigger things. Help create that norm.
- **Ask the CEO to reinforce it.** One comment from the CEO like "I noticed Sarah's team gave us two of their best people for the churn work, that's the kind of leadership we need" changes the status calculation for every manager listening.

The goal is to make the manager who hoards talent look parochial, and the one who shares it look like a leader.

## 3. Go through managers, not around them

It's tempting to recruit people directly or have the CEO order transfers. Both burn relationships you'll need later.

- **Talk to the manager first.** Before approaching their people, explain what you're working on and why you want this particular person.
- **Ask what they need.** Backfill budget? Timing around a key deadline? A shorter commitment? Solve their problem and you often get a yes.
- **Offer reciprocity.** Access to what your team learns, early looks at tools that help their group, or help on one of their stubborn problems.
- **Use executive mandates sparingly.** CEO backing is essential as a background fact, but invoking it directly should be a last resort. Every forced transfer creates an enemy and a story about you.

## 4. Look past the obvious stars

The people everyone knows are stars are the most expensive to get and often the most embedded in the core's critical work. Taking them may even worsen churn, which undermines the whole point.

Look instead for:

- **Hidden high performers:** People doing excellent work who aren't yet recognized. Their managers resist less, and the opportunity means more to them.
- **Positive deviants:** The support rep whose customers never churn, or the implementation lead whose projects always finish on time. They've already figured out something the organization hasn't.
- **Boundary spanners:** People with relationships across departments. They're valuable for their networks as much as their skills, and they'll help your work spread.
- **The restless:** Capable people who are bored or frustrated. They might leave the company otherwise, which gives their managers a reason to let them go to you rather than lose them entirely.

Ask around: "Who do people go to when something is really broken?" The answers are rarely the names on the high-potential list.

## 5. Make the team attractive enough that people pull themselves in

The best recruiting is when people ask to join. That shifts the dynamic: the manager is now deciding whether to block an employee's request, which carries its own reputational cost.

- **Work on problems that matter.** Nothing attracts talented people like a hard, important problem, and churning customers qualify.
- **Make the work visible.** Share progress in open demos and updates. Let people see what the team does.
- **Offer executive exposure.** Access to the CEO and senior leaders is a powerful draw for ambitious people.
- **Build the team's reputation early.** The first few people you bring in set the team's status. If they're respected, others will want in. If the team gets labeled a dumping ground or an elite club, recruiting gets harder.

## 6. Reduce the career risk for the people themselves

Talented people know innovation teams can be career dead ends. The team gets cut, the project fails, and they've lost their place in the core.

- **Guarantee a return path**, in writing if possible.
- **Protect their performance reviews.** Make sure their work with you counts, and that their home manager isn't the one evaluating them on work they never saw.
- **Promise visibility.** Commit to putting their work in front of leadership.
- **Treat failure as learning, not a mark against them.** And mean it. The first time someone gets punished for a failed experiment, recruiting dries up.

## A practical sequence

1. **Weeks 1–4:** Map the talent. Ask widely who the problem-solvers are. Identify managers likely to be allies.
2. **Weeks 4–8:** Recruit two or three people on part-time or time-boxed terms, ideally from sympathetic managers. Pick a problem that helps their home teams.
3. **Weeks 8–12:** Deliver a visible win. Credit the contributing managers loudly.
4. **After that:** Use that success, and those managers' improved reputations, to recruit the next wave on longer terms.

## The bottom line

You're negotiating in a status economy. Managers will give up their best people when doing so makes them look good, costs them little, and comes back to them in some form. Make the first few contributions low-risk, reward the managers who say yes, and let early success do the recruiting from there.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if the CEO himself starts protecting certain managers or tells me not to take people from key teams?

**Assistant:**

Take it seriously, but don't assume it's a betrayal. A CEO protecting certain teams could mean several different things, and your response should depend on which one it is.

## First, figure out what's actually happening

**He may simply be right.** If the company is losing customers, the teams keeping current accounts alive may be the last thing he can afford to destabilize. A CEO saying "don't touch the people holding the ship together" is often sound judgment, not politics.

**He may be protecting relationships.** Long-tenured managers are part of the CEO's own social capital. They've likely been loyal to him through hard times, and he depends on them to run the company. You're new and unproven. When those managers complain, he'll tend to side with them, especially early on. That's not a verdict on you; it's how status and trust work in any organization.

**Managers may have lobbied him.** If they couldn't block you directly, they may have gone over your head. That tells you who feels threatened, which is useful information.

**He may be hedging his bet on you.** Some CEOs hire a change agent, then quietly limit their reach once the organization pushes back. This is the scenario to watch for, but it's rarely clear from a single instruction.

## Respond with curiosity, not resistance

Your first move should be a conversation, not a counterargument. Ask questions like:

- "Help me understand which teams you consider critical right now, and why."
- "Is this about timing, or are these teams off-limits for the foreseeable future?"
- "What would you be comfortable with? Advice and consultation from those people, even if not their time?"
- "Is there anything you've heard that I should know about how my approach is landing?"

The last question is important. If managers have complained, you want to hear it directly, and asking signals confidence rather than defensiveness. It also gives the CEO a chance to tell you something he might not otherwise volunteer.

Whatever the answer, **comply visibly and without complaint.** Going around the CEO, or grumbling about his decision to others, would be the fastest way to lose his trust and confirm the managers' story that you're a disruptive outsider.

## Negotiate what you can get instead

A "no" on transfers rarely means a "no" on everything. Explore alternatives that give you what you need without costing those teams their people:

- **Access instead of time.** Can you interview their people, shadow them, or review their data? Often the knowledge matters more than the labor.
- **Advisory roles.** A few hours a month from a protected person as a sounding board costs their manager almost nothing.
- **Customer access.** Their teams may hold the relationships with at-risk customers. Being in those conversations may be worth more than borrowing staff.
- **Future commitments.** "Once the Q3 renewals close, could we revisit?"

## Turn the protected managers into allies

This is the status move most people miss. If the CEO has signaled that certain managers are untouchable, those managers now have visible standing. Fighting them is a losing game. Recruiting them into your work is a winning one.

- **Invite them to shape the work.** Ask them to identify the problems your team should tackle, or to review what you're building. People support what they help create.
- **Solve their problems first.** If your early work makes the protected teams' jobs easier, they become your advocates, and their word carries weight with the CEO.
- **Give them credit.** When your team succeeds on something connected to their area, make sure they share in it.

A protected manager who says "the innovation team really helped us" to the CEO is worth more than any person you could have borrowed from them.

## Widen your talent search

If key teams are off-limits, look elsewhere:

- **Less central teams** that have strong people but less political protection.
- **External hires** for a few specific skills, though keep outsiders to a minority so the team doesn't read as a foreign body.
- **Contractors or partners** for short, well-defined work.
- **Customers themselves.** Design partnerships with a few carriers can provide insight no internal hire could.

## Reset expectations explicitly

This is the step that protects your reputation. If your resources shrink, your mandate needs to adjust with them. Otherwise, you'll be judged later against goals you were never equipped to hit.

Have a direct conversation: "Given that these teams are off-limits, here's what I think we can realistically achieve in the next six months, and here's what we'll have to defer. Does that match your expectations?"

Then follow up in writing with a brief summary. This isn't about building a paper trail against him. It's about making sure you and the CEO share the same picture, so that if results come slower than he hoped, the constraint is already part of the story.

## Watch for patterns, not single events

One restriction is normal. What you want to monitor is whether it becomes a pattern. Warning signs include:

- The list of protected teams keeps growing.
- Budget or headcount promised to you gets delayed or reduced.
- The CEO stops including you in key meetings or decisions.
- You hear about his concerns about you from others rather than from him.
- Early wins go unacknowledged while setbacks get attention.

If you see several of these together, it's time for a candid conversation about your mandate: "When you hired me, we talked about producing outsized results. I want to make sure I'm still set up to deliver that. Has your thinking changed?" It's better to surface a gap between what he says and what he's willing to back while you still have time to address it.

## The bottom line

A CEO protecting key teams is usually a sign that you haven't yet accumulated enough status to be trusted with the organization's most valuable resources. That's normal for someone new. The response is to accept the constraint gracefully, get creative about what you can access, make the protected managers into allies, and reset expectations so you're judged fairly. Status in organizations is earned through demonstrated results and reliability. As you build it, the constraints tend to loosen on their own.

## Turn 4

**User:** How do I pick that first 90-day win so it actually impresses the CEO without stepping on the protected teams' turf?

**Assistant:**

The best first win sits at the intersection of three things: **what the CEO already cares about, what customers are feeling, and what no team currently owns.** Find that intersection and you can impress the CEO while making the protected teams look good rather than threatened.

## Start with what "impressive" means to this CEO

Don't guess. Before choosing anything, listen for:

- **His language.** What does he say in all-hands meetings, board updates, and one-on-ones? If he keeps mentioning a specific lost account, a competitor, or "implementation times," that's your signal.
- **What keeps him up at night.** Ask directly: "If you could make one customer problem disappear this quarter, what would it be?"
- **What the board is pressing him on.** A win that gives him a good story for his own bosses is worth far more than one that only impresses you.

There's also a status dimension here. The CEO will be most impressed by a win that **validates his own judgment**: his decision to hire you, and his belief that existing talent can turn things around. A win achieved with insiders, on a problem he's named, lets him say "I was right." A win that implies the organization was broken reads as criticism, even when it's accurate.

## Look for orphan problems

The safest and often most valuable territory is **problems that fall between teams**. Every large organization has them: issues everyone sees, but nobody owns because they cross departmental lines.

In insurance software, common orphan problems include:

- **Handoffs between sales and implementation.** What was promised in the deal doesn't match what implementation delivers, and customers start the relationship frustrated.
- **The gap between implementation and support.** Knowledge gets lost when a project goes live, and new customers struggle in their first months.
- **Early churn signals.** Warning signs are scattered across support tickets, usage data, and account conversations, but nobody pulls them together.
- **Why deals are lost.** Sales has theories, product has theories, but nobody has systematically asked the prospects who said no.

Working on these doesn't take anything from anyone. It fills a gap each team has probably complained about. And it puts you in the brokerage position we discussed earlier, connecting groups that don't normally talk.

## Candidate wins that tend to work

Here are types of first projects that fit the criteria, adapted to your situation:

**Churn risk diagnosis.** Interview recently churned and at-risk customers, combine what you learn with support and usage data, and produce a clear picture of why customers leave and which accounts are most at risk. It's fast, cheap, directly tied to the CEO's concerns, and it hands the account teams something useful.

**Lost-deal analysis.** Talk to 10 to 15 prospects who chose a competitor. Sales teams rarely get honest answers, but a neutral party often does. The findings can reshape how the company sells.

**Rescue one at-risk account, together with the account team.** Pick one important customer showing signs of leaving and work alongside its existing team to fix the underlying problem. A saved renewal is a concrete, dollar-denominated win. But the account team must get the credit, or you'll create an enemy.

**Cut friction in one specific process.** For example, shorten implementation time for one new customer by removing a known bottleneck. A before-and-after number is easy for the CEO to understand and repeat.

**Unblock one stalled deal.** If a promising prospect is stuck on a missing capability or integration, a small team building a proof of concept can tip the decision. This one is higher risk, but a new customer win directly addresses the "struggling to win new business" problem.

## Score your options

Once you have a few candidates, evaluate them honestly:

| Criterion | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| CEO relevance | Does this address something he has explicitly named? |
| Customer impact | Will customers notice, or say something about it? |
| Measurability | Can we show a clear before and after? |
| Feasibility | Can we deliver meaningful results in 90 days with the people we can actually get? |
| Turf safety | Does it fill a gap rather than compete with an existing team? |
| Credit-sharing | Can protected teams share in the success? |
| Storytelling | Can the CEO repeat it in one sentence to the board? |

The strongest candidate won't necessarily be the most ambitious. It will be the one that scores well across the board, especially on feasibility. A modest win delivered on time builds more status than an ambitious project that's still "almost there" at day 90.

## Pre-wire before you commit

Before you announce anything, have quiet conversations with the managers whose areas your project touches, especially the protected ones.

- **Share the idea as a draft.** "I'm thinking about looking at why customers churn in their first year. Does that step on anything you're doing? What would make it useful to you?"
- **Incorporate their input visibly.** If they suggest a change, make it and tell them you did.
- **Offer them a role.** An advisory seat, early access to findings, or the chance to present results together.

Pre-wiring turns potential opponents into co-owners. It also means that when the CEO asks those managers what they think of your project, they already have a stake in its success.

## Define success with the CEO up front

Before starting, agree with the CEO on what success looks like at day 90. Be specific: "We'll have interviewed 20 churned customers and delivered a prioritized list of the top five churn causes with recommended fixes," or "We'll have shortened implementation for this customer from 16 weeks to 10."

This does two things. It ensures you're aiming at something he values, and it protects you from shifting expectations. If you hit the target you agreed on together, the win is unambiguous.

One caution: **churn and revenue metrics often lag beyond 90 days.** A renewal you help save may not formally close until next year. Choose leading indicators you can show within the window, such as customer sentiment, an at-risk account agreeing to a renewal conversation, faster implementation milestones, or a prospect advancing to the next stage.

## Hedge with a small portfolio

Rather than betting everything on one project, consider one main effort plus one or two small side bets. If the main project stalls for reasons outside your control, such as a customer going silent or a key person getting pulled back to their home team, you still have something to show. Keep the side bets genuinely small so they don't drain focus.

## Make the win visible the right way

How you present the win matters as much as the win itself.

- **Bring the customer's voice.** Nothing impresses a CEO like a customer quote or, better yet, a customer on a call saying something changed. It's far more persuasive than your own slides.
- **Lead with credit for others.** Open by naming the teams and people who made it possible, especially from protected groups.
- **Connect it to his agenda.** Frame the result in terms of his stated priorities, not your team's activities.
- **End with what's next.** Show how this first win points to larger opportunities. That's how a single success becomes a case for expanded mandate.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Choosing a technology demo with no customer outcome.** Clever prototypes impress engineers; CEOs of struggling companies want customers kept and deals won.
- **Picking a project whose findings shame a team.** "We discovered support is failing customers" may be true, but presented that way it makes enemies. Frame findings as system problems, not team failures.
- **Taking credit for an account team's save.** Even if your contribution was decisive, let them own it. The goodwill is worth more than the credit.
- **Overreaching.** Promising to fix churn in 90 days sets you up to fail. Promising to understand it clearly and fix one piece of it sets you up to succeed.

## The bottom line

Your first win isn't just about results. It's a status signal that tells the organization what kind of leader you are. The ideal project solves a problem the CEO has named, falls in the gaps between teams, produces a visible customer outcome, and leaves the protected managers looking better than before. Get that right, and you'll have earned the credibility to take on bigger, riskier bets next.
