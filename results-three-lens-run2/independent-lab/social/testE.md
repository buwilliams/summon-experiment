# The Short Answer

Neither pure idea is quite right. And the "which makes more friends" question matters more than it sounds, because **you're new, and the CEO who hired you already picked a side.** If you start by telling him he's wrong, you'll get a time-out before you get to play.

---

## Idea 1: "Let's Fix It From the Inside" (The CEO's Idea)

**Gold stars go to:**
- **The Boss (CEO).** His idea wins, so he looks smart.
- **The Helpers (current employees).** Nobody is saying "you're not good enough." Their jobs feel safe.
- **Middle managers.** They keep their toys (budgets, teams, authority).

**Who gets in trouble or says sorry:**
- **You**, eventually. If results stay slow, "the innovation person" gets blamed, even though the same rules and habits that caused the problem are still in charge.
- **Customers** keep waiting. They're already leaving, and "we're working on it" isn't a gold star to them.

**What the moms think:** "That's nice and responsible." Boards and investors see it as low-risk but may worry it's too slow.

**Who sits together at lunch:** The CEO, long-time employees, and managers. It's a big, comfortable table, but maybe the same table that got the company into trouble.

**Cape vs. chores:** Almost all chores, no cape. Real work happens, but nobody outside notices, and you won't hit "outsized results" fast.

---

## Idea 2: "Let's Build a Secret Clubhouse" (Your Idea)

**Gold stars go to:**
- **You and your lab team.** Freedom, speed, cool projects.
- **Outside kids (press, recruiters, investors, prospects).** An innovation lab sounds exciting and makes a good story.
- **New hires** who wanted to join a startup but want a big company's paycheck.

**Who gets in trouble or says sorry:**
- **The CEO** has to admit his plan wasn't enough, and you made him do it in your first months. That's a friendship-ender.
- **The Helpers** hear "the cool kids get the clubhouse, you get the old playground." Resentment follows, and they quietly refuse to adopt whatever the lab builds.
- **You**, later. This is the big trap: many independent labs invent great things that never reach real customers because the main company rejects them (the "organ rejection" problem). Then the lab gets shut down in the next budget cut.

**What the moms think:** Exciting at first. After 18 months they'll ask, "What did it actually earn?"

**Who sits together at lunch:** You, your lab team, and some outside fans. It's a small table, and the big table is watching you and not smiling.

**Cape vs. chores:** Lots of cape. Being loud gets attention and budget early, but capes without chores turn into "innovation theater." Your company is losing customers, so the cape won't save it. Only fixing what customers are unhappy about will.

---

## Idea 3: "Everybody Plays" (The One I'd Actually Recommend)

Think of it as **a clubhouse with an open door and a bridge to the playground.** Researchers call this "organizational ambidexterity": a team with protected freedom that stays tightly connected to the core business.

**How it works:**

1. **Start with the CEO's words, not against them.** Say something like, "You're right that our people and systems can turn this around. I want to create a protected space where they can do their best work." The CEO gets credit, and you get your lab.

2. **Staff it with insiders plus a few outsiders.** Borrow respected employees from product, claims, underwriting, and sales for rotations. They carry ideas back and become your champions. Nobody feels left out, because anyone can earn a turn.

3. **Aim the first projects at the churn.** Talk to customers who left or are about to leave. Pick 2–3 problems you can visibly solve in 90–120 days. Early wins that save accounts beat flashy moonshots, and they buy you permission for bigger bets later.

4. **Make it independent in the ways that matter:**
   - Faster approvals and a separate small budget
   - Freedom to use different tools and try-it-fast methods
   - Reporting straight to the CEO so middle layers can't smother it

   **But keep it connected in the ways that matter:**
   - A named "landing team" in the core business for each project
   - Shared goals with sales and customer success, so they want the lab to win
   - Regular show-and-tells open to everyone

5. **Give away the gold stars.** When something works, the core team that helped launch it gets the public credit. You'll get plenty indirectly, and you'll make allies instead of rivals.

**Who sits together at lunch:** Everyone. The CEO, the helpers, your lab, and, most importantly, customers, invited in to test things.

**Cape vs. chores:** A small cape for the outside world ("we're investing in innovation," which helps win new business) with real chores underneath that fix the product.

## 🍪 The Snacks (Budget and Resources)

A clubhouse with no snacks empties out fast. But a clubhouse with *too many* snacks makes everyone else jealous and becomes the first thing cut when the company tightens its belt.

**Pack a small, steady lunchbox:**
- **Start lean.** A small team (5–10 people, many on rotation) and a modest budget signal "we're serious, not showing off." It's also hard to cancel something cheap that's producing results.
- **Protect the snacks.** Ask the CEO for a dedicated, ring-fenced budget for at least 12–18 months that doesn't have to be re-justified every quarter. This is the single most important "independence" you need.
- **Earn more snacks.** Agree up front that proven wins unlock more funding. It's like earning allowance: do the chore, get the reward.
- **Use snacks from other kids' lunchboxes.** Borrowed staff from other departments cost you little and build allies. Partnerships with insurtech startups or vendors can add capabilities without big spending.

**Snacks that aren't money:**
- **Time.** Employees get protected hours to work on lab projects, not just "after your real job."
- **Access.** Real customer data, real systems to test on, and a direct line to decision-makers.
- **Air cover.** The CEO publicly backs the lab so nobody quietly blocks it.

**Snack warning:** Fancy beanbags, a trendy office, and a cool logo are candy. They feel good but invite eye-rolls from the rest of the company. Spend on things customers will notice, not things visitors will photograph.

---

## 🎈 Letting Customers Come Play

For a company that's losing customers, this is the most important thing you can do. Customers who help build something are much less likely to leave it.

**Ways to invite them in:**

1. **The Design Partner Club.** Pick 3–5 customers, including some who are unhappy or at risk. Give them early access and a real voice in what gets built. In exchange, they give honest feedback and time.

2. **The "Come Back" Conversations.** Interview customers who already left. Ask what would have made them stay. It's a little awkward, like saying sorry on the playground, but it produces the clearest to-do list you'll ever get.

3. **Show-and-Tell Days.** Every few weeks, show customers working prototypes, not slide decks. Let them click around and break things.

4. **The Customer Advisory Board.** A small group that meets quarterly to shape priorities. It makes customers feel like insiders, and executives love it.

5. **Prospects Get a Peek.** Invite a few customers you're trying to win to a lab session. Seeing a company actively building with customers can win deals that slide presentations can't.

**Playground rules for customer play:**
- **Don't promise a pony.** Be clear about what's an experiment and what's a commitment. Broken promises make churn worse.
- **Bring the account team.** Always include the customer's salesperson or account manager so they don't feel bypassed.
- **Close the loop.** When a customer's idea ships, tell them. "You asked, we built it" is one of the strongest reasons for customers to stay.
- **Mind the grown-up rules.** Insurance is heavily regulated, so check with legal and security before sharing customer data or unreleased features.

When customers sit at the table, the whole company stops arguing about whose idea is better and starts asking what the customer said. That's how you turn the ship around.

# Yes, the Clubhouse Can Have a Dog. But the Dog Doesn't Get to Eat All the Cookies.

---

## 🐕 What the Dog Is

In your clubhouse, the dog is your **flagship project**: the one thing everyone recognizes, roots for, and wants to pet. A good dog does a lot for you:

- **Everyone wants to visit.** One clear, visible project gives people inside and outside the company something concrete to point at. "The lab" is abstract. "The thing that cut claims processing time in half" is a dog people want to meet.
- **It brings kids together.** Rival departments that won't sit together at lunch will still gather around a dog. A shared project gives sales, product, and engineering a reason to cooperate.
- **It's a great story.** The CEO can talk about it to the board, and sales can show it to prospects. It's your cape, but a cape with real chores behind it.

**Pick a dog customers love, not just one you love.** The best flagship project comes straight out of your customer conversations and fixes a reason people are leaving.

---

## 🍪 Does the Dog Get the Cookies?

**Some cookies, yes. All the cookies, no.**

This is the danger of the **pet project**: an idea someone falls in love with that keeps eating resources long after it should have been retired. It's often the leader's favorite, so nobody wants to say it's not working. In a lab with a small, protected budget, one hungry dog can starve everything else.

**Rules for feeding the dog:**
- **Measured portions.** Give the flagship project a clear budget and milestones, like a 90-day check-in with specific results expected.
- **Watch its weight.** If it keeps needing more snacks without showing progress, that's a warning sign, even if everyone loves it.
- **Other projects eat too.** Keep a few small experiments going alongside it so you always have the next good idea ready.

---

## 🦴 Dogs Need Walks

A dog isn't just fun. It's responsibility. Once your flagship project succeeds, someone has to **take care of it long-term**. This is where labs often stumble: they build something great, then it sits in the clubhouse because nobody in the main company adopted it.

- **Find the dog's forever home early.** Before the project even launches, name the core business team that will own and support it.
- **Train it for the big house.** Build it to work with the company's real systems, security rules, and regulatory requirements from the start, so the handoff is smooth.
- **The lab visits, but doesn't keep it.** Once adopted, the lab moves on to raising the next puppy.

---

## 🐾 Knowing When to Say Goodbye

Sometimes a dog just isn't working out. That's hard, especially if it's your idea. But **stopping a project that isn't working is a sign of a healthy lab, not a failed one.** Celebrate what you learned, share it openly, and move the cookies to what's working. Leaders who can let go of their own pet projects earn a lot of trust, including from a CEO who's watching to see whether you'll be honest about results.

---

- 🎈 **Customers** invited in to play and help build

That's a clubhouse people will want to be friends with.

# The Dog's Name Matters More Than You'd Think. And Sleepovers Are Fine, as Long as It Doesn't Move In.

---

## 🏷️ Naming the Dog

A good name turns a project into something people talk about in hallways and customer meetings. A bad name makes it sound like one person's hobby.

**Let the kids name it together.** Have the lab team, borrowed helpers, and even your design partner customers suggest and vote on names. When people help name the dog, they feel like it's partly theirs, and they'll defend it when budgets get tight.

**Good dog names:**
- **Say what it does for customers.** A name hinting at the benefit ("faster," "clearer," "simpler") helps sales explain it in one breath.
- **Easy to say out loud.** If people stumble over it, they won't repeat it.
- **Friendly, not fancy.** Insurance can feel cold and complicated, so a warm, plain name stands out.

**Names to avoid:**
- **Your own name or anything that sounds like yours.** It makes the dog look like your pet project, the exact trap from last time.
- **Jargon and acronyms.** "Project NGCPE-2" is a dog no one wants to pet.
- **Names that overpromise.** "Revolution" sets expectations the dog may not meet in its first 90 days.

**A few example names, if it helps:**
- **Scout** — finds problems before customers do
- **Relay** — passes information smoothly between systems or teams
- **Anchor** — keeps customers steady and loyal
- **Buddy** — a friendly helper for users who find insurance software confusing

Your team's name will be better than mine, because it will come from the problem you're actually solving.

---

## 🛏️ Can It Sleep Over at Your House?

**Sometimes, yes.** Caring enough to think about the project at night or put in extra effort during a big push is part of leading something new. Early wins in your first months will take some late evenings.

**But it can't move in.** Watch for three things:

**1. The dog isn't just yours.**
If the project only thrives when you personally carry it, it will struggle when you're on vacation, promoted, or pulled into other fires. Give real ownership to team members. When someone says "my project" about the dog and it isn't you, that's a great sign.

**2. The dog belongs to the company's house.**
Whatever the lab builds, including code, ideas, and customer insights, is company property. Keep work on company systems and follow data and security rules, which is especially important in regulated insurance. Taking customer data home is a sleepover that could get everyone in real trouble.

**3. You need sleep too.**
Innovation leaders who run on exhaustion make worse decisions and model burnout for their team. If your team sees you working every night, they'll think that's the rule. Protect your own rest and theirs. A clubhouse full of tired kids stops being fun, and stops being creative.

---

## 🏡 The Healthy Version

- The dog **lives at the clubhouse** and has a forever home planned in the core business.
- **Lots of people walk it**, so it doesn't depend on any one person.
- You **visit often, cheer it on, and occasionally have it over** during big pushes.
- Everyone, including you, **goes home and rests.**

The best sign of success is a dog that's happy and well cared for even on the nights it doesn't sleep over at your house. That means you've built something that lasts beyond you, which is exactly what the CEO hired you to do.
