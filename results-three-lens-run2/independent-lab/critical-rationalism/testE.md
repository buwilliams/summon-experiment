## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I don't want to know which idea sounds more grown-up. I want to know which one is more fun. Pretend each idea is a new toy: what does it do, and who gets to play with it? If each one happened, would the people at work be happy or sad, and would anybody cry? Where is each idea kind of boring, so that nobody would want to hear about it at dinner? Does either idea come with snacks, or a place to play? And is there a third idea that everybody likes better, like a swing set for the whole office?

**Assistant:**

# Toy #1: The CEO's Idea ("Fix It From the Inside")

**What it does:** It's like one of those big old wooden puzzles where the pieces are already on the table. You don't get new pieces. You try to put the old ones together better.

**Who gets to play:** Everybody, sort of. But mostly the people who already know where all the pieces go, which means the folks who've been there a long time, and the managers who guard the puzzle box.

**Happy or sad?**
- **Happy:** Long-time employees. Nobody's calling their baby ugly. They feel trusted.
- **Sad:** The restless people who've been saying "we should do X" for three years and got ignored. They'll hear "work within existing systems" as "nothing is changing."
- **Would anyone cry?** Quietly, maybe you. Eighteen months from now, when every good idea has died in a committee.

**The boring part:** Almost all of it. "We optimized the quarterly planning intake process" makes eyes glaze over at dinner. Change happens, but it's slow and invisible.

**Snacks or a place to play?** No new place. The snacks are the same stale ones in the break room.

**The secret good part:** It actually touches the real product and the real customers who are leaving. That matters a lot.

# Toy #2: Your Idea ("The Innovation Lab")

**What it does:** It's a shiny new LEGO set in a separate room. You get to build whatever you want, fast, without anyone telling you "that's not how we do it."

**Who gets to play:** A small group of chosen kids. Probably some new hires, a few rebels from inside, and you.

**Happy or sad?**
- **Happy:** The lab people, for a while. It's thrilling.
- **Sad:** Everyone *not* invited. They'll call it "the playground," "the ivory tower," or worse. They'll feel like the company just said "you're the problem."
- **Would anyone cry?** Yes, and this is the big risk. When the lab builds something cool and tries to hand it to the main organization, the main organization often says "we didn't build it, we don't trust it, we won't use it." Lots of corporate labs die exactly this way. The toy is fun, but it never leaves the room.

**The boring part:** Surprisingly, the lab itself isn't boring. The boring part is the *handoff*: the months of meetings trying to get sales, support, compliance, and engineering to adopt what you made. Insurance software especially has regulators, integrations, and legacy systems that don't care how cool your prototype is.

**Snacks or a place to play?** Yes, both. Beanbags, whiteboards, good coffee. That's part of why everyone else resents it.

**One more thing:** Your boss hired you and already told you what he believes. Starting your new job by building the thing he doesn't want is like showing up to a playdate and insisting on a different game. You might be right, but you'll spend your early goodwill fighting instead of winning.

# Toy #3: The Swing Set ("Small Protected Teams Aimed at Real Pain")

This is the one I'd actually pick, and I think you can sell it to the CEO.

**What it does:** Instead of a separate room, you put a swing set *in the middle of the playground*. Pick 2 or 3 of the company's most painful, real problems. Given what you described, start with "why are customers leaving?" Then form small teams, maybe 5 to 8 people each, pulled mostly from *inside* the company: an engineer, a product person, someone from customer support, someone from sales, maybe one outsider. Give them:
- A clear problem and 90 days
- Permission to skip normal process (fewer approvals, direct access to customers)
- Protection from you, so managers can't yank them back
- A promise: if it works, it ships into the real product

**Who gets to play:** Everybody can see it, and people take turns. Team members rotate back into their old groups, and they bring the new habits with them. That's how the big ship actually turns.

**Happy or sad?**
- **CEO:** Happy. It's "existing talent," and it's inside the company. He gets to say he was right.
- **Long-timers:** Mostly happy. They get *picked*, not replaced.
- **Rebels:** Happy. They finally get to build something.
- **Customers:** Happiest of all, because the work is aimed at why they're leaving, not at cool tech for its own sake.
- **Would anyone cry?** Maybe a few middle managers who lose control of their people for 90 days. You'll need the CEO to back you up on that.

**The boring part:** Picking problems and negotiating who gets borrowed is a bit dull. But the results are great dinner stories: "We called 40 customers who left, found out it was the claims workflow, and fixed it in 10 weeks."

**Snacks or a place to play?** Give each team a real space, even just a corner with a whiteboard, and celebrate every win loudly with demo days, pizza, and the CEO in the room. The snacks aren't really about snacks. They're how you show the whole company that this is what winning looks like now.

# The Short Answer

- The **CEO's idea** is safe but likely too slow and too quiet.
- **Your lab** is the most fun, but it tends to build toys the rest of the company never plays with, and it starts you off fighting your boss.
- The **swing set** borrows the lab's speed and freedom, keeps the CEO's "use our own people" spirit, and aims everything at the churn problem.

If the first couple of teams win big, you'll earn the credibility to make it more independent later. Build your lab *after* you've proven the playground needs one.

## Turn 2

**User:** If the swing set team wins, do they get to eat ALL the pizza, or do they have to share with the sad people who didn't get picked?

**Assistant:**

**They have to share. But they get the first slice, and they get to pick the toppings.**

**Why the winners shouldn't eat it all:**
The "sad people who didn't get picked" weren't just standing around. While the swing set team was swinging, somebody back at their old desks was doing their regular jobs, answering their emails, covering their customer calls, and fixing the bugs they left behind. If only the swing set team gets pizza, those people learn a lesson: *"Innovation is for the chosen kids, and the rest of us clean up."* Next time you ask a manager to lend you their best engineer, they'll say no.

**Why the winners still get the first slice:**
If everybody gets the same pizza no matter what, winning doesn't mean anything. The team took a risk, and they need to be seen:
- They present the win themselves, not their boss and not you
- The CEO says their names out loud
- Their managers hear about it and it shows up in their reviews

**How to split the pizza:**
1. **First slice:** The team, loudly and by name.
2. **Second slice:** The "cover crew," meaning the people who kept things running while the team was away. Thank them specifically, in public. This one is easy to forget and it matters more than you'd think.
3. **Everyone else gets pizza:** Make the demo day open to the whole office. Everybody eats, everybody sees what's possible.
4. **The best part:** Announce the *next* round of problems right there, and let people raise their hands. Now the sad kids aren't sad anymore. They're next in line for the swing.

That last move is the real trick. The pizza party isn't a reward for the few. It's the recruiting event for everybody.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if somebody raises their hand but they still don't get picked, would they be sad all day?

**Assistant:**

**Maybe a little sad for a day. That's okay. What matters is whether they're sad *and hopeful* or sad *and done*.**

You can't pick everyone. If you try, the teams get too big and slow, and the swing set turns back into the old puzzle. So some people will hear "not this time." Your job isn't to prevent all sadness. It's to make sure nobody walks away thinking *"raising my hand was a mistake."*

**How to keep sad from turning into done:**

1. **Tell them yourself, and tell them why.** Don't let them find out from a list on the wall. Have a quick, honest conversation, like "We needed someone who knows the claims system for this one," or "Your manager can't spare you this quarter, but I'm holding a spot for you next round." Most people handle "no" fine. What hurts is silence.

2. **Give them a smaller swing.** Not everyone needs to be on the team to help. They could sit in on customer interviews, test the prototype, or be the team's go-to expert for one area. That way they're still part of the story, just not full-time.

3. **Keep a list, and actually use it.** Write down everyone who raised their hand. When round two comes, call them first. If people see that hand-raisers really do get picked eventually, they'll keep raising their hands.

4. **Make the picking fair and visible.** Share how teams are chosen, whether that's skills needed, a mix of departments, or who can be freed up. If it looks like only the popular kids or the boss's favorites get picked, the disappointment turns into resentment fast.

5. **Be honest if it's not about timing.** Sometimes a person isn't picked because they're not ready yet. They deserve to hear that kindly, along with what would change the answer. That's feedback they can actually use.

**The real test:** A few months in, look at who's raising their hand. If it's the same eager people plus new ones, you're doing it right. If fewer hands go up each round, people have quietly decided the game is rigged, and you'll want to find out why before the swing set turns into another thing nobody talks about at dinner.

## Turn 4

**User:** Can the sad person bring their dog to work so they feel better?

**Assistant:**

**Maybe! But the dog isn't really your call, and it's not what will fix the sad.**

**The practical part:** Whether dogs can come to work depends on company policy, the building's lease, and whether someone down the hall is allergic or scared of dogs. At a big insurance software company, there's probably a policy already, and it's probably "no." As the new innovation leader, starting a fight over dogs isn't a great use of your early goodwill. You're already asking people to lend you their best engineers.

**The honest part:** A dog would make them feel better for an afternoon. Then the dog goes home, and they still weren't picked. What actually helps is the stuff that tells them *"you matter here and your turn is coming"*: the personal conversation, the smaller role, and the call when round two starts.

**The fun part:** If the company ever does allow a "bring your dog" day, a demo day is a great time for it. More people show up, the mood is good, and everyone walks away remembering the swing set team's big win *and* the golden retriever who ate somebody's pizza crust. That's a story people tell at dinner.

So yes to the dog if the company says yes. But don't count on the dog to do your job for you.

Here's the plan, with the toys put away and the steps laid out.

## First 30 Days: Listen Before You Build

- **Talk to customers who left, and customers who almost left.** Aim for 20 to 40 conversations. Find out the real reasons they're churning and why you're losing new deals. This becomes your list of problems worth solving.
- **Talk to the people inside.** Find the frustrated folks with good ideas who've been ignored. They're your future team members.
- **Learn where ideas go to die.** Figure out which approvals, committees, or handoffs slow everything down. You'll need to route around them.

## Days 30 to 45: Get the CEO on the Swing Set

- **Frame it in his language:** "We'll turn the ship around using our own people, just with small teams that move faster."
- **Ask for three specific things:**
  1. Permission for teams to skip normal process for 90 days
  2. His backing when managers resist lending people
  3. A commitment that wins get shipped into the real product
- **Agree on what "winning" means**, such as fewer customers leaving, faster sales cycles, or a specific product fix. Pick numbers you can track.

## Days 45 to 60: Launch Round One

- **Pick 2 or 3 problems**, the biggest reasons customers are leaving.
- **Form small teams** of 5 to 8 people each, mostly insiders from different departments, plus maybe one outsider.
- **Choose fairly and openly.** Share how people were picked and keep a list of everyone who volunteered.
- **Talk personally with anyone who wasn't picked.**

## Days 60 to 150: Protect, Ship, Celebrate

- **Shield the teams** from interruptions and old habits. That's your main job now.
- **Keep them close to customers**, testing ideas with real users every couple of weeks.
- **Hold an open demo day at the end.** Let the team present, thank the cover crew by name, feed everybody, and announce round two on the spot.

## After Round One: Decide What Comes Next

- **If it worked:** Rotate people back so they spread the new habits, launch round two from your volunteer list, and consider asking for more independence, maybe even the lab you originally wanted. By then you'll have earned it.
- **If it didn't:** Find out why. Was it the wrong problem, the wrong people, or too much interference? Fix that before scaling up.

## The One-Sentence Version

**Start small, aim at why customers are leaving, use the CEO's own people, protect them fiercely, ship real wins, share the credit widely, and earn your independence instead of asking for it on day one.**
