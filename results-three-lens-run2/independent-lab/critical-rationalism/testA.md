## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Neither strategy is right on its own, and framing it as a choice between them may be the biggest risk. Here's a way to think it through.

## First, check what problem you're solving

Your company is **churning customers and losing deals**. That's usually a core business problem: product gaps, poor implementation, outdated UX, weak integration, slow release cycles, pricing, or service quality. An independent innovation lab is designed for a different problem: exploring new business models or disruptive technology the core can't pursue.

If customers are leaving because the existing product is painful, a lab building the future won't stop the bleeding. Before deciding on structure, find out why customers leave and why you lose deals. Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, sales, and support. The answer should drive the structure.

## Where each view is right and wrong

**The CEO is right that:**
- Innovation that never reaches the core business doesn't matter. The core has the customers, distribution, data, domain expertise, and revenue.
- Many corporate labs become "innovation theater": demos and hackathons with no commercial impact.
- A turnaround needs broad cultural change, not a small elite group.

**You're right that:**
- Large organizations reliably kill new ideas through budgeting cycles, risk aversion, legacy architecture, and metrics built for the existing business.
- Some work (new products, AI-native approaches, new pricing models) needs different processes, talent, and timelines.
- Asking the core to innovate while it hits quarterly targets usually means innovation loses.

**The classic failure of each:**
- *Working only within the system:* innovation gets absorbed, diluted, and deprioritized.
- *A fully independent lab:* it builds things the core rejects (the "organ rejection" problem), creates resentment, and gets cut at the first budget squeeze.

## What the evidence supports: a connected, not independent, team

Research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly and Tushman) and Govindarajan and Trimble's *The Other Side of Innovation* points to a hybrid:

1. **A small dedicated team** with its own budget, metrics, and working methods, protected from the core's planning and approval processes.
2. **Strong links to the core:** shared customers, borrowed engineers and domain experts, access to data and distribution, and a clear path for handing successful work into the main business.
3. **Senior sponsorship**, ideally the CEO, who resolves conflicts between the new team and the core.
4. **Different success metrics:** learning milestones and customer validation early, revenue later.

The key point: separate *how the team operates*, but integrate *what it works on and where results go*.

## A practical path forward

- **Don't lead with a structural fight.** The CEO hired you and has a stated belief. Opposing it in your first weeks spends credibility you haven't built yet.
- **Diagnose first (30–60 days).** Understand churn and lost deals. This earns trust and tells you what innovation actually needs to deliver.
- **Deliver an early win inside the core.** Pick a visible, customer-facing problem tied to churn and fix it fast with a small cross-functional team. This proves you can work within the system, which is what the CEO values.
- **Use that win to make the case for protected space.** "We moved fast because we bypassed X, Y, and Z. To do this repeatedly, and to take bigger bets, we need a team with these specific freedoms." That's a much easier argument than "we need an independent lab."
- **Build a portfolio.** Most effort goes to fixing and improving the core, some to adjacent opportunities, and a small amount to longer-term bets. The dedicated team matters most for the last two.

## The bottom line

The CEO is right that the turnaround has to happen through the core business. You're right that some innovation needs protection from it. The winning approach is a small, protected team that is tightly connected to the core, starting with the problems causing churn. Once you've proven you can move the core's numbers, you'll have the credibility to get the independence you want.

If you share more about why customers are leaving, I can help you figure out which kind of innovation effort fits best.

## Turn 2

**User:** How do I figure out why customers are actually leaving if people internally all have different theories about it?

**Assistant:**

Conflicting theories are normal, and useful. Treat them as hypotheses to test, not opinions to referee. Your job is to go to the evidence that no internal group controls, especially customers.

## Step 1: Collect the theories on purpose

Spend a week or two interviewing leaders and frontline people across sales, customer success, support, product, engineering, implementation, and finance. Ask each:
- Why do you think customers leave? Why do we lose deals?
- What evidence is that based on?
- Which accounts would you point to as examples?
- What would change your mind?

Write the theories down as a list. You'll notice a pattern: **each function tends to blame another** (sales blames product, product blames sales and implementation, everyone blames price). That's not dishonesty, just each group seeing its own slice.

This step also builds relationships and makes people feel heard, which matters when you present findings later.

## Step 2: Look at the hard data

Before talking to customers, see what the numbers already show:
- **Segment churn:** by customer size, product line, line of insurance, region, tenure, contract type, implementation partner, and account team. Churn is rarely uniform. It often clusters in ways that immediately rule theories in or out.
- **Timing:** Do customers leave after a failed implementation, a price increase, a major release, a champion leaving, or at first renewal?
- **Leading indicators:** support ticket volume and severity, usage trends, escalations, NPS or CSAT, delayed upgrades.
- **Win/loss records:** who you lose to, at what stage, and in which segments.

Be skeptical of CRM "reason lost" fields and exit surveys. They default to easy answers like "price" or "budget," which often hide the real issue.

## Step 3: Talk directly to customers

This is the most important step and the one most companies skip.

**Who to talk to:**
- 10–20 churned customers, across different segments
- 10–15 lost prospects, especially ones that chose a specific competitor
- Several at-risk accounts that haven't left yet
- A few happy, loyal customers, for contrast

**How to do it well:**
- **Use neutral interviewers.** Former customers are more candid with a third party or with you (as someone new) than with their old account manager. Win/loss research firms exist for this if budget allows.
- **Talk to more than one person per account.** The executive who signed, the day-to-day users, and IT often give very different reasons.
- **Reconstruct the story, don't ask for the reason.** Asking "why did you leave?" gets a tidy, polite answer. Instead walk through the timeline: When did you first start thinking about alternatives? What happened just before that? Who was involved? What did you evaluate? What almost made you stay? This "switch interview" approach, from Jobs-to-be-Done research, surfaces the real triggers.
- **Listen for the gap** between what customers expected and what they got.

## Step 4: Triangulate and synthesize

Map what you heard against the original list of internal theories:
- Which theories are **confirmed** by customers and data?
- Which are **contradicted**?
- What came up that **nobody internally mentioned**? This is often the most valuable finding.

Usually you'll find two or three root causes that explain most churn, often varying by segment. Something like: "Mid-size carriers leave after painful implementations; large carriers leave because we're slow on cloud and integrations; price is the stated reason but rarely the real one."

## Step 5: Present it in a way people can accept

- **Lead with customer voices.** Short quotes and audio or video clips are far more persuasive than your interpretation. It's hard to argue with a former customer explaining exactly why they left.
- **Show the theory scorecard openly.** Credit people whose theories held up, and frame contradicted theories neutrally ("the data didn't support this as a major driver").
- **Avoid a blame narrative.** Most churn is cross-functional: a sales promise, an implementation gap, and a product limitation compounding each other. Framing it as a system problem gets you allies instead of defensive departments.
- **Tie findings to action.** End with the two or three problems worth solving first. That leads naturally to the early win we discussed.

## Realistic timeline

About 6 to 8 weeks: 1–2 weeks of internal interviews and data pulls, 3–4 weeks of customer interviews, and 1–2 weeks of synthesis. That's fast enough to show momentum and thorough enough to be credible.

## Why this helps you beyond the answer

Done well, this makes you the person in the company who understands customers best. That gives you credibility with the CEO, a fact base that ends circular debates, and a clear, defensible mandate for where innovation should focus.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if the CEO doesn't want to wait 6 to 8 weeks before I start showing results?

**Assistant:**

You don't have to choose between research and results. Run them in parallel, and make the research itself produce visible output from the first week.

## First, find out what "results" means to him

Ask directly: "What would you need to see in the next 30 days to feel confident this is working?" He might mean revenue saved, a customer win, a visible change, or just evidence of momentum and a clear plan. Many CEOs who say "I want results now" actually want to see that someone is moving fast and has a grip on the problem. Knowing which one you're dealing with changes your plan.

## Compress and parallelize the diagnosis

- **Shrink the first round.** You don't need 40 interviews to see patterns. Eight to ten churned or lost customers often reveal the main themes within two to three weeks. Treat the rest as ongoing confirmation.
- **Run tracks at the same time.** Internal interviews, data analysis, and customer calls don't need to be sequential.
- **Get help.** Borrow an analyst for the data work and have customer success book interviews while you run them.

## Deliver early wins while you diagnose

**1. Save at-risk accounts now.** Identify the 5–10 largest accounts showing warning signs (upcoming renewals, escalations, falling usage). Visit or call them personally, understand their issues, and coordinate a fix across teams. Retaining even one significant account is a concrete, dollar-denominated result, and these conversations double as research.

**2. Fix obvious, known pain.** Every struggling company has problems everyone already agrees on: a notorious bug, a painful onboarding step, a missing integration customers keep requesting. Pick one that is visible to customers and fixable in weeks, and get it done with a small cross-functional team.

**3. Help sales with a live deal.** Join a few active competitive deals, hear directly why prospects hesitate, and help remove a specific blocker. A win you contributed to is powerful early proof.

## Make progress visible

- **Send a short weekly update** to the CEO: what you learned, what you did, what's next. Include one or two customer quotes each week.
- **Share findings as they emerge.** Don't wait for a polished final report. "After six customer conversations, three have independently raised the same implementation problem" is a result, and it shows the CEO you're converging.
- **Frame it as a 30/60/90-day plan** so he can see when to expect which kinds of results.

A sample shape:
- **Days 1–30:** Launch at-risk account saves, start customer interviews, pick one quick fix, weekly updates with early patterns.
- **Days 31–60:** First account saves or deal contributions, quick fix shipped, top churn drivers identified.
- **Days 61–90:** Clear priorities presented, first larger initiative underway, case made for how the innovation team should operate.

## Protect against the real risk

The danger in rushing is picking the wrong problem because it was loud, not because it matters. Guard against it by choosing early wins that are **low-cost and reversible**, and by being explicit that bigger bets will follow the evidence. You can say to the CEO: "I'll show you tangible progress every week, and I'll make sure the big investments go where they'll actually stop churn."

That positions you as someone who moves fast *and* thinks clearly, which is exactly what a CEO hiring for "outsized results" wants to see.

## Turn 4

**User:** How do I get other departments to give me people and help on these early wins when I don't have any authority over them?

**Assistant:**

This is the core challenge of most innovation roles: you're accountable for results but own few of the resources. The good news is that early wins tied to churn serve almost everyone's goals, so you're usually asking people to do something they already want done.

## Start from their goals, not yours

Every department head has metrics they're judged on. Find out what they are, then frame your request as help with them:
- **Customer success:** retention, renewals, escalations
- **Sales:** quota, win rate
- **Support:** ticket volume, resolution time
- **Product and engineering:** roadmap delivery, quality, fewer fire drills
- **Implementation:** on-time go-lives, fewer overruns

Instead of "I need two engineers for my initiative," try: "This bug is behind a third of your team's escalations. If you give me one engineer for three weeks, I'll clear the path so we fix it fast, and your team gets the credit."

## Make the ask small and specific

Vague requests for "support" get deferred. Specific, time-bound ones get approved:
- A named person, for a set number of hours or weeks
- A clear goal and end date
- Minimal disruption to their existing work

"Can I borrow Maria for four hours a week for the next month?" is far easier to say yes to than "Can your team help with the innovation program?"

## Build relationships before you need them

The internal interviews from your diagnosis phase are the ideal time for this. When you meet department leaders:
- Ask what's frustrating them and what they wish leadership understood
- Look for places where you can help them, such as passing along customer insights, removing a blocker, or advocating for their priorities
- Follow up on what they told you

People help those who've helped them or listened to them. Deposit before you withdraw.

## Find the willing people

In every organization there are people who are frustrated with the status quo and eager to fix things. They're often mid-level, close to customers, and energized by being asked. Look for:
- The support lead who's been complaining about the same bug for a year
- The account manager fighting to save a key customer
- The engineer who has a fix in mind but no time allocated

Recruit them with their manager's blessing. Motivated volunteers get more done than reluctant assignees, and they become advocates for you in their departments.

## Give away the credit

This is the most powerful tool you have. When a quick win succeeds, make sure the department and the individuals get visible recognition, especially in front of the CEO and their own leadership. Once leaders see that working with you makes them look good, future requests get much easier. Hoarding credit is the fastest way to lose cooperation.

## Use the CEO's backing carefully

The CEO's support is real leverage, but invoking it directly ("the CEO wants this") makes people comply grudgingly and resent you. Better approaches:
- Ask the CEO to state the priority publicly, such as in a leadership meeting: "Reducing churn is our top priority, and I've asked [you] to lead some fast efforts. I expect everyone to support them."
- Keep the CEO informed of who is helping so he can recognize them.
- Escalate only when truly blocked, and when you do, frame it as a trade-off for leadership to decide ("Engineering can't free anyone without delaying X. Which matters more?") rather than a complaint about a person.

## Reduce the cost of saying yes

Do as much of the work yourself as possible: coordinate, write up requirements, schedule meetings, handle updates, and deal with approvals. The less effort you demand from other departments, the easier it is for them to participate.

## Show momentum early

Your first win is the hardest to staff. Once you have one visible success, people want to be associated with the next one. That's another reason to start with something small, fixable, and customer-visible: it creates proof that working with you pays off.

## The underlying principle

Without authority, your influence comes from three things: **making others successful, being useful, and being trusted.** If each department leader sees you as someone who helps them hit their goals, brings them customer insight, and shares credit generously, you'll find people much more willing to lend resources than your org chart would suggest.
