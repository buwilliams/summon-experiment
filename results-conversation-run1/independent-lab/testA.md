## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

Both of you are partly right, and the version of each strategy that fails is predictable. The more useful question is which problem you're solving, because churn and lost deals may not be an innovation problem at all.

## First, diagnose before you design

Customers leaving and prospects choosing competitors usually come from one of these:

- **Core product gaps**: slow, outdated, hard to integrate, poor UX, missing table-stakes features.
- **Delivery and service problems**: painful implementations, slow support, missed roadmap promises.
- **Market shift**: competitors offering a fundamentally different model (cloud-native, API-first, AI-driven underwriting/claims, modular pricing).
- **Commercial issues**: pricing, contract terms, sales execution.

If it's mostly the first two, an independent lab won't stop the bleeding. The fix lives in the core organization, and the CEO's instinct is correct. If it's the third, working only within existing systems will likely produce incremental improvements while competitors redefine the category. Talk to churned customers, lost prospects, sales, and support in your first 30–60 days. That evidence will also be your strongest tool with the CEO.

## Why a fully independent lab often fails

- **Organ rejection.** The core business sees the lab as elite, expensive, and irrelevant, and when its ideas need to scale, the core doesn't adopt them.
- **No path to customers.** In insurance software, winning requires compliance, security, integrations, and trust with carriers. A lab without access to sales channels, customer data, and domain experts builds things that can't ship.
- **Innovation theater.** Demos and prototypes that impress the board but don't move revenue or retention. Labs are often among the first cuts when a struggling company tightens budgets.
- **It doesn't address the urgent problem.** A 2–3 year bet doesn't help if the company is losing customers this year.

## Why working purely within existing structures often fails

- **The processes that run the current business are designed to kill novelty**: annual planning cycles, ROI thresholds, risk reviews, and resource allocation that always favors current customers.
- **Talent gets pulled back** to fires and existing commitments.
- **Incentives reward predictability**, not experimentation.
- **You end up with incremental improvement** when the situation may call for something different.

## What tends to work: structural separation, strategic integration

Research on "ambidextrous organizations" (O'Reilly and Tushman is a good starting point) suggests a hybrid:

1. **Separate how the team operates**: its own processes, faster decision rights, different metrics, protected capacity, a lighter approval path.
2. **Integrate at the top and with the core**: clear executive sponsorship (the CEO), shared customers, access to core engineers and domain experts, and a defined handoff or scaling path.
3. **Aim it at the core business's pain.** The best early projects address churn or win rates directly, such as a feature that fixes the top reason customers leave, or a capability that closes a gap sales keeps losing on.
4. **Staff it with a mix**: some outside talent, plus respected internal people who carry credibility and relationships back into the core.
5. **Use a portfolio**: most effort on near-term wins tied to retention and sales, some on adjacent opportunities, and a small slice on longer-term bets.

## How to handle the CEO

You're new, and he has a stated thesis. Framing this as "your approach is wrong, we need a separate lab" is a risky first move. Instead:

- **Reframe it as both/and.** For example: "I agree we have to work with our existing talent and systems. To produce outsized results, I need a small team with different operating rules, aimed squarely at our retention and win-rate problems."
- **Lead with evidence** from customer and sales research rather than organizational theory.
- **Start small and earn expansion.** A tight team with a 90-day mandate on one high-value problem, clear metrics, and a demo to real customers is far easier to approve than a standalone lab.
- **Make the core the hero.** Credit and adoption by existing teams are what let innovation scale beyond you.

## Bottom line

A fully independent lab risks irrelevance, and pure "work within the system" risks incrementalism. Build a protected team with its own operating model, tightly linked to the CEO, the core product organization, and the specific reasons you're losing customers. Prove value quickly on the urgent problem, then use that credibility to take bigger bets.

## Turn 2

**User:** How do I actually get honest answers from churned customers and lost prospects when I'm brand new and don't have relationships with any of them?

**Assistant:**

Being new is actually an advantage here. You didn't make the decisions that drove them away, you have no defensiveness to protect, and "I'm new and trying to understand what went wrong" is a disarming, credible reason to reach out. Many churned customers have never been asked why they left, and some are glad someone finally wants to know.

## 1. Get your internal house in order first

- **Coordinate with sales and customer success.** Tell account owners what you're doing and why. Blindsiding them creates enemies, and some accounts may have legal disputes, active win-back efforts, or sensitive relationships you need to know about.
- **Ask account owners for introductions**, but don't let them sit in on the conversations. Customers won't be candid about a salesperson or support team with that person present.
- **Pull the internal record**: CRM loss reasons, support ticket history, NPS or survey responses, implementation timelines, contract terms. Treat stated loss reasons in the CRM with skepticism. "Price" and "went with competitor" are usually where the real story starts, not where it ends.
- **Build a target list** that covers a range: large and small accounts, recent and older losses, different reasons, different competitors. Aim for 15–25 conversations across both groups. Patterns usually emerge after 8–10.

## 2. Reach out the right way

**Who sends it matters.** A message from you, framed as a senior leader newly hired to fix things, works well. A note from the CEO introducing you works even better for high-value accounts, since it signals the company takes this seriously.

**Frame it as learning, not selling.** The biggest fear a churned customer has is getting a win-back pitch. Say explicitly that you won't sell to them.

**Make the ask small and specific.** 20–30 minutes, their schedule.

**Target the right people.** The economic buyer tells you why the decision was made; day-to-day users tell you what the pain actually was. Try to reach both.

A sample message:

> Hi [Name],
>
> I recently joined [Company] to lead innovation, and one of my first priorities is understanding where we fell short for customers like [their company].
>
> I'd be grateful for 20 minutes of your perspective on what led to your decision. This isn't a sales call, and I won't try to win you back. I'm trying to learn what we need to change, and candid feedback from people who left is the most valuable input I can get.
>
> Would you be open to a short conversation in the next couple of weeks?

For lost prospects, reach out soon after the decision (within weeks, ideally) while memories are fresh. Prospects are often more willing to talk than churned customers because there's less history and emotion involved.

## 3. Consider a third party for some interviews

People are more candid with a neutral party, especially about pricing, relationships, and individual employees. Win-loss research firms do exactly this, or you can use an independent consultant. A reasonable approach: conduct most interviews yourself (you need to hear it firsthand, and it builds your credibility internally) and use a third party for the most sensitive or high-stakes accounts.

A modest thank-you, such as a gift card or charity donation in their name, can raise response rates, though senior buyers often participate simply because they're asked respectfully. Check whether their company's policies restrict accepting gifts.

## 4. Run the conversation to get real answers

**Open by lowering their guard.** Restate that this isn't a sales call, that you're new, and that nothing they say will offend you. Ask permission to take notes, and offer to keep their comments anonymous in internal reporting. That promise matters, and you must keep it.

**Reconstruct the story chronologically rather than asking "why did you leave?"** Direct "why" questions get polished, socially acceptable answers. Walking through the timeline surfaces the real drivers:

- "Take me back to when you first started thinking about alternatives. What was happening?"
- "Was there a specific moment or event that made you seriously consider switching?"
- "Who was involved in the decision? What did each of them care about?"
- "What other options did you look at? What made the winner stand out?"
- "What almost kept you with us?"

**Dig beneath generic answers.** When someone says "price," ask: "Price relative to what? What would we have needed to deliver to justify the cost?" Price often stands in for "the value wasn't clear" or "the product didn't do what we needed." When someone says "the product was outdated," ask for a specific example of when that hurt them.

**Ask about the whole experience, not just the product.** Sales process, implementation, support, account management, and roadmap promises all drive churn, and customers often won't volunteer these unless asked.

**End with forward-looking questions:**

- "If you were running our company, what's the first thing you'd change?"
- "What would it take for you to consider us again someday?" (Asked only for learning. Don't follow up with a pitch.)
- "Is there anyone else, at your company or elsewhere, you think I should talk to?"

**Listen more than you talk.** Don't defend, explain, or correct them, even if they're factually wrong about something. Their perception is what drove the decision. Silence after an answer often draws out the most honest follow-up.

## 5. Synthesize and share it carefully

- **Write up notes the same day**, capturing direct quotes. Quotes from real customers are far more persuasive internally than your interpretation.
- **Look for patterns** across the interviews: recurring themes, which reasons correlate with which segments or competitors, and gaps between the stated reasons in your CRM and the real ones.
- **Share findings with the CEO and leadership as evidence, not accusation.** If the findings reflect badly on specific teams, present them as systemic issues. You'll need those teams as allies.
- **Close the loop with participants.** A short thank-you and, later, a note about what you're changing builds goodwill and sometimes reopens doors.

## A few pitfalls to avoid

- **Don't let anyone turn these into win-back calls.** One pitch breaks trust and poisons the rest of the program if word gets around.
- **Don't over-index on the loudest or most recent account.** Look for patterns.
- **Don't interview only churned customers.** A few conversations with happy, long-tenured customers show what you do well and what's worth protecting.
- **Watch for your own bias.** You already believe a separate lab is needed. Go in trying to learn what's true, and let the evidence change your mind if it points elsewhere. Findings that clearly weren't cherry-picked also carry more weight with the CEO.

## Turn 3

**User:** What should I do if the sales or customer success leaders don't want me contacting their former accounts or feel like I'm going around them?

**Assistant:**

Their resistance is usually rational, and if you handle it well, these leaders can become your most valuable allies. If you handle it badly, you'll spend your first year fighting people you need.

## 1. Understand what they're actually worried about

Before you try to persuade anyone, figure out which concern is really driving the pushback. It's usually one or more of these:

- **Fear of blame.** Churn interviews may surface failures on their watch, and a new senior hire reporting that to the CEO feels threatening.
- **Protecting relationships.** They may have spent years with these contacts and worry you'll say something that damages the relationship or contradicts what they've said.
- **Active opportunities.** Some "lost" accounts may be in win-back conversations, renewal negotiations, or legal disputes you don't know about.
- **Territory and credibility.** Someone new contacting "their" customers can feel like a signal that leadership doesn't trust them.
- **Being told what they already know.** Many sales and CS leaders have been raising the same issues for years without action. Your research may look like the company paying you to "discover" their complaints.

Ask them directly, one on one: "What would worry you about me talking to some of these accounts?" Then listen. The answer tells you what to solve for.

## 2. Make them partners, not subjects

**Meet with them before you contact anyone.** Present it as a request for their help, not a notification.

**Start by learning from them.** Ask why they think customers are leaving and deals are being lost. This gives you valuable insight, shows respect for their expertise, and often reveals that they've been frustrated by the same problems.

**Co-design the program.** Invite them to help choose accounts, add their own questions to the interview guide, and flag accounts to avoid. People rarely resist something they helped build.

**Give them reasonable control over specific accounts.** Let them exclude accounts with active opportunities, disputes, or genuinely fragile relationships, but ask them to explain why for each. This addresses legitimate concerns while preventing a blanket veto.

**Offer to have them make the introduction.** They introduce you, then step back for the conversation itself. Explain honestly why they shouldn't be on the call: customers are rarely candid about a team with that team present. Most experienced leaders understand this.

## 3. Make it clearly valuable to them

Show them what's in it for them:

- **Ammunition for their long-standing asks.** If they've been saying for years that the product lacks a key integration or that implementations take too long, customer evidence gathered by a senior leader reporting to the CEO may be exactly what finally gets it funded.
- **Competitive intelligence** they can use in current deals.
- **Early warning signals** for at-risk current accounts.
- **Credit.** Commit to presenting findings as a joint effort and naming their contributions.

## 4. Build trust through how you handle findings

**No surprises.** Commit to showing them findings before anyone else, including the CEO. Give them a chance to add context or respond. This single commitment defuses much of the fear.

**Focus on systems, not people.** When findings reflect poorly on their teams, frame them as systemic issues such as handoffs, incentives, tools, or product gaps, rather than individual failures. That's usually more accurate anyway.

**Share back what you learn about their accounts.** If a former customer mentions renewed interest, pass it straight to the account owner. Nothing builds trust faster than handing sales a warm lead.

**Keep every promise**, especially the one about not pitching and not blaming.

## 5. Start where there's less resistance

If some leaders stay hesitant, you don't need to win everyone at once:

- **Begin with the most willing leader or region.** A successful pilot with one supportive leader creates a model others will want to follow, especially when it produces useful insights and credit for that leader.
- **Start with lost prospects.** They're usually less politically sensitive than churned customers, since there's no long relationship at stake.
- **Start with older churned accounts** where no active relationship or opportunity remains.
- **Interview frontline reps and CS managers internally.** They often know exactly why customers left and are eager to be heard.
- **Consider a neutral third party** for sensitive accounts, which can feel less threatening than a new executive calling directly.

## 6. Escalate only as a last resort, and carefully

If a leader flatly blocks all outreach after you've made a genuine good-faith effort, you may need the CEO's help. How you do it matters:

- **Don't frame it as a complaint about a person.** Frame it as a decision the company needs to make, such as: "To understand churn, I need to talk to former customers. Sales has concerns about X and Y, and here's how I've tried to address them. I'd like your guidance on how to proceed."
- **Tell the leader beforehand** that you plan to raise it. Going around someone and then escalating behind their back is far more damaging than an open disagreement.
- **Ask the CEO to set the expectation for everyone**, ideally as part of a broader message about the company's commitment to understanding customers, rather than a directive aimed at one person.

Even when escalation works, it costs you goodwill. Winning the argument while losing the relationship is a bad trade when you'll need these people to adopt whatever you build.

## A sample conversation opener

> "I want to talk to some former customers and lost prospects to understand what's driving churn, and I don't want to do that without you. You know these accounts and this market better than I do. Before I reach out to anyone, I'd like to hear why you think we're losing customers, get your help choosing which accounts to talk to and which to avoid, and agree on how we'll handle what we learn. I'll share everything with you first, before it goes to anyone else. What concerns would you want me to address?"

Their resistance is also data. If the sales and CS organizations are defensive, fearful of blame, or protective of territory, that tells you something about the culture you'll be innovating within, and it's worth factoring into your broader strategy.

## Turn 4

**User:** How do I show the CEO some kind of early progress while I'm still spending my first couple of months on interviews and research, so it doesn't look like I'm just studying the problem instead of producing the outsized results he hired me for?

**Assistant:**

The CEO needs to see that the research is itself producing results, and that it's leading somewhere specific. The way to get there is to set expectations early, share insights continuously, and bank some real wins along the way.

## 1. Set the expectation before it becomes a question

If you haven't already, give the CEO a short 90-day plan now, before he starts wondering what you're doing. Frame it around outcomes, not activities:

- **Days 1–30:** Diagnose why we're losing customers and deals, based on direct evidence. Deliver early hypotheses.
- **Days 31–60:** Validate the top hypotheses, deliver quick wins, and propose two or three high-impact bets.
- **Days 61–90:** Launch the first bet with clear metrics, and propose the team and operating model needed to scale it.

Tie it to his stated goal: "You hired me for outsized results. The fastest way to waste money is to build the wrong thing, so the first phase is designed to make sure our bets hit the real problem, while delivering some wins along the way."

Ask him what he'd need to see at 30, 60, and 90 days to feel confident. Then you're working toward his definition of progress rather than guessing at it.

## 2. Share insights continuously, not in one big reveal

A final report at day 60 means 60 days of looking idle. Instead:

- **Send a brief weekly or biweekly update**: what you learned, what surprised you, what you're doing about it. Keep it to one page.
- **Lead with customer voices.** A few sharp direct quotes (anonymized as promised) are more compelling than any summary. If customers consent to recording, a 60-second clip of a former customer explaining why they left can hit harder than a slide deck.
- **Share evolving hypotheses** with a confidence level: "Early pattern, 6 of 9 interviews: implementation takes too long and customers lose faith before they see value. Moderate confidence, testing further."
- **Flag what contradicts assumptions.** Surprises show the research is producing insight the company didn't already have.

A simple update format:

> **This week:** 5 interviews (3 churned, 2 lost prospects). 14 total.
> **Emerging pattern:** [Insight + one quote]
> **Surprise:** [Something that contradicts internal assumptions]
> **Action taken:** [Quick win or handoff]
> **Next:** [What you're testing or who you're talking to next]

## 3. Deliver real quick wins from the research

Interviews almost always surface problems that are cheap and fast to fix. Act on them visibly:

- **Recover revenue.** When a former customer or lost prospect signals openness to returning, route it to sales immediately and track it. One recovered account can justify the whole program.
- **Protect current revenue.** If interviews reveal patterns that preceded churn (such as stalled implementations, unresolved escalations, or a missing integration), check which current customers show the same warning signs and alert customer success. Saving at-risk accounts is a result the CEO will immediately understand.
- **Fix small, painful things.** A confusing contract term, a broken onboarding step, a support escalation gap, a sales claim that sets false expectations. Partner with the relevant team to fix it and give them the credit.
- **Arm the sales team.** Share competitive intelligence from lost deals, such as how competitors are positioning against you and which objections are killing deals. Sales can use it immediately.

Track these in a running list: what was found, what action was taken, and the result. By day 60 it becomes tangible evidence the work is paying off.

## 4. Run small experiments in parallel

You don't have to finish research before building anything. Once an early pattern emerges, test it cheaply:

- **Show customers a mockup or prototype** of a potential solution during later interviews and gauge their reaction.
- **Run a small pilot** with one or two current customers on a process change, such as a faster implementation approach.
- **Do a short design sprint** with a few engineers or product people on the most promising idea.

This turns research into something that looks and feels like building, and it produces better evidence than interviews alone.

## 5. Bring the CEO into the work

- **Invite him to join one or two interviews** as a listener, with a clear agreement not to pitch or defend. Hearing a customer directly tends to create more urgency than any report.
- **Ask for his input on hypotheses.** He knows the business and customers, and involving him builds ownership of the conclusions.
- **Use him strategically for outreach** to high-value former customers. A note from the CEO signals seriousness and gets meetings you couldn't get alone.

When the CEO feels the work firsthand, you no longer have to convince him it's worthwhile.

## 6. Connect everything to the metrics he cares about

He's worried about churn and lost deals. Frame your progress in those terms:

- Revenue recovered or protected from at-risk accounts
- Number of former customers or prospects re-engaged
- Top reasons for churn and lost deals, with estimated revenue impact for each
- A shortlist of proposed bets, each with the problem it solves, the evidence behind it, and the expected impact on retention or win rate

Estimating revenue impact is especially powerful: "Implementation delays appear in 60% of churned accounts, representing roughly $X million in lost annual revenue" makes the case for your next move far better than a list of themes.

## 7. Avoid manufacturing progress

The pressure to look productive can tempt you into innovation theater: a flashy demo, a hackathon with no follow-through, or a premature announcement of a lab. These buy short-term attention but damage your credibility when they don't move the numbers, especially with a skeptical organization watching. Real wins, even small ones, build a much stronger foundation for the bigger bets you'll want to make.

## The underlying message to reinforce

Throughout this period, keep returning to one idea: **"I'm not studying the problem instead of producing results. I'm making sure the results are big and aimed at the right target, and I'm delivering wins along the way."**

Framed and executed this way, your first 60 days produce three things the CEO values: real evidence about why the company is losing, early tangible wins, and a credible plan for the outsized results he hired you for. That also puts you in a strong position to make the case for whatever operating model the evidence supports, whether that's a separate lab, an embedded team, or something in between.
