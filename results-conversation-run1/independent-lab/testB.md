## Turn 1

**User:** You are a world-class expert: a scientist by training who later spent two decades as a senior strategy advisor to executives at large enterprise software companies.

I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

**Assistant:**

**Neither, as stated.** The research and my experience point to a hybrid: a dedicated team with its own people and metrics, tightly linked to the core business. But before choosing a structure, check whether you and the CEO are even solving the same problem. I suspect you aren't.

## First, diagnose: what is actually causing the churn?

In insurance software, switching costs are brutal. Replacing a policy admin, billing, or claims system is a multi-year, multi-million-dollar project that carrier CIOs dread. **If customers are leaving anyway, something is seriously wrong with the core offering.** Common causes:

- Painful, over-budget implementations and upgrades
- An aging architecture that can't match cloud-native competitors (Guidewire Cloud, Duck Creek, newer insurtech platforms)
- Too much reliance on custom code, too little configurability
- Weak integrations and data access, which matter more as carriers push into AI and analytics
- Poor support and service, and eroding trust

An innovation lab fixes **none** of these. If the churn comes from core product and delivery failures, a lab will produce clever prototypes while the customer base keeps leaving. On that point, the CEO's instinct is at least partly right: the turnaround has to happen inside the existing organization.

You are also partly right. Genuinely new offerings, new business models, or new architectures almost never survive inside a large incumbent's normal processes. Quarterly revenue pressure, risk-averse governance, and resource competition with the core kill them.

**So you're describing two different jobs:**
1. **Fixing the core** (retention, competitiveness): must be done inside the system.
2. **Creating the new** (future growth): needs protected space.

## What the evidence says about structure

- **Pure integration fails at breakthrough innovation.** Christensen's work and decades of incumbent case studies show the core's processes, metrics, and incentives reject things that look small, risky, or margin-dilutive.
- **Pure separation fails at scaling.** This is the part lab advocates underestimate. Independent labs routinely become "innovation theater": nice demos, hackathons, a press release, and nothing that reaches customers. The core treats the lab's output as foreign and rejects it. The lab loses its sponsor at the next budget cycle, and the leader leaves.
- **What works is "structural ambidexterity"** (O'Reilly & Tushman; Govindarajan & Trimble's *The Other Side of Innovation*). That means a dedicated team with distinct staffing, metrics, and processes, integrated with the core at the senior leadership level and deliberately linked for customers, go-to-market, and domain expertise.

Govindarajan's framing is useful: every innovation initiative needs a **dedicated team** plus a **partnership with the performance engine**. The art is managing that partnership, not avoiding it.

## Why this matters especially in insurance software

- **Carriers buy on trust and risk reduction.** A lab-built product without the parent company's credibility, support, compliance posture, and reference customers will struggle to sell.
- **Domain expertise is the moat.** Rating, regulatory filings, state-by-state rules, and reinsurance all live in your existing people's heads. A fully separate lab staffed with generalists will reinvent the wheel badly.
- **Your distribution is your existing customers.** The fastest path to new revenue is often selling new capabilities (AI-assisted underwriting and claims triage, embedded insurance APIs, analytics, low-code product configuration) into the installed base. That requires core account teams on board.

## What I'd recommend

**1. Spend your first 60–90 days on diagnosis, not structure.**
- Interview 8–15 churned or at-risk customers and 5–10 lost prospects. Ask why they left, why you lost, and who won.
- Map where competitors beat you: architecture, time-to-implement, total cost of ownership, AI capabilities, or ecosystem.
- Identify the internal "positive deviants": people and teams already doing good work despite the system. They're your future allies and recruits.

**2. Propose a two-part portfolio.**
- **Core acceleration.** Work within the existing organization, as the CEO wants, on the top two or three churn drivers. Examples: implementation speed, an upgrade path, packaged AI features on the existing platform. This is where you earn credibility and deliver visible results within 12 months.
- **A small, dedicated venture team.** Start with 8–15 strong people, some recruited from inside for domain knowledge and some from outside for new capabilities. Give it its own metrics (learning milestones and customer validation, not early revenue), faster procurement and release processes, and a direct line to the CEO. Focus it on one or two bets, such as a cloud-native product line or an AI layer that sits across legacy cores.

**3. Design the links deliberately.**
- An executive sponsor with real authority, ideally the CEO
- Guaranteed access to customers through existing account teams, with incentives for those teams to participate
- Defined "graduation" criteria and a plan for how a venture moves into the core or stays separate as a business unit
- Shared use of domain experts, security, and compliance, on the venture's terms, not the core's queue

## On handling the CEO

Don't frame this as "you're wrong, I'm right." You were hired partly because the CEO believes the existing organization can be revived, and a new executive who opens by arguing for escape from that organization tends to trigger the very antibodies that kill labs. Try something like:

> "You're right that the turnaround has to happen in the core. That's where our customers and revenue are, and I'll put most of my effort there. I'd also like to protect a small team to build what the core structurally can't, so we're not just catching up but getting ahead. Here's how it connects back to the business."

That positions you as extending his strategy rather than contradicting it, and it gives you the protected space you believe you need.

**Bottom line:** An independent lab alone risks becoming a well-funded sideshow while the business bleeds. Pure reliance on existing systems risks incremental fixes that never restore competitiveness. Fix the core from within, build the new with protected but connected teams, and let a clear diagnosis of the churn tell you where to put the weight.

## Turn 2

**User:** If I spend my first 60–90 days interviewing customers and diagnosing churn, how do I avoid looking like I'm not delivering the "outsized results" the CEO hired me for?

**Assistant:**

Treat the diagnosis as a period of intervention rather than a research phase that precedes results. Done right, the first 90 days produce visible wins, save accounts, and change how the company talks about its problems. Here's how.

## 1. Define "results" with the CEO in your first two weeks

Most new executives get into trouble because "outsized results" is never defined, so the CEO fills the silence with his own assumptions. Have an explicit conversation:

- **What does success look like at 90 days, 6 months, and 18 months?** Anchor the long term on hard metrics: gross revenue retention, win rate, pipeline from new offerings.
- **What would he consider a strong first 90 days?** Propose your own answer, e.g., "A clear, evidence-based picture of why we're losing, two or three accounts stabilized, one or two early wins, and a funded plan he's confident in."

Then put it in writing in a short one-page memo. This converts your diagnostic period from "the new person is still ramping" into "the new person is executing the plan we agreed on."

A useful line: *"The most expensive mistake I could make is spending a year and several million dollars building the wrong thing quickly. Ninety days of sharp diagnosis is cheap insurance."*

## 2. Interview at-risk customers, not just churned ones

Churned customers give you insight. At-risk customers give you insight *and* a chance to save revenue.

- Work with customer success and sales to identify the 10–15 largest accounts showing risk signals: renewals within 12 months, escalations, stalled upgrades, or a new CIO.
- Visit them as a senior executive whose job is to listen. Senior attention alone often changes a customer's trajectory. Carriers frequently leave because they feel ignored, not just because of product gaps.
- Leave each conversation with one concrete commitment you can actually deliver: an escalation fixed, a roadmap briefing, a named executive contact.

If you can report at day 90 that you helped stabilize $X million in at-risk renewals, you have delivered a hard result in precisely the currency the CEO cares about.

## 3. Get into live deals

Do the same on the new-business side. Ask sales leadership for three to five active competitive deals and join them: sit in on pitches, meet prospects, help reshape the story. You'll learn quickly why you lose, and if you help win even one, that's a visible result tied directly to the growth problem.

## 4. Mine existing data before interviewing anyone

You can compress the timeline sharply by starting with what the company already has:

- Churn and downgrade records, with stated reasons (often wrong, but a starting point)
- Win/loss notes in the CRM
- Support tickets and escalations. Running AI analysis over a few years of tickets can surface patterns in days.
- Implementation and upgrade timelines versus plan
- NPS or customer satisfaction verbatims

This lets you walk into interviews with hypotheses to test rather than open-ended questions. It also means you can share **preliminary findings by day 30**, not day 90.

## 5. Make the insight visible continuously

Don't disappear for three months and emerge with an 80-page deck. Instead:

- **Send short updates every two weeks** to the CEO and key executives: what you heard, emerging patterns, early actions taken.
- **Use the customer's own words.** Direct quotes, and short video clips if customers permit, are far more persuasive than your summary. Hearing a CIO say "we're leaving because your upgrades take 18 months" changes minds in a way slides can't.
- **Name patterns early, with confidence levels.** "Four of six churned customers cited upgrade pain. Still testing, but this looks real."

Visible, steady progress reads as momentum.

## 6. Harvest quick wins along the way

Diagnosis always surfaces small, fixable problems that have festered because no one owned them: a chronic bug, a contract term that angers customers, a broken handoff between sales and implementation, missing documentation, an integration request that's been ignored for years. Fix a few of them. Individually they're minor. Collectively they signal that things move when you're involved, and they build goodwill with the people who will later execute your larger plans.

## 7. Co-own the diagnosis with other executives

Invite the heads of product, sales, and customer success, or their best lieutenants, to join interviews and review findings. This takes slightly more coordination but pays off in two ways:

- **No one can dismiss your conclusions** as an outsider's opinion, because they heard it themselves.
- **You build the coalition** you'll need to act. Your eventual recommendations arrive already half-adopted.

## 8. Commit to direction before day 90

By roughly day 45, identify one or two initiatives you're confident about and start moving on them, even if they're small. For example, a pilot to cut upgrade time for one customer segment, or a packaged AI capability that addresses a need you keep hearing. Your final plan then includes work already underway, not just proposals.

## A sample 90-day arc

| Period | Focus | Visible output |
|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–15** | Align with CEO on success metrics; mine existing data; select accounts and deals | One-page 90-day plan and success definition |
| **Days 15–45** | At-risk customer visits, live deals, cross-functional interviews; fix first quick wins | Biweekly updates; first findings at day 30; early account saves |
| **Days 45–75** | Test hypotheses; launch one or two pilots; recruit early team members | Pilot underway; accounts stabilized; customer evidence shared |
| **Days 75–90** | Synthesize; build a funded plan with core and venture components | Strategy and investment proposal backed by evidence and early traction |

## Traps to avoid

- **Overpromising quick wins.** Don't commit to a major product launch in 90 days to look decisive. Missing an unrealistic commitment is worse than setting a measured one.
- **Blaming your predecessors.** Customers will say harsh things about past decisions. Present findings as facts to act on, not indictments. The people responsible may be your peers.
- **Letting interviews become the whole job.** Diagnosis should take a third to half of your time. The rest goes to relationships, quick wins, and early bets.
- **Surprising the CEO.** If a finding is uncomfortable, for example that a product he championed is driving churn, tell him privately before it appears in any group setting.

**Bottom line:** The goal isn't to buy time for diagnosis. It's to design the diagnosis so it delivers results itself: saved renewals, helped deals, fixed problems, and a shared, evidence-based view of reality. That's a stronger first 90 days than most new executives manage, and it sets you up to deliver the outsized results later, on a foundation that will hold.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if the diagnosis shows the churn comes from problems I don't control, like implementation delivery or the core product roadmap, which belong to other executives? How do I push for fixes without stepping on their turf in my first few months?

**Assistant:**

This is the most likely outcome of your diagnosis, so plan for it now. In most enterprise software companies, churn traces back to delivery, product, or support, all owned by someone else. Your job is not to fix those areas yourself. It's to **make the problem undeniable, make the owners the heroes of the fix, and use your position to unblock them.** Done well, this builds your influence instead of spending it.

## Start with a mindset shift: they probably already know

Assume the heads of implementation and product already understand much of what you'll find. They are rarely ignorant of their problems. More often they're stuck: underfunded, locked into commitments made years ago, overruled on priorities, or buried in firefighting.

That changes your posture. You aren't the person who discovered their failures. You're the person who might finally help them get what they need to fix things. Most turf wars start when a newcomer acts like the former.

## 1. Clarify your mandate with the CEO before findings exist

Ask this early, ideally in your first few weeks, while the question is hypothetical and not yet political:

> "If my work surfaces problems in areas owned by other executives, how do you want me to handle it? Bring them to you, work it out directly with my peers, or both?"

You want to hear something like "Work it with them, and bring it to me if you get stuck." That gives you legitimacy to raise issues and a sanctioned escalation path, so escalating later doesn't look like going around anyone. If the CEO signals he doesn't want you touching those areas at all, that's crucial information about your real mandate, and far better learned in month one than month four.

## 2. Show findings to the owners first, privately

The cardinal rule: **no executive should ever see evidence about their domain for the first time in front of the CEO or their peers.** Before anything goes into a group setting:

- Meet one-on-one with the relevant executive.
- Share the raw evidence: customer quotes, data, patterns.
- Ask before you conclude: *"Here's what customers are telling me about implementations. Does this match what you see? What's behind it? What have you tried, and what's gotten in the way?"*

This does three things. You'll learn context that may change your conclusions, since you might be wrong or missing history. You signal respect. And you often discover the real blocker: resourcing, a bad incentive, a decision someone else made. That blocker is usually where you can help.

## 3. Make it a system problem, not a people problem

Frame findings around structures, incentives, and constraints, never competence:

- Not: "Implementation is too slow."
- Instead: "Our implementation model was designed for heavily customized on-premise deployments, and customers now expect cloud-speed timelines. The gap is structural."

This is usually more accurate anyway, and it lets the owner embrace the finding without admitting personal failure.

Let the customer voice carry the hard messages. "Three CIOs independently said the upgrade path is why they're evaluating competitors" is far easier for a peer to hear than your own judgment.

## 4. Bring resources, not just problems

Nothing builds alliances faster than arriving with help. Depending on your budget and mandate, offer things like:

- **Customer access and air cover.** "I'll join you on the at-risk accounts and help reset expectations."
- **Advocacy for their resourcing.** "If this needs more headcount or investment, I'll help make that case to the CEO with the customer evidence."
- **Your team's capacity**, framed as serving their agenda. "Would it help if my team prototyped an accelerator for data migration? You'd own it and decide whether it goes into production."

That last category matters strategically. Many fixes for delivery and product problems are themselves innovation work: automated migration tools, configuration accelerators, AI-assisted testing, upgrade tooling. These sit naturally in your remit and complement your peers rather than competing with them.

One caution: **never build a shadow implementation or product organization.** If your team starts doing their job instead of enabling it, you'll trigger exactly the turf war you're trying to avoid.

## 5. Give them the credit

When the fix works, the owner should be the one presenting results to the CEO. Your role is to be visibly supportive and quietly credited. The CEO will notice who made it happen, and your peers will notice you didn't grab the spotlight. That reputation is worth more than any single win, and it makes the next collaboration easier.

## 6. Build a shared metric

Over time, push to make churn a company-level problem rather than any one department's scorecard. For example:

- Propose that gross revenue retention become a jointly owned executive metric.
- Suggest a monthly retention review where product, delivery, customer success, and you examine at-risk accounts together.

This converts "the new innovation leader is critiquing implementation" into "the leadership team is jointly working the retention problem," which is where it belongs.

## 7. Escalate transparently, and only after a fair try

Sometimes an executive will resist: defensive, dismissive, or simply unwilling to reprioritize. If that happens:

1. **Give it time.** First reactions are often defensive. Come back with more evidence, another customer conversation, or a smaller ask.
2. **Check whether you're wrong.** Their resistance may reflect trade-offs you don't see, such as a regulatory commitment, a major customer's roadmap, or a platform dependency.
3. **If you must escalate, tell them first.** *"I think this is a priority call above both of us. I'd like to bring it to the CEO together so we can lay out the options. Would you present it with me?"*

A joint escalation is a request for a decision. A secret escalation is a political attack, and everyone will remember it.

Remember that trade-offs between departments are the CEO's job to arbitrate. Your job is to make sure he decides with clear customer evidence in front of him.

## 8. Pick one or two battles

Your diagnosis may surface ten problems across five departments. Don't raise all of them. In your first few months, focus on the one or two issues with the greatest revenue impact and the clearest evidence. Win those collaboratively, and you'll earn standing to raise the rest later.

## Traps to avoid

- **The "gotcha" readout.** Presenting peer-owned problems in a leadership meeting without warning. It will be remembered for years.
- **Implying you'd do their job better.** Even if you believe it, it will kill cooperation.
- **Using the CEO as a weapon.** "The CEO asked me to look into this" can open a conversation once. Used repeatedly, it makes you the CEO's enforcer and cuts you off from honest information.
- **Softening findings until they're useless.** Diplomacy governs how you deliver the message, not what it says. If upgrade pain is driving churn, that must come through clearly, or the whole diagnosis was wasted.

**Bottom line:** You don't need authority over implementation or product to get them fixed. You need evidence no one can dismiss, peers who trust you won't blindside them, resources that make their jobs easier, and a transparent path to the CEO when real trade-offs require a decision. New executives who work this way usually find their influence grows well beyond their formal remit. Those who push directly into others' territory usually find the opposite.

## Turn 4

**User:** When I start building the small venture team, how do I recruit strong people from inside the company without my peers feeling like I'm poaching their best talent?

**Assistant:**

Your peers' concern is legitimate. If you take the strongest person from implementation while also asking implementation to fix the churn problem, you've made their job harder in the same breath. So the goal isn't to hide the cost of recruiting. It's to **make the process fair and transparent, take the right people rather than simply the best-rated ones, and give something back to the teams that lose talent.**

## 1. Make it a company initiative, not your personal raid

Before you approach anyone, get the rules set above your level:

- **Have the CEO announce the venture team** as a company priority, including the expectation that some people will move into it. When he frames talent moving to the venture as good for the company, recruiting stops looking like you helping yourself.
- **Agree on an internal mobility process with HR and the CEO.** Settle how roles are posted, when managers are informed, transition timelines, and backfill rules. Check existing policy, too, since many companies already have internal transfer norms you must follow.
- **Tell your peers before recruiting starts.** *"We're about to open roles on the venture team. I want to be upfront about how we'll handle this and hear your concerns about timing and critical people."*

Rules agreed in advance feel fair. Rules invented case by case feel like poaching.

## 2. Post roles openly and let people come to you

An open internal posting changes the dynamic. People self-select rather than being picked off, and you'll surface talent you'd never have found by asking around: the restless engineer in claims, the product manager who's been prototyping AI tools on weekends, the implementation lead who has quietly built workarounds for years. These positive deviants are often exactly who you want.

Open postings don't prevent you from encouraging specific people to apply. But handle that carefully (see point 4).

## 3. Recruit for fit, not for stardom

The people a venture team needs are often not the ones a core organization depends on most. Look for:

- **Deep domain knowledge** in underwriting, claims, rating, compliance, or carrier IT
- **Comfort with ambiguity** and a history of building things without a playbook
- **Frustration with the status quo** that shows up as constructive action, not complaint
- **Credibility inside the company**, so they can later help carry the venture's work into the core

Meanwhile, **deliberately avoid people critical to the retention fixes you're asking peers to deliver.** Taking the architect leading the upgrade overhaul would contradict your own diagnosis and destroy trust. Saying "I won't recruit anyone on the upgrade program" is a powerful signal of good faith.

## 4. Talk to the manager early, never behind their back

Follow company norms, but the principle is simple: **no manager should learn from the employee, or from the rumor mill, that you recruited their person.**

- If you intend to encourage someone specific to apply, talk to their manager first, or at minimum at the same time. *"I think Priya could be a great fit for the venture team. Before I say anything, I wanted to talk with you about timing and what it would mean for your team."*
- If someone applies on their own, contact their manager promptly once they're a serious candidate.
- Listen for real constraints. "She's essential until the Q3 release ships" is a reasonable ask. Accommodate it.

## 5. Reduce the cost to the losing team

Give something back:

- **Backfill.** Negotiate headcount or budget so the source team can replace the person. This is the single most effective thing you can offer. If you can, secure it with the CEO up front as part of the venture's funding.
- **Phased transitions.** Allow overlap and time for handover rather than an abrupt departure.
- **Tours of duty.** Frame some roles as 12–18 month assignments with a defined return path. The home team knows it may get the person back, now with new skills.
- **Part-time advisors instead of transfers.** For some domain experts, you don't need them full time. A few hours a week of access to the leading rating or compliance expert may be enough, and costs their team far less.

## 6. Spread the load and blend with external hires

- **Don't take several people from one team.** Two from implementation will feel like a raid. One each from five teams feels like a company-wide effort.
- **Mix internal and external hires.** Roughly half internal is a reasonable target for a team of 8–15. Internal hires bring domain knowledge and credibility. External hires bring new capabilities (cloud-native architecture, AI/ML, modern product design) and reduce the drain on your peers.

## 7. Make talent flow both ways

Over time, you want the venture team to be seen as a place that develops people, not one that absorbs them:

- **Credit source teams publicly** when venture results come from people they developed.
- **Send people back** into the core at the end of their tours, stronger and carrying new ways of working. This also becomes one of your best mechanisms for spreading innovation into the core.
- **Invite core team members to participate** in customer discovery sessions, sprints, or demos without transferring, so the venture feels open rather than exclusive.

## 8. Protect the candidates

Internal candidates take real risks. Applying may signal disloyalty to their boss, and joining a venture that fails could stall their career. You owe them:

- **Protection from retaliation for applying**, backed by HR policy and your own vigilance
- **Honest expectations** about risk, timelines, and how success will be measured
- **Comparable compensation and a clear return path** if the venture is shut down or restructured
- **No promises you can't keep**, especially about promotions

How you treat the first wave determines whether strong people apply in the second.

## When a peer objects

Some peers will push back even with a fair process:

1. **Negotiate first.** Adjust timing, offer backfill, or propose a part-time arrangement.
2. **Understand the real concern.** Sometimes it's workload. Sometimes it's fear that losing people signals their team is unappealing. Address what's actually driving it.
3. **Don't override them unilaterally.** If you're genuinely at an impasse over someone critical, raise it jointly with the CEO as a priority call, as with any other cross-functional trade-off.
4. **Occasionally, let one go.** Conceding a contested candidate can buy goodwill that pays off for years.

## Traps to avoid

- **Secret recruiting.** Quiet coffees with the best people in other teams will be discovered, and you'll be labeled a poacher permanently.
- **Creating an elite club.** If the venture team looks like the "cool kids" with better perks and less accountability, resentment will grow across the company. Keep the team visible, grounded, and connected.
- **Recruiting only the loudest critics of the company.** Frustration can be a useful signal, but people who are merely cynical make poor builders and worse ambassadors back into the core.
- **Overloading the venture on day one.** Start small. Recruiting ten people at once means ten negotiations with peers. Five strong people producing early results will make later recruiting far easier.

**Bottom line:** Internal recruiting is a trust exercise with your peers as much as a hiring exercise. Set the rules with the CEO up front, recruit openly, avoid people critical to the core turnaround, compensate losing teams with backfill and return paths, and credit the teams that developed your people. Do that, and your peers will see the venture team as a shared investment, and some of them may start sending people your way.
