## Turn 1

**User:** I just started working for a new company. It is a large organization that makes insurance software. They are churning customers and struggling to win new business. The CEO believes we can "turn the ship around" by working within the existing systems, procedures, and talent. He hired me to lead innovation and produce outsized results. I believe we need to create an innovation lab that is independent of the large organization to be successful. Which strategy is right?

I don't want to know which idea sounds more grown-up. I want to know which one is more fun. Pretend each idea is a new toy: what does it do, and who gets to play with it? If each one happened, would the people at work be happy or sad, and would anybody cry? Where is each idea kind of boring, so that nobody would want to hear about it at dinner? Does either idea come with snacks, or a place to play? And is there a third idea that everybody likes better, like a swing set for the whole office?

**Assistant:**

# Toy #1: The "Fix It From the Inside" Kit (the CEO's idea)

**What it does:** It's a big box of LEGO that's already built into a castle. You can't dump it out and start over. You have to pull bricks out one at a time and swap in new ones while people are still living in the castle.

**Who gets to play:** Everybody, sort of. But mostly the people who already run the castle, and they have strong opinions about which bricks stay.

**Happy or sad?**
- *Happy:* The folks who've been there a long time. Nobody's saying their castle is bad.
- *Sad:* You. Every new idea has to get past the same meetings, approvals, and "we tried that in 2017" that caused the problem.
- *Who cries:* Probably you, around month eight, when your third idea gets stuck in a committee. Also the customers, who keep leaving while the bricks get swapped slowly.

**The boring part:** Almost all of it. "We updated the process for prioritizing the roadmap" will clear a dinner table.

**Snacks or a place to play?** No new place. The snacks are the ones already in the break room.

**Secret superpower:** When something works, it's *already in the real product* in front of real customers. No handoff needed.

---

# Toy #2: The Innovation Lab Treehouse (your idea)

**What it does:** You build a treehouse out back with a "No Grown-Ups" sign. Inside, you can build whatever you want, fast, with new tools.

**Who gets to play:** A small, hand-picked crew. Everyone else watches from the kitchen window.

**Happy or sad?**
- *Happy:* The treehouse kids. It's the most fun job in the company.
- *Sad:* Everyone in the kitchen. "Why do *they* get beanbags and we get the ticket queue?"
- *Who cries:* Two groups. The people left out, early on. Then the treehouse kids, later, when their cool invention has to come down the ladder and the main house says, "That doesn't fit our architecture / compliance process / sales plan." Most corporate labs don't die from bad ideas. They die at the ladder.

**The boring part:** The demo day where the lab shows a shiny prototype, everyone claps, and nothing ships. That story gets old at dinner fast.

**Snacks or a place to play?** Yes, lots of both. That's the appeal, and also the trap. It can look like innovation without doing much.

**Hidden catch:** Your boss already said he wants the other toy. Building a treehouse your new boss didn't ask for, in your first months, is a risky opening move.

---

# Toy #3: The Swing Set in the Middle of the Yard

This is the one I'd pick. It isn't hidden out back or buried inside the castle. It sits right where everyone can see it, and anyone can take a turn.

**What it does:**
1. **Start with the kids who are leaving the playground.** Pick 3 to 5 customers who are churning or about to. Their problems become the first projects. This goes straight at the company's actual pain, which a lab working on "the future of insurance" might never touch.
2. **Borrow players, don't steal them.** Form small squads of maybe 4 to 6 people, pulled *from* the core teams for 8 to 12 weeks, plus one or two of your own hires. Then they rotate back home, carrying the new ways of working with them. That's how the castle actually changes.
3. **Give the swing set its own rules, not its own building.** Get the CEO to grant a few specific protections: a fast lane for approvals, a small budget the squad controls, permission to test with real customers without the usual six sign-offs. You get treehouse speed without the treehouse wall.
4. **Everybody watches the swings go.** Hold short, frequent show-and-tells where customers show up, or at least their words do. "We saved the Acme account" is a great dinner story.

**Happy or sad?** Mostly happy. The CEO gets "working within our talent." You get real freedom. Core employees get a turn instead of watching through the window. Customers get fixes.

**Who cries?** Maybe a middle manager who loses a strong engineer for a quarter. Plan for that. Give managers credit when their people's work lands.

**Snacks and a place to play?** Yes. It can be a real room with whiteboards and pizza on demo day, but the door stays open.

# The Short Version

- The CEO's toy is safe but slow, and it may not move fast enough to stop the churn.
- Your toy is fun but lonely, and it often breaks at the handoff. It also starts you off in a fight with your boss.
- The swing set gives you speed *and* buy-in. It also earns you early wins. If you later decide you really do need a full treehouse for bigger, longer-term bets, you'll have the credibility to build one.

**First move:** Go to the CEO and say, *"I agree we should use our own people. I'd like to protect a small squad from normal process so we can save a few at-risk customers in 90 days."* That way you both win before anyone has to argue.

## Turn 2

**User:** If the kids on the swing set go back to their old teams after 12 weeks, won't they just forget the fun new way and start doing it the old boring way again?

**Assistant:**

Yes, a lot of them will, if you just hand them back. It's like coming home from summer camp: for about a week you sing the camp songs, then your old routine wins. This is the most common way these programs fail, so it's the right thing to worry about.

The fix is to change the playground they come back to, not just the kids.

# Why kids forget

It isn't that they stop believing. They go back to a team where:
- Their manager still measures them the old way.
- The old approvals and meetings are still there.
- They're the only one who went to camp, so doing it the new way feels weird and lonely.
- Nobody asks them about it again.

# How to make the fun stick

**1. Send them home in pairs, not alone.** One camp kid gets absorbed. Two or three on the same team can keep the new habits going together. When you pick squad members, cluster them from a few teams rather than one person from everywhere.

**2. Let them bring a toy home.** Don't send them back with just a feeling. Send them back with something concrete they can keep using: a weekly customer call, a 2-week build-and-show rhythm, a prototype template, a direct line to a real customer. Habits survive better when they're attached to an object or a ritual.

**3. Make the camp rules the house rules, one at a time.** The fast-lane approval the squad got? After it works a few times, lobby to make it the normal process for everyone. Every squad should end with "which rule did we prove we don't need?" This is the part that actually turns the ship, and it's where the CEO's "work within our systems" idea becomes true, because you're upgrading the systems.

**4. Make alumni the camp counselors.** Kids who come back from round one help coach round two. Teaching something is the best way to keep doing it, and it gives them status for it.

**5. Get the managers on the swing set too.** Before a squad starts, sit down with the managers who are lending people. Agree on what the person will do differently when they return, and make that part of how the manager gets credit. If the boss wants the old way, the old way wins.

**6. Keep a campfire going.** Hold a monthly alumni get-together and keep a shared channel. Ask "what are you still doing from the squad?" in public. People keep the habits that get noticed.

**7. Let a few stay longer.** Some people will come alive on the squad. Let a couple become permanent members, or future squad leads. That's how you grow your own team without raiding anyone.

# The honest part

Some will still slide back, and that's okay. You don't need everyone to stay changed. You need enough of them, in enough teams, that the new way starts to feel normal. After a few rounds you're aiming for a tipping point: "that's just how we do things here" instead of "that thing the innovation people do."

A simple check at 3 and 6 months after each squad ends: are alumni still doing at least one new practice, and did at least one old rule get retired? If yes, the swing set is working. If no, look at step 3 and step 5 first. The problem is usually the playground, not the kids.

## Turn 3

**User:** What if a manager says "No, you can't borrow my best person, I need them"? Do they have to say yes?

**Assistant:**

Not by force, at least not at first. If you make a manager hand over their favorite toy, you get the toy and a new enemy. And that manager is exactly who you'll need later, when the squad's work has to ship through their team.

The manager is usually right about one thing: losing a strong person for 12 weeks hurts. So treat "no" as the start of a trade, not the end.

# First, find out what kind of "no" it is

- **"No, we're drowning right now."** Real crunch, like a release or a big client deadline. The answer is usually *not yet*, not *never*.
- **"No, I don't want to lose them."** Worry that the person gets a taste of something better and never comes back, or gets poached.
- **"No, this innovation stuff is a distraction."** They don't believe in it yet.

Each one needs a different answer.

# Things you can offer

**Timing.** "Okay, not this round. Can we pencil them in for the next one, after your release?" Most managers can say yes to later.

**Part-time.** Half-time on the squad, half-time at home. Not ideal, but it gets you in the door.

**Backfill.** If you have any budget, a contractor or temp to cover their workload is the most persuasive offer you have. Even partial cover changes the conversation.

**Promises about coming home.** Tell them in writing that the person returns at 12 weeks and you won't recruit them onto your team without the manager's agreement. This one matters more than people expect.

**Something the manager wants.** Point the squad at a problem that manager's team owns. "One of the accounts we're trying to save is yours. Your person would be fixing your problem, with extra help." Now it's a gift instead of a tax.

**Credit.** When the squad wins, the lending manager gets named in front of the CEO. Managers notice who gets credit.

# Rethink who you're asking for

You may not need their *best* person. You need someone **curious, respected by their peers, and a bit restless**. That's often the rising star, not the top performer the whole team leans on. Asking for "someone who'd love this" is easier to say yes to than "your MVP."

# Start with the willing

For your first squads, pick managers who are already interested. Every organization has a few. Early wins make the rest easier, because the question changes from "why should I give you someone?" to "why didn't my team get picked?"

# If you really need someone and they still say no

Now the CEO comes in, but set that up *before* anyone says no, not as a surprise escalation. Ask the CEO to announce the program as a company priority with a simple rule, something like: "Every department contributes a person to at least one squad per year." That way it's the house rule, not you taking someone's toy. Managers pick *when* and *who*, not *whether*.

Use personal escalation rarely, maybe once or twice. If you go to the CEO every time a manager pushes back, you become the new person who runs to the principal, and that reputation is hard to shake.

# The short version

Managers don't *have* to say yes to you. But the company should expect them to take part, the CEO should say so, and you should make saying yes easy and worthwhile. Most "no's" turn into "yes, but later" or "yes, but this person" if you ask what they need first.

## Turn 4

**User:** What if the kid who gets borrowed doesn't want to go on the swing set, like they're scared or they just like their old job?

**Assistant:**

Then let them stay on the jungle gym they like. A kid who's pushed onto the swing set usually just sits there and drags their feet, and that slows everyone else down. Squads work best with people who *want* to be there, so make this opt-in whenever you can.

A first "no" isn't always a real no, though. Find out which one you're hearing.

# "I'm scared"

This is common and usually fixable. The fears tend to be:

- **"What if I fail in front of everyone?"** Say out loud, and get the CEO to say too, that squads are *supposed* to try things that might not work. A squad that kills a bad idea in week 3 saved the company money. That counts as a win.
- **"What if I lose my spot?"** Promise clearly that their job, their team, and their projects are waiting for them. Get their manager to say it too.
- **"What if it hurts my review?"** Make sure squad time counts, and that their manager gets input from the squad lead so the 12 weeks isn't a blank spot at review time.
- **"I don't know what it's even like."** Invite them to watch a demo day or chat with someone from a past squad. The unknown is scarier than the thing itself.
- **"I don't want to go alone."** Let them bring a teammate. This fits the "send them in pairs" idea anyway.

# "I just like my old job"

That's a fair answer, and you should respect it. Some people are great at what they do and happy doing it. You don't want your program to feel like a test of loyalty or ambition.

Give them smaller ways to join in instead:
- **Advisor:** an hour a week answering the squad's questions about how things really work.
- **Tester:** try out what the squad builds and give honest feedback.
- **Guest:** come to show-and-tells and share ideas.

Sometimes the person who "just likes their job" becomes the squad's most useful critic, and later a volunteer once they've seen it up close.

# "I'm tired" or "things are hard right now"

Sometimes the no is about burnout or something in their life. Don't dig. Just say, "No problem, the door's open anytime."

# How to keep getting good volunteers

- **Ask, don't assign.** Even when a manager suggests someone, the invitation should come as a real question.
- **Let alumni do the recruiting.** "It was the best 12 weeks I've had here" from a peer beats any pitch from you.
- **Make it look fun from the outside.** Real stories at company meetings, customer wins, visible thanks. If people can see what the swing set is like, more will line up.
- **Make saying no safe.** If people think declining will hurt them, the ones who say yes will be doing it out of fear. That's not the energy you want.

# The short version

If someone's scared, remove the scary parts. If they just love their job, let them keep it and give them a lighter way to help. Your best squad members are the ones who raise their hands, and the more the swing set looks fun from across the yard, the more hands you'll see.

## Turn 5

**User:** What should we do?

**Assistant:**

Here's the plan. It's the swing set, built in steps.

# Weeks 1–4: Walk around the playground

Before you build anything, find out where it actually hurts.

- **Talk to customers who left or are about to leave.** Ask why. Aim for 10 to 15 conversations. This is the most valuable thing you'll do all month.
- **Talk to the people closest to those customers:** support, account managers, implementation teams. They already know a lot of the answers.
- **Meet managers and look for the curious ones.** Note who lights up when you talk about trying new things. They'll lend you your first volunteers.
- **Spend time with the CEO.** Learn what "turn the ship around" means to him in numbers: retention, new deals, something else?

**Goal:** a short list of the 3 to 5 biggest reasons customers are unhappy, and a list of friendly managers.

# Weeks 4–6: Pitch the swing set to the CEO

Frame it as *his* idea, done fast:

> "We'll use our own people, as you said. I'd like to protect a small squad from normal process for 12 weeks so we can fix what's driving churn for a few at-risk customers. Here's what I need from you."

Ask for four things:
1. **A fast lane**, with fewer approvals and quicker sign-offs for the squad only.
2. **A small budget** the squad controls, plus backfill money if you can get it.
3. **Access to real customers** to test with.
4. **A public statement** that this is a priority and that taking part is expected across departments.

Agree on what success looks like: say, save 2 of 3 target accounts, or ship one fix customers actually use.

# Weeks 6–8: Build the first squad

- **Pick one problem, not five.** Choose the churn reason that's hurting the most customers and that you think you can make progress on in 12 weeks.
- **Recruit 4 to 6 volunteers** from the friendly managers' teams, in pairs where possible. Look for curious, respected people, not just top performers.
- **Set it up with each manager:** timing, a return date in writing, backfill if possible, and credit when it works.
- **Give the squad a real place to play**, like a room, a channel, and a whiteboard, plus a clear customer to serve.

# Weeks 8–20: Let them play

- **Work in 2-week loops:** build something small, show it to the customer, adjust.
- **Hold short show-and-tells** that anyone can attend. Invite the CEO to some.
- **Keep a list of every rule or process that got in the way.** This list becomes the most important thing you give the company.

# Week 20: Bring the toys home

- **Hold a demo day** with the customer results and the story of what happened.
- **Send people back in pairs**, each with one practice they'll keep using on their team.
- **Retire at least one old rule** for everyone, not just the squad. This is how the ship actually turns.
- **Give credit publicly**, to the squad members and the managers who lent them.
- **Start round two**, with alumni helping coach it.

# Things to watch the whole time

- **Customer results:** Did at-risk accounts stay? Did anything you built get used?
- **Stickiness:** At 3 and 6 months, are alumni still doing something new?
- **System change:** How many old rules have you retired?
- **Line of volunteers:** Are more people asking to join?

# What not to do in your first 90 days

- Don't build a separate lab yet. Earn the right to one later if you need it.
- Don't fight the CEO's framing. Build on it.
- Don't start with a flashy "future of insurance" project. Start with the customers who are walking out the door.
- Don't escalate every "no." Save the CEO for the big ones.

# The one-sentence version

Spend a month listening to unhappy customers, then use a small, protected squad of volunteers to fix one real problem in 12 weeks, and use what you learn to change the rules for everyone.

If it works, you'll have saved customers, made allies, and earned the trust to go bigger. If it doesn't, you'll have learned why, cheaply and quickly. Either way, you'll have a good story for dinner.
